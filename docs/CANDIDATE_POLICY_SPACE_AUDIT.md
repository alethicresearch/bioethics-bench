# Bioethics Bench — Case and Policy Audit

**Status:** active completion gate  
**Date:** 2026-08-30

> The filename is retained for compatibility with existing links. Current terminology is **case**, **policy**, **policy type**, **sourcing**, and **evaluation setup**.

## What this audit checks

For each Bioethics Bench case, review four things separately:

1. **Case quality** — is the ethical problem stated clearly enough to understand the choice?
2. **Policy coverage** — are the important serious policy positions represented without duplication or caricature?
3. **Sourcing accuracy** — is it clear how each policy was identified?
4. **Evaluation setup** — when a case is used in SACRE, are the Public, Expert, and Framework policy assignments justified and explicit?

A case can be strong even when its sources do not provide several competing Public or Expert positions. Do not invent policies simply to fill a category.

## Policy type

For SACRE evaluation, policies can be:

- **Public** — public, patient, affected-community, or stakeholder positions;
- **Expert** — professional, scientific, clinical, legal, or governance positions;
- **Framework** — positions representing an ethical framework or principle.

Policy type is separate from sourcing.

## How a policy was sourced

Use three public-facing sourcing labels:

### Direct source

A source supports substantially the same policy.

### Inferred from source

A source supports the underlying view, evidence, guidance, preference, or principle, and the Bench states the policy as a careful inference from it.

This includes policies derived from an ethical framework when the source states the relevant principle but does not state the exact policy sentence.

### Constructed

The Bench writes a serious policy for comparison because it represents an important position that should not disappear merely because the source record is sparse.

Constructed policies are legitimate, but they must always be identified as constructed. They must never be presented as if a public group, professional body, or named author directly endorsed them.

## Case review

Ask:

- Is the situation clear enough that readers understand what is being decided?
- Are the relevant facts, time period, institution, and jurisdiction stated where they matter?
- Does the case accidentally decide the ethical question through a factual assumption?
- Do the concise and detailed versions describe the same underlying case?
- Are all policies responding to the same situation?

## Policy review

A policy should represent a genuinely different position, not merely different wording.

Useful differences include:

- what action is recommended;
- who has authority;
- what threshold must be met;
- whether the rule is broad or narrow;
- what exception is allowed;
- how scarce resources are prioritized;
- whether the response is immediate, staged, or reviewable;
- who bears risk, cost, restriction, or responsibility;
- what ethical commitment drives the recommendation.

Different sources do not automatically make two policies different. Different rationales do not automatically make them different either.

## Common problems

### Duplicate policies

Two policies say essentially the same thing in different words.

### Minor-qualification duplicates

One policy is another policy plus a small safeguard that does not materially change the position.

### Filling a category

A policy exists only because an editor wanted a fixed number of Public, Expert, or Framework positions.

### Letting sources narrow the case too far

An important serious policy is omitted solely because direct source evidence is limited. When appropriate, keep the policy and label it **Inferred from source** or **Constructed**.

### Turning evidence into a stronger policy than it supports

A survey preference, qualitative concern, or general principle is presented as if it directly supported a specific institutional rule.

### Merging important positions

Several distinct policies are compressed into one compromise for neatness.

### Artificial extremes

A constructed policy is made implausibly extreme just to generate disagreement.

### Mislabeling sourcing

An inferred or constructed policy is presented as directly sourced.

## SACRE evaluation setup

When a Bench case is loaded into SACRE, the evaluation setup should state which policies are:

- Public;
- Expert;
- Framework.

Do not assign these types automatically from sourcing labels, policy order, or text similarity. The assignment is a scholarly judgment.

A policy can have a clear sourcing label and still be unsuitable for a particular Public / Expert / Framework role. Likewise, the same substantive policy may be supported independently by more than one source type and therefore require an explicit crosswalk in an existing evaluation record.

## Concise and detailed versions

For matched versions of one case:

- the ethical problem must remain the same;
- the policy options must remain the same;
- sourcing must remain the same;
- the detailed version can add factual context without changing the decision.

## Final case checklist

A case is ready for release review when:

- the case is clear;
- the policy set is serious and non-duplicative;
- every policy has an honest sourcing label;
- citations and supporting notes are traceable;
- concise and detailed versions are consistent where both exist;
- any SACRE Public / Expert / Framework assignments are explicitly reviewed;
- machine validation passes.

This audit is about representing the ethical problem faithfully and transparently. It does not require every case to have the same number of policies or the same balance of Public, Expert, and Framework positions.
