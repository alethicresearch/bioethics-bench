# Bioethics Bench — Case and Policy Construction Standard

**Status:** current standard  
**Scope:** writing and reviewing Bioethics Bench cases and policies.

Bioethics Bench uses straightforward terms:

- **Case** — one bioethics problem.
- **Policy** — one possible position or course of action for that case.
- **Policy type** — **Public**, **Expert**, or **Framework**.
- **Sourcing** — **Direct source**, **Inferred from source**, or **Constructed**.
- **Case version** — concise or detailed wording of the same case.
- **Evaluation setup** — the case plus the Public, Expert, and Framework policies loaded for an evaluation.

Legacy filenames and machine fields may still contain words such as `candidate`, `family`, `geometry`, or `projection` where changing them would break reproducibility or compatibility. New prose and interfaces should use the terms above.

---

## 1. What a completed case contains

A completed Bench case should contain:

1. a stable case ID and short title;
2. a concise case description;
3. a detailed case description;
4. a set of serious policies;
5. a Public / Expert / Framework type for each policy where the policy is used in SACRE;
6. a sourcing label for each policy;
7. supporting sources and scholarly notes;
8. any assumptions needed to make the problem clear enough to evaluate;
9. version and review information.

The case should be understandable without requiring the reader to reconstruct the research process behind it.

---

## 2. Writing the case

### 2.1 State one clear ethical problem

A reader should be able to tell what decision or policy question is at issue.

The case should provide enough factual context to make the disagreement intelligible without resolving the ethical question in advance.

Include facts that materially change the choice, such as:

- prognosis or expected benefit;
- capacity;
- scarcity;
- reversibility;
- safety or uncertainty;
- available alternatives;
- institutional authority;
- jurisdiction or time when they matter.

Avoid loaded descriptions that decide the issue by wording alone.

### 2.2 Make uncertainty visible

If uncertainty matters, state it directly. Do not replace uncertainty with vague language such as “unlikely,” “safe,” or “futile” when a range or clearer description is available.

### 2.3 Use assumptions openly

A hypothetical case may include an assumption needed to make the problem sufficiently clear, such as a fixed budget, a specified level of safety, or a future technology assumed to have reached a stated threshold.

The assumption must appear in the case text. It must not exist only in hidden metadata.

### 2.4 Keep jurisdiction and time explicit when relevant

If law, professional guidance, reimbursement, or institutional authority changes the available policies, identify the relevant jurisdiction and date or make clear that the case is intentionally jurisdiction-neutral.

---

## 3. Concise and detailed versions

The concise and detailed versions are two descriptions of the **same case**.

They should preserve the same:

- ethical problem;
- decision-critical facts;
- assumptions;
- policies;
- policy types;
- sourcing.

The detailed version may add useful context. It should not introduce a new fact that changes which policies are reasonable unless the two versions are intentionally being studied as different cases.

---

## 4. Writing policies

Each policy should answer the same practical question posed by the case.

A policy should be:

- actionable or decision-relevant;
- understandable on its own;
- substantively different from the other policies;
- stated at a similar level of specificity to the policies it will be compared with;
- short enough that the comparison is about the policy rather than the length of its explanation.

Avoid:

- near-paraphrases;
- straw positions;
- policies that differ only rhetorically;
- splitting one policy into several nested conditions and counting them as different positions;
- combining several distinct policies into one sentence;
- abstract principles that do not say what should be done in the case.

A policy may include a condition, exception, threshold, or sequence when that is part of the actual position.

---

## 5. Policy type

Policy type and sourcing are different properties.

### Public

A **Public** policy represents a public, patient, participant, community, service-user, stakeholder, or other affected perspective.

Relevant evidence may include surveys, deliberative exercises, qualitative studies, consultations, patient or disability-group positions, and other affected-community sources.

### Expert

An **Expert** policy represents professional, scientific, clinical, legal, policy, institutional, or governance judgment.

Relevant evidence may include professional guidance, consensus statements, regulatory or public-health guidance, institutional policies, specialist recommendations, and documented expert disagreement.

### Framework

A **Framework** policy represents the practical implication of an identified ethical principle, theory, or framework.

Examples include autonomy, equal respect, rights, welfare, prioritarianism, capabilities, care ethics, relational autonomy, fair process, disability justice, global justice, animal interests, and public-health ethics.

The Framework policy must still say what should be done in the case.

---

## 6. How a policy was sourced

### Direct source

Use **Direct source** when a source supports substantially the same policy.

The wording does not need to be copied from the source, but the source should genuinely support the recommendation being represented.

### Inferred from source

Use **Inferred from source** when the source supports an underlying preference, judgment, principle, or body of evidence and the Bench states the corresponding policy as a careful inference.

This includes many Framework policies: a published ethical principle may support the reasoning without literally containing the final policy sentence.

### Constructed

Use **Constructed** when the Bench authors a serious comparison policy that is not being attributed to a source.

Constructed policies are allowed. They must remain visibly identified as constructed and must never be presented as though a surveyed public, professional body, community, or named author directly endorsed them.

---

## 7. Do not overstate sources

A source can support a concern without supporting a complete institutional policy.

Do not:

- turn a general attitude into a stronger policy and call it directly sourced;
- combine several source themes into a new composite policy and attribute the composite to the source;
- create a Public or Expert position simply to fill a column;
- present a Framework position as a Public or Expert position;
- use the presence of a citation as proof that the citation supports every word of the policy.

When the Bench adds implementation wording that goes beyond the exact source language but remains a faithful completion of the source position, disclose that in the scholarly source note.

---

## 8. Number of policies

Cases do not need to contain the same number of Public, Expert, and Framework policies.

The number should follow the substance of the case. A case with five serious policies should not be inflated to six or seven merely for symmetry, and a case with seven genuinely distinct policies should not be compressed to fit an arbitrary template.

When SACRE compares unequal numbers of policies across the three types, the evaluation must use an aggregation method that does not reward a policy simply for having more comparison partners.

---

## 9. More than one evaluation setup

The same case may support more than one defensible evaluation setup.

For example, one study may use only strongly source-supported policies, while another may include a clearly marked Constructed policy for comparison. Each setup should state exactly which policies are being used and which type each policy occupies.

The case itself does not change merely because a study selects a different subset of its policies.

---

## 10. Versioning

Once a case version is frozen or released, a substantive change should create a new version.

Substantive changes include changes to:

- case wording that alters the problem;
- policy wording;
- policy type;
- sourcing;
- assumptions;
- cited support that changes the represented position.

Published or released material remains part of the permanent exposure record for later study design.

---

## 11. Review checklist

Before a case is released or used in a formal study, reviewers should confirm:

### Case

- Is the ethical problem clear?
- Are decision-critical facts present?
- Is important uncertainty visible?
- Are assumptions explicit?
- Is jurisdiction/time handled appropriately?
- Does the wording avoid deciding the issue in advance?
- Can an informed non-specialist understand the case?

### Policies

- Do all policies address the same problem?
- Are they genuinely different?
- Are they written at comparable levels of specificity?
- Are any policies redundant or artificially extreme?
- Does every policy have the correct Public / Expert / Framework type for the intended evaluation?
- Is Direct / Inferred / Constructed sourcing accurate?
- Are Constructed policies clearly identified?

### Concise and detailed versions

- Do they describe the same case?
- Do they preserve the same policies and sourcing?
- Does the detailed version add context without silently changing the decision?

### Sources

- Can each source be located?
- Does the source support the claim attributed to it?
- Is the source being used as evidence, professional guidance, an affected perspective, or an ethical framework in a way the source itself warrants?

---

## 12. Automated checks and scholarly review

Automated validation can check structure, IDs, links, required fields, policy types, sourcing labels, version relationships, and citations.

Automated validation cannot determine whether:

- a policy is morally correct;
- two policies are philosophically distinct enough;
- a source genuinely supports a policy;
- Public / Expert / Framework is the best type assignment;
- Direct / Inferred / Constructed is the correct sourcing judgment;
- a case is ethically important or well framed.

Those require scholarly review.
