# M055 — Direct-to-consumer genetic results and relatives' interests

**Case-family identity:** M055  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** consumer genomics / confidentiality  
**Subdomain:** familial genetic risk; DTC testing  
**Tags:** direct-to-consumer genetics, relatives, confidentiality, disclosure, familial risk  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** direct-to-consumer genetic testing service.  
**Decision question:** When a validated consumer genetic result indicates a serious, preventable risk that is likely shared by identifiable close relatives, what disclosure-support policy should the testing service use?

The decision is the service's policy, not the consumer's personal moral duty and not a clinician's duty within an established treatment relationship.

## 2. Concise representation

A direct-to-consumer genetic testing service identifies a result in an adult consumer that, after confirmatory testing, indicates a high risk of a serious inherited cancer syndrome. First-degree relatives have a substantial chance of carrying the same pathogenic variant, and established surveillance could reduce their risk of severe disease. The consumer does not want the service to contact relatives and says the result is private. The service must decide whether confidentiality leaves all family disclosure under the consumer's control while offering communication tools, whether it should actively support and follow up on consumer-mediated disclosure, or whether enrollment should include a narrow pre-consented pathway allowing de-identified warning of identifiable relatives when a serious actionable familial risk is confirmed and the consumer refuses to inform them.

## 3. Detailed representation

A commercial direct-to-consumer genetics service offers health-risk analysis and confirmatory clinical testing for selected high-impact results. An adult consumer is confirmed to carry a pathogenic variant associated with a serious inherited cancer syndrome. Close biological relatives have a substantial probability of carrying the same variant, and evidence-based surveillance or preventive intervention could substantially reduce morbidity or mortality if they learn of the risk. The consumer understands this but does not want relatives contacted, citing privacy and difficult family relationships. The service is not the relatives' treating clinician and does not already hold a fiduciary clinical relationship with them. It must choose its prospective policy: keep results under consumer control while providing counseling and family-letter tools; actively support, remind and facilitate consumer-mediated disclosure without contacting relatives itself; or require at enrollment a narrowly defined consent allowing the service to send a de-identified warning pathway to identifiable relatives when a validated serious actionable familial risk is present and the consumer refuses disclosure. The benchmark assumes any direct warning would reveal the minimum information needed to let a relative seek independent counseling/testing and would not disclose the consumer's full report.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- DTC result has been clinically confirmed;
- familial risk is serious and materially actionable;
- relatives are identifiable and at substantial risk;
- consumer refuses family notification;
- service lacks an ordinary clinician–relative treatment relationship;
- any warning could be de-identified/minimum necessary;
- the strongest warning policy is disclosed prospectively in consumer consent.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

A relative may not carry the variant and may prefer not to know genetic risk. Family contact can also expose relationship information or cause distress. The benchmark fixes substantial actionability but does not assume disclosure is harmless.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral consumer-service ethics. The scenario assumes each represented prospective policy is legally available; it is not a claim about current privacy law.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume the DTC result is clinically confirmed before any family action.
- For this benchmark, assume the familial condition is serious and has effective surveillance/prevention.
- For this benchmark, assume a narrow direct-warning policy can be described prospectively in consent and can minimize disclosure of the consumer's identity/information.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- consumer-controlled confidentiality with communication tools;
- structured consumer-mediated disclosure support and follow-up;
- narrow pre-consented direct warning pathway for serious actionable risk when consumer disclosure fails.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

DTC users show selective, conditional sharing rather than automatic family disclosure. A longitudinal qualitative study found most participants disclosed to some immediate relatives, fewer than half to extended relatives and substantial ambivalence about when and with whom to share. Broader familial-genetics research shows communication may be incomplete and relatives' understanding can depend heavily on how results are explained. This supports both respect for consumer control and the practical importance of active communication assistance, but it does not directly establish support for provider override.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

There is not a mature DTC professional guideline authorizing direct provider warnings to relatives comparable to ordinary clinical genetics guidance. Bioethics scholarship proposes several models. A 2022 analysis argues for moving some responsibility to DTC providers for serious actionable familial risks using pre-emptive consumer consent, de-identification and staged warning that respects a relative's right not to know. This is a normative proposal, not established professional consensus.

The strongest defensible expert-like architecture for present corpus purposes is therefore consumer-mediated disclosure support, not an invented direct-warning professional rule.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Confidentiality / consumer autonomy:** genetic information was obtained through a private consumer relationship → disclosure without permission can undermine trust and expose sensitive family information → keep control with the consumer while offering support.
- **Relational responsibility / shared genetic information:** a variant simultaneously describes risk in biological relatives → confidentiality does not erase foreseeable preventable harm to others → actively facilitate and follow up on consumer-mediated communication.
- **Rule of rescue / minimum-necessary warning:** when risk is serious, preventable and the consumer refuses, the moral cost of silence may exceed the privacy intrusion → a prospectively consented, de-identified, minimal warning can be justified while allowing relatives to decline further information.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- conflating a commercial DTC service with a treating clinician;
- acting on an unconfirmed consumer result;
- treating genetic risk as deterministic;
- ignoring a relative's right not to know;
- making the direct-warning candidate seem professionally established when it is a normative proposal;
- failing to minimize identity/confidential information in any warning pathway.
