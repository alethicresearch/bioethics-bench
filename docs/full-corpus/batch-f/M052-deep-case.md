# M052 — Polygenic embryo screening for non-disease traits

**Case-family identity:** M052  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** reproductive genetics / embryo selection  
**Subdomain:** polygenic embryo screening; non-disease traits  
**Tags:** PGT-P, polygenic scores, embryo selection, height, cognition, eugenics, reproductive autonomy  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** fertility-clinic policy committee.  
**Decision question:** Should a fertility clinic offer embryo ranking using polygenic scores for non-disease traits such as predicted height or cognition-related measures?

The case is specifically about non-disease trait ranking. Polygenic disease-risk screening is included only as a comparator boundary, not as the primary decision.

## 2. Concise representation

A fertility clinic is approached by a commercial genetics service offering polygenic embryo scores for non-disease traits such as predicted adult height and cognition-related educational measures. The scores are probabilistic, depend partly on ancestry and environment, and can only modestly shift expected trait distributions among the limited embryos available in a typical IVF cycle. The clinic already performs validated genetic testing for serious monogenic disease. It must decide whether to prohibit embryo ranking for non-medical traits, allow informed patients to use available trait scores as an exercise of reproductive choice, or limit polygenic embryo ranking to health-related disease-risk applications that meet a defined evidence threshold.

## 3. Detailed representation

A fertility clinic is revising its policy on polygenic embryo screening. A commercial laboratory can calculate polygenic scores from IVF embryos for multifactorial disease risks and for non-disease traits such as predicted adult height or cognition-related educational measures. For this benchmark, assume the laboratory's analytic pipeline is technically reliable in the narrow sense that it reproduces its specified scores, but the trait predictions remain probabilistic, ancestry-sensitive and substantially influenced by environment; the expected difference between embryos available within one IVF cycle is modest. The clinic's question is not whether parents may receive ordinary genetic information, but whether clinicians should participate in ranking embryos specifically by non-disease trait scores. The clinic must choose among prohibiting such trait ranking, permitting it after detailed counseling about limitations and social risks, or allowing polygenic ranking only for validated health-related disease risks while excluding non-medical traits.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- requested use concerns non-disease traits;
- scores are probabilistic rather than deterministic;
- predictive performance varies across ancestry/context and environmental effects remain substantial;
- within-family embryo differences are modest;
- clinic already distinguishes monogenic disease testing from polygenic trait ranking;
- the decision is clinic participation, not criminal prohibition of parent preferences.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Trait prediction and social meaning will change as datasets and methods evolve. The benchmark intentionally does not stipulate near-perfect predictive accuracy because current clinical controversy includes whether modest probabilistic predictions should be used at all.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Evidence-dated 2026 fertility-clinic policy. The scenario assumes the clinic is legally permitted to choose whether to offer the service.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume the commercial score is analytically reproducible but only modestly predictive of non-disease traits.
- For this benchmark, assume all represented clinic policies are lawful.
- For this benchmark, assume patients receive accurate counseling about uncertainty and ancestry limitations if the clinic offers the service.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- prohibit nonmedical polygenic trait ranking;
- permit informed reproductive choice despite probabilistic limitations;
- restrict polygenic embryo ranking to health-related applications meeting a defined evidence/actionability threshold.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

A nationally representative US survey found that attitudes toward polygenic embryo screening vary substantially by what is being screened. Later US research comparing conditions and traits found stronger eugenics concern around trait selection than around disease conditions. Interviews with IVF patients similarly identify a sharper moral boundary around selecting for height, cognition and other traits than around reducing health risks. These studies support a disease-versus-trait distinction and broad public ambivalence, but further candidate audit is needed to determine whether they support two action-distinct clinic policies rather than simply different levels of approval.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

ASRM's 2026 Ethics Committee opinion states that PGT-P remains nascent and unproven, is not recommended for current clinical use and should remain under IRB supervision; it specifically says PGT-P should not be used for trait selection such as height or intelligence. A 2025 survey of US reproductive endocrinologists also found very low approval for physical or behavioral trait screening, though clinicians were more divided on whether PES should be legally allowed in general.

Current expert guidance therefore converges strongly against clinic use for non-disease traits. The fact that experts debate PES generally should not be used to manufacture a professional pro-trait-selection candidate.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Reproductive liberty:** parents ordinarily make many consequential choices affecting future children → uncertain trait information can be one input among others if coercion and deception are absent → allow informed use of trait scores.
- **Child open-future / anti-instrumentalization:** selecting embryos for socially valued performance traits can impose expectations and treat future persons as designed projects → reproductive liberty does not require clinical participation in every preference → prohibit nonmedical trait ranking.
- **Justice / anti-eugenic boundary:** polygenic scores reflect population data, social valuation and unequal predictive performance → clinical endorsement can intensify stratification and encode contested norms as biological merit → restrict embryo ranking to sufficiently validated health-risk applications rather than enhancement traits.
