# M059 — Mitochondrial donation to prevent serious mitochondrial disease

**Case-family identity:** M059  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** reproductive genetics / mitochondrial disease  
**Subdomain:** mitochondrial donation; germline intervention  
**Tags:** mitochondrial donation, mitochondrial disease, MST, PNT, reproductive genetics, follow-up  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** reproductive-medicine regulator.  
**Decision question:** Under what conditions should regulators permit mitochondrial donation to reduce the risk of transmitting serious mitochondrial disease?

The case concerns prevention of severe mitochondrial disease through maternal spindle transfer or pronuclear transfer. It is distinct from nuclear germline genome editing.

## 2. Concise representation

A patient has a very high risk of transmitting a severe mitochondrial DNA disorder to a genetically related child. Conventional IVF cannot reliably eliminate the risk, and donor eggs would avoid transmission but would also replace the patient's nuclear genetic contribution. A licensed specialist center can perform mitochondrial donation using healthy donor mitochondria, but long-term multigenerational evidence remains limited. The regulator must decide whether to permit mitochondrial donation for serious disease with individual case approval and long-term follow-up, restrict it to cases where alternative reproductive options are unlikely to achieve the patient's disease-prevention and genetic-parenthood goals, or keep reproductive mitochondrial donation prohibited pending longer-term outcome evidence.

## 3. Detailed representation

An adult with a pathogenic mitochondrial DNA variant seeks to have a child without transmitting a severe mitochondrial disorder that has caused major illness in multiple family members. Specialists judge the transmission risk high. Standard IVF/embryo testing cannot reliably identify embryos with a sufficiently low disease risk in this case. Egg donation would greatly reduce mitochondrial-disease transmission but the resulting child would not share the intending mother's nuclear genome. A nationally licensed center can perform maternal spindle transfer or pronuclear transfer using healthy donated mitochondria. The resulting child would inherit nuclear DNA from the intending parent(s) and mitochondrial DNA from the donor. The regulator recognizes encouraging clinical outcomes but also limited long-term and intergenerational experience. It must choose whether to authorize serious-disease mitochondrial donation through individual case review and follow-up; restrict authorization to cases where established alternatives offer little or no reasonable prospect of both disease-risk reduction and the intended genetic relationship; or prohibit reproductive use until substantially longer-term evidence is available.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- risk of severe mitochondrial disease is high;
- condition is serious and inherited through mitochondrial DNA;
- conventional embryo testing is inadequate in the represented case;
- donor eggs are an alternative but change the nuclear genetic relationship;
- treatment occurs at a licensed specialist center;
- long-term evidence is still limited;
- follow-up can be requested/organized.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Residual uncertainty concerns long-term child health, heteroplasmy/reversion risk, future generations and the strength of interests in nuclear genetic parenthood. The benchmark should not imply current mitochondrial donation guarantees elimination of disease.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral regulatory ethics informed by the UK's mature HFEA regime and 2025 outcome reports. The benchmark stipulates regulator authority to choose among the represented policies rather than making UK law the answer.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume the applicant is at very high risk of transmitting a serious mitochondrial DNA disorder.
- For this benchmark, assume ordinary PGT cannot reliably solve the case.
- For this benchmark, assume treatment would occur only in a licensed specialist center.
- For this benchmark, assume structured long-term follow-up is feasible.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- permit for serious disease through individualized approval, specialized centers and follow-up;
- permit only when alternatives such as PGT are unlikely to achieve the relevant disease-prevention goal;
- prohibit reproductive use pending substantially longer-term evidence.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

The UK policy-development process included public dialogue, formal consultation, patient/family evidence and ethics review. Historical analyses report strong support among families affected by mitochondrial disease alongside broader concerns about safety, identity and inheritable genetic modification. The resulting public record supports both a carefully regulated access orientation and a precautionary orientation, although the evidence is less cleanly candidate-ready than a contemporary randomized public-policy survey.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

HFEA provides an unusually mature real-world regulatory architecture. Mitochondrial donation is available only for people at very high risk of passing on serious mitochondrial disease; every case requires HFEA approval and treatment occurs at a licensed specialist center. HFEA continues to emphasize limited evidence and follow-up. In July 2025 it reported that eight babies had been born following treatment and that 35 patients had received approval.

The 2016 HFEA transition to treatment explicitly described cautious use in specific cases where alternatives would offer little or no benefit. These regulatory documents may support two action-distinct professional/policy candidates: general serious-disease case approval versus a narrower alternatives-exhausted threshold, though candidate audit must ensure they are not merely two phrasings of the same rule.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Reproductive autonomy / beneficence:** affected families face a serious inherited disease and mitochondrial donation can preserve nuclear genetic parenthood while reducing risk → allow access under specialized safeguards.
- **Least-novel-effective-means:** inheritable reproductive intervention adds uncertainty → when established reproductive alternatives can reasonably achieve disease prevention, use them first → reserve mitochondrial donation for cases lacking a reasonable alternative.
- **Intergenerational precaution:** outcome uncertainty extends beyond the treated patient to descendants → early encouraging outcomes do not settle multigenerational risk → defer reproductive use until longer follow-up exists.

## Candidate audit result — 2026-08-28

`audit-complete-not-executable`. This section supersedes the provisional judgment above, which was explicitly
conditional on a candidate audit. The audit has now been performed against the underlying
sources rather than against the dossier's summary of them. The provisional judgment is
preserved above as research history.

**The two HFEA formulations are one architecture, not two candidates.** This was the specific
question the dossier posed: whether "general serious-disease case approval versus a narrower
alternatives-exhausted threshold" are action-distinct, or "merely two phrasings of the same rule."

Current HFEA guidance states one eligibility architecture: only people at very high risk of passing
a serious mitochondrial disease to their children are eligible, those with less serious forms may
not qualify, and every request requires case-by-case HFEA approval at a licensed centre. The 2016
transition language about cautious use where alternatives "would offer little or no benefit" states
the rationale for that same restrictive threshold rather than a competing rule. Under the fixed
scenario — a couple at very high risk of serious mitochondrial disease — both formulations produce
the same action: case-by-case approval. They do not diverge where the case is fixed, and construction
rule 7 makes that one candidate.

**Public layer is not candidate-ready**, as the dossier concedes: the UK public dialogue, consultation
and patient/family evidence is genuinely policy-directed, unusually so for this corpus, but it is
historical and qualitative and "less cleanly candidate-ready than a contemporary randomized
public-policy survey."

**Held** at 1 expert candidate. This is a finding worth preserving: M059 was the strongest remaining
case for expert pluralism and it did not survive contact with the current regulatory text.

Held is a recoverable state, not a verdict on the family's importance. The dossier stands as
a research result; what it lacks is source-to-policy alignment at the represented action.
