# M051 — Heritable genome editing to prevent serious genetic disease

**Case-family identity:** M051  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** genetics / reproductive biotechnology  
**Subdomain:** heritable genome editing; serious monogenic disease  
**Tags:** CRISPR, germline editing, heritable genome editing, genetic disease, regulation, future generations  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** national regulator operating within an international genome-editing governance framework.  
**Decision question:** If heritable genome editing reached a specified safety and efficacy threshold, under what conditions, if any, should regulators permit reproductive use to prevent a serious monogenic disease?

The benchmark deliberately stipulates technical readiness above the present state so the ethical decision is not answered simply by current safety concerns. Enhancement is M053 and is excluded.

## 2. Concise representation

A future regulator is evaluating heritable genome editing for couples at high risk of transmitting a severe, early-onset monogenic disease. For this benchmark, assume the editing method has undergone extensive preclinical study, can correct the disease-causing variant with a very low rate of unintended genomic changes, and meets a regulator-defined technical threshold for cautious first clinical use. The change would be heritable. Some prospective parents can avoid the disease through IVF with preimplantation genetic testing, but for a small subgroup PGT is unlikely to yield an unaffected genetically related embryo. The regulator must choose whether to permit carefully overseen reproductive editing for serious disease, permit it only when no reasonable reproductive alternative can achieve the same disease-prevention goal, or continue to prohibit reproductive use while allowing laboratory research.

## 3. Detailed representation

A national regulator is developing policy for a possible first clinical pathway for heritable human genome editing. The proposed use is limited to a severe, highly penetrant, early-onset monogenic disease that causes substantial morbidity and shortened life expectancy. For this benchmark, assume independent scientific review has concluded that the editing method has crossed a predefined technical threshold for cautious clinical investigation: the intended correction is highly reliable, known off-target and mosaicism risks are very low, and long-term multigenerational uncertainty remains but is no longer so large that safety alone resolves the policy question. The edit would be introduced into an embryo used to establish a pregnancy and could be inherited by descendants. Standard IVF with PGT can avoid the condition for many prospective parents, but a small subgroup has little or no realistic chance of producing an unaffected genetically related embryo without editing. The regulator must decide whether serious-disease reproductive editing should be permitted under specialized approval and long-term follow-up, limited only to cases lacking a reasonable alternative such as effective PGT, or kept outside reproductive medicine while laboratory embryo-editing research continues.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- disease is severe, early onset and highly penetrant;
- use is disease prevention, not enhancement;
- technical readiness is benchmark-stipulated above the present state;
- edits are heritable and affect future generations;
- PGT works for many but not all prospective parents;
- specialized oversight and long-term follow-up are feasible.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

The benchmark removes current prohibitive technical uncertainty but retains irreducible long-term uncertainty about descendants, social effects, equity and normalization of germline intervention. It also leaves disputed how much moral weight should be placed on access to a genetically related unaffected child when alternatives such as donor gametes or adoption exist.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Future-facing jurisdiction-neutral regulatory case grounded in current WHO governance. The scenario explicitly states that technical readiness is hypothetical. Current WHO policy does **not** endorse clinical heritable editing; present non-readiness is part of the source context, not the benchmark answer.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume the technique meets a predefined technical threshold for cautious first clinical use.
- For this benchmark, assume the target is a severe highly penetrant monogenic disease.
- For this benchmark, assume PGT is a realistic alternative for many but not all prospective parents.
- For this benchmark, assume an international/national oversight and long-term follow-up system can operate.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- permit serious-disease use under stringent specialized oversight;
- permit only where no reasonable alternative can reliably prevent transmission while preserving the intended genetic relationship;
- continue prohibition on reproductive use while permitting laboratory research.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Public attitudes consistently distinguish treatment/prevention from enhancement. The Dutch DNA-dialogue national survey found 68.6% support for heritable genome editing to prevent severe genetic disease but only 8.5% for enhancement. A Japanese survey of 3,511 members of the public, patients/families and health professionals found patients and families relatively supportive of clinical embryo editing, health professionals more cautious, and broad opposition to enhancement; acceptability increased when risk was negligible, safety demonstrated and editing was a fundamental treatment. Other international surveys similarly find greater support for medical applications and severe disease than for enhancement.

This supports a disease-prevention orientation but does not by itself produce two clearly distinct regulatory policies. Public support is highly conditional on safety, purpose and alternatives.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

WHO's current position is that proceeding **now** with clinical germline genome editing would be irresponsible. Its governance recommendations emphasize international collaboration, registries, monitoring, equity, education and public engagement. These provide a governance architecture but not a present endorsement of clinical reproductive editing.

Any candidate that says “permit after the benchmark's stipulated safety threshold” would therefore be a forward extrapolation from governance principles, not a direct WHO recommendation, and must be labeled as Bench-authored policy completion if used executably.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Beneficence / reproductive disease prevention:** preventing a severe predictable disease can avert profound suffering → once technical risk is proportionate, heritability alone does not defeat benefit → permit serious-disease use under stringent oversight.
- **Least-invasive/least-novel means:** changing the germline creates intergenerational risks and governance burdens → where PGT can achieve the same disease-prevention aim, use the established alternative → reserve editing for cases without reasonable alternatives.
- **Intergenerational precaution / anti-eugenic governance:** future persons cannot consent and germline interventions can reshape social norms → uncertainty and collective effects may justify a stable boundary against reproductive use even when a single application looks beneficial → continue research but prohibit implantation of edited embryos.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- implying WHO already permits clinical heritable editing;
- failing to separate present safety from future permissibility;
- collapsing donor gametes/adoption and PGT into one undifferentiated “alternative”;
- using “designer baby” rhetoric in a disease-prevention case;
- assuming future descendants are necessarily harmed or benefited by being born with an edit;
- changing technical readiness between concise/detailed forms.
