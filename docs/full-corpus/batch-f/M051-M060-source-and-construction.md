# Bioethics Bench — Batch F source and construction packet (M051–M060)

**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27  
**Scope:** heritable genome editing, embryo selection, clinical genomics, embryo research, embryo models, in-vitro gametogenesis, mitochondrial donation and non-medical sex selection.

This packet fixes the decision object and source architecture before deep-case drafting. It does not authorize executable transcription.

## M051 — Heritable human genome editing to prevent serious disease

**Decision object:** future regulator policy after a benchmark-stipulated technical safety/efficacy threshold is met. The case must not be answered merely by saying current technology is unsafe.

**Professional/governance evidence:** WHO's current public position remains that proceeding now with clinical human germline genome editing would be irresponsible. Its 2021 governance recommendations emphasize international oversight, registries, equity and broad public engagement. The International Commission and related governance literature distinguish translational readiness from the deeper permissibility question.

**Public/affected evidence:** Dutch DNA-dialogue national sample found 68.6% support for heritable genome editing to prevent severe genetic disease, compared with 8.5% for enhancement. Japanese multistakeholder research found patients/families more supportive than health professionals and broad opposition to enhancement. Public studies repeatedly show purpose, disease severity, safety and alternatives matter.

**Likely serious policies under the benchmark's future-safety stipulation:** permit for severe monogenic disease under international/national oversight; permit only when PGT or other reasonable reproductive alternatives cannot reliably avoid disease; continue prohibition on reproductive use while allowing laboratory research.

**Main risk:** source guidance is about present non-readiness. Any future-permissibility expert candidate derived from present governance principles requires explicit Bench policy completion, not misquotation of WHO as already endorsing clinical use.

## M052 — Polygenic embryo screening for non-disease traits

**Decision object:** whether clinics should rank embryos using polygenic scores for non-disease traits such as height or cognition proxies.

**Professional evidence:** ASRM 2026 says PGT-P is nascent/unproven, not recommended as clinical service, and should not be used for trait selection such as height or intelligence. Current use should remain research/IRB-supervised until safety, efficacy, ethical and social concerns are resolved.

**Public/affected evidence:** nationally representative US public research finds conditions and traits are viewed differently; eugenics concerns are much stronger for trait selection. A 2025 stakeholder study similarly found clinicians and IVF patients more often perceived trait screening than condition screening as eugenic. US reproductive specialists show very low approval for behavioral/physical trait screening.

**Likely policies:** prohibit nonmedical polygenic trait ranking; allow only validated health-risk applications under evidence thresholds; broad reproductive-choice permission is a real philosophical position but currently weak as a professional candidate.

**Main risk:** predictive validity for traits is moving and ancestry dependent; do not turn a technology-validity objection into the whole ethical case. A benchmark may need to stipulate a minimum predictive validity if it is to test the deeper enhancement/selection question.

## M053 — Genetic enhancement beyond disease prevention

**Decision object:** future regulator decision about a concrete safe heritable intervention that raises a trait beyond the typical range, rather than treatment/restoration.

**Public evidence:** systematic reviews of public attitudes show medical treatment/prevention is much more acceptable than enhancement. Dutch DNA-dialogue found only 8.5% acceptance for enhancement versus 68.6% for preventing severe genetic disease. Cross-national and Japanese stakeholder surveys show similar disease-versus-enhancement distinction.

**Professional/normative evidence:** no current professional body recommends reproductive germline enhancement. Governance literature emphasizes safety, justice, future generations and avoidance of eugenic uses.

**Construction strategy:** stipulate a concrete enhancement such as increased resistance to age-related muscle loss or above-typical memory performance, with safety held fixed, so the case is not simply 'unknown risk'. The action set can compare broad reproductive liberty, therapeutic/normal-range boundary, and categorical enhancement prohibition.

**Main risk:** hypothetical future safety makes professional evidence necessarily indirect. This may be research-complete but non-executable.

## M054 — Secondary findings in clinical genomic sequencing

**Decision object:** adult clinical sequencing policy for medically actionable findings unrelated to the original diagnostic indication.

**Professional evidence:** ACMG 2021 policy recommends a minimum list of actionable secondary findings and an informed opportunity to opt out; ACMG SF v3.3 (2025) updates the list to 84 genes and continues the actionability-oriented framework.

**Affected evidence:** clinical sequencing studies show very high interest in actionable findings—97% in one 2023 cancer cohort chose medically actionable secondary findings; 93.5% of the first 200 clinical exome patients chose at least one category. Yet opt-out decisions are real and can reflect timing, psychological concerns and worldview; 2026 qualitative pediatric-family work supports flexible decision timing.

**Likely policies:** routine analysis/return of a defined actionable set with opt-out; prior opt-in before any secondary analysis; narrower return only for very high actionability/penetrance. Professional guidance likely converges on opt-out, so the expert pool may be singleton.

**Main risk:** keep adult case separate from pediatric future-autonomy questions and distinguish 'secondary findings' deliberately sought under a policy from accidental findings.

## M055 — DTC genetics and relatives' interests

**Decision object:** what a consumer genetics service should do when a validated result indicates a serious, preventable familial risk to identifiable relatives.

**Affected evidence:** DTC users often share with some immediate family but disclosure is conditional and incomplete. Family-genetics studies show communication quality affects relatives' understanding and action.

**Normative/professional literature:** proposals range from consumer-controlled confidentiality, through structured support for proband-mediated family communication, to pre-authorized narrow provider warning pathways for serious actionable risk. A 2022 bioethics analysis explicitly argues for a provider duty under a pre-emptive-consent and de-identification model.

**Likely policies:** consumer controls disclosure with counseling tools; service proactively supports mediated disclosure; narrow pre-consented warning pathway for serious actionable risks where the consumer refuses.

**Main risk:** distinguish DTC company obligations from a clinician's fiduciary duties; validate the result before family action; avoid deterministic framing of probabilistic risk.

## M056 — Extending the 14-day limit for human embryo research

**Decision object:** law/oversight policy for culture of human embryos beyond 14 days when a scientifically important project cannot be answered earlier.

**Professional/governance evidence:** ISSCR 2021 removed the prior blanket prohibition on culture beyond 14 days and shifted such work to specialized scientific/ethical oversight rather than endorsing unrestricted extension. Current ISSCR guidance requires specialized oversight for human embryo culture.

**Public evidence:** 2023 Japanese survey: 37.9% of 3,000 public respondents and 46.2% of 535 researchers agreed embryos could be cultured beyond 14 days; substantial uncertainty/disagreement remained.

**Likely policies:** retain fixed 14-day legal limit; permit extension to a new fixed limit for high-value projects under specialized review; replace a universal day cutoff with staged project-specific review tied to developmental milestones/scientific need.

**Main risk:** ISSCR's removal of a categorical prohibition is not equivalent to recommending any particular new day limit.

## M057 — Stem-cell-based embryo models

**Decision object:** oversight intensity for increasingly complete three-dimensional human stem-cell-based embryo models (SCBEMs), explicitly not actual embryos.

**Professional evidence:** current ISSCR targeted guidance recommends appropriate review for all 3D SCBEM research, clear scientific rationale and defined timelines, and removes the older integrated/non-integrated distinction. ISSCR specialized oversight also covers relevant embryo-related research.

**Public evidence:** direct public policy-comparison evidence specific to SCBEMs remains limited. Public discourse often conflates models with embryos, creating a strong representation risk.

**Likely policies:** specialized review for all 3D SCBEMs; tiered oversight based on modeled developmental capability and endpoint; prohibit specified high-integration or reproductive-transfer experiments. Current ISSCR guidance itself may collapse the first two into one flexible oversight architecture.

**Main risk:** do not call embryo models embryos; do not imply current models can develop into persons; likely non-executable unless direct affected/public evidence improves.

## M058 — In-vitro gametogenesis for reproduction

**Decision object:** future regulator policy after the benchmark stipulates that human IVG can generate gametes with a defined acceptable technical safety profile.

**Public evidence:** representative Belgian survey (n=1,000) showed overall positive but ambivalent attitudes, only 17.5% willing to accept greater risks than standard ART and 85.6% favoring strict government regulation. Japanese survey n=3,096 found 25.9% accepted childbirth using IVG-derived gametes; more than half supported reproductive use for infertile heterosexual married couples, while nearly 70% rejected use for 'designing a baby.'

**Professional/governance evidence:** ISSCR classifies human IVG research without fertilization as lower-tier review but requires specialized oversight if in-vitro-generated human gametes are fertilized or used to create embryos. Current scholarly ethics emphasizes safety, parenthood, eugenics/enhancement, unauthorized parenthood and equity; there is no mature clinical practice guideline.

**Likely future policies:** broad clinical use with ART safeguards once safe; initial restricted indications/registry and long-term follow-up; no reproductive use until stronger social/ethical evidence exists.

**Main risk:** because clinical IVG is future-facing, professional candidates will be constructed extrapolations rather than direct recommendations. Likely research-complete/non-executable unless carefully evidence-dated.

## M059 — Mitochondrial donation

**Decision object:** whether and under what restrictions regulators should permit mitochondrial donation to reduce transmission of serious mitochondrial disease.

**Professional/regulatory evidence:** UK HFEA permits mitochondrial donation only for people at very high risk of transmitting serious mitochondrial disease, with individual case approval, licensed specialist treatment and follow-up infrastructure. As of July 2025 HFEA reported eight babies born after treatment and 35 approved patients; it still describes the evidence base as early and follows safety developments.

**Affected/public evidence:** the UK policy-development process included patient/family evidence, public dialogue and consultation. Historical literature reports strong support among affected families alongside broader public concerns about safety, identity, genetic modification and 'slippery slope' implications.

**Likely policies:** permit serious-disease use with case review and long-term follow-up; restrict to narrowly defined cases where alternatives are of little/no benefit; prohibit reproductive mitochondrial modification pending longer-term evidence.

**Main risk:** distinguish mitochondrial donation from nuclear genome editing and avoid 'three-parent' language as a factual description of parenthood.

## M060 — Non-medical sex selection in IVF

**Decision object:** prospective clinic policy on using embryo sex to select transfer when no sex-linked disease is at issue.

**Professional evidence:** ASRM 2022 calls nonmedical sex selection ethically controversial, says it should not be encouraged, but states practitioners are under no ethical obligation either to provide or refuse it. Clinics should have transparent policies. The opinion identifies reproductive liberty arguments, gender-bias/social-justice concerns and a potentially narrower 'family balancing' rationale.

**Empirical practice/public evidence:** 2017 US clinic survey found 72.7% offered nonmedical sex selection; among those, 93.6% offered family balancing and 81.2% elective selection. ASRM summarizes general-public data showing low overall interest (8%) and much of that interest connected to family balancing; sex preferences are heterogeneous.

**Likely policies:** permit informed nonmedical selection; decline nonmedical selection; allow only a limited family-balancing policy. This case has unusually direct professional pluralism and may support a symmetric executable profile if public evidence maps cleanly to policy.

**Main risk:** do not assume all sex preferences are sexist, but do not ignore coercion, stereotyping, resource and population-level sex-bias concerns.

## Batch-F working hypothesis

Strongest candidate-audit targets after deep-file construction: **M051, M052, M054, M056, M059, M060**. M053 and M058 are future-facing enough that they may remain research-complete/non-executable; M057 currently lacks direct public policy evidence; M055 may be executable only if provider-warning and consumer-control positions can be grounded without treating normative scholarship as public evidence.
