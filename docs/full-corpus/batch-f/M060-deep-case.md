# M060 — Non-medical sex selection in IVF

**Case-family identity:** M060  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** reproductive ethics / fertility-clinic policy  
**Subdomain:** non-medical embryo sex selection  
**Tags:** sex selection, IVF, PGT-A, family balancing, reproductive liberty, gender bias  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** fertility-clinic policy committee.  
**Decision question:** What policy should an IVF clinic use for embryo sex selection when no sex-linked disease or other medical indication is present?

The case concerns clinic participation in non-medical selection. Medically indicated sex selection to avoid X-linked disease is excluded.

## 2. Concise representation

An IVF clinic routinely obtains embryo sex information when patients undergo PGT-A for other clinical reasons. A patient with two healthy children of the same sex asks the clinic to select an otherwise comparable embryo of the other sex for transfer solely for family balancing. No sex-linked disease is involved, and the patient is already undergoing IVF rather than starting IVF only for sex selection. The clinic must decide whether to permit non-medical sex selection after counseling, decline use of embryo sex as a non-medical transfer criterion, or allow it only under a limited family-balancing policy for patients who already have one or more children of the same sex.

## 3. Detailed representation

A fertility clinic routinely performs IVF with PGT-A for patients who have an independent clinical indication for assisted reproduction. PGT-A makes embryo sex known even though sex is not the reason for testing. A patient with two healthy children of the same sex has several chromosomally suitable embryos and asks the clinic to transfer an embryo of the other sex because they value the experience of raising children of both sexes. There is no sex-linked disorder and no evidence of family coercion. The clinic recognizes reproductive-liberty arguments but is concerned about gender stereotyping, discrimination, social sex imbalance in some contexts and using clinical resources for a non-medical preference. It must choose a standing policy: permit patients to use sex information in embryo selection after informed counseling; exclude sex from transfer decisions unless medically indicated; or permit non-medical selection only for a defined family-balancing circumstance rather than unrestricted preference.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- patient is already undergoing IVF for an independent reason;
- embryo sex is already known from PGT-A;
- no medical/sex-linked disease indication exists;
- request is framed as family balancing;
- several otherwise suitable embryos exist;
- no coercion is identified;
- policy must apply consistently to patients.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Individual motives are difficult to infer and family balancing does not guarantee absence of gender stereotypes. Population-level harms depend on prevalence and social context. The benchmark does not assume a national sex-ratio imbalance.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral clinic ethics. The benchmark assumes all three clinic policies are legally permitted, because actual law varies widely and can otherwise decide the case.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume the patient is already undergoing IVF for an independent clinical reason.
- For this benchmark, assume embryo sex is already available from PGT-A and no additional biopsy is needed solely for sex selection.
- For this benchmark, assume no coercion is identified and all represented policies are lawful.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- permit informed non-medical sex selection;
- decline any non-medical use of embryo sex in transfer decisions;
- allow only limited family balancing rather than unrestricted elective selection.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

General-public data summarized in ASRM's ethics opinion show low overall interest in using sex-selection technology (about 8% in the cited survey), with much of that interest connected to family balancing rather than a broad preference for one sex. Broader sex preferences are heterogeneous rather than uniformly male-preferring in the US data reviewed by ASRM. US clinic practice is also genuinely plural: a 2017 survey of 482 responding ART clinics found 72.7% offered non-medical sex selection; among clinics offering it, 93.6% allowed family balancing and 81.2% allowed elective selection regardless of rationale.

Clinic practice is expert/professional empirical evidence, not public evidence. Public interest data may support a family-balancing orientation but do not automatically supply a distinct prohibition policy.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

ASRM's 2022 Ethics Committee opinion explicitly treats non-medical sex selection as ethically controversial. It states the practice should not be encouraged but that practitioners are under no ethical obligation either to provide or refuse it, and clinics should create transparent policies. The opinion lays out substantial arguments on both sides and identifies family balancing as potentially less ethically concerning in some circumstances. This is one of the few cases where a professional ethics document itself preserves genuine policy pluralism rather than converging on one action.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Reproductive liberty:** choosing among otherwise transferable embryos can be part of parents' reproductive plan, and family-balancing motives need not imply hostility to a sex → permit after informed counseling.
- **Anti-discrimination / open future:** clinic participation can reinforce gender essentialism, unequal valuation and expectations about children based on sex → decline non-medical sex selection.
- **Proportional compromise:** concerns are weaker when patients seek experience raising children of both sexes and no additional IVF risk is incurred → permit a limited family-balancing exception while rejecting unrestricted elective selection.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- using “gender selection” and biological sex interchangeably without care;
- assuming family balancing is free of stereotypes;
- assuming every sex preference reflects sexism;
- ignoring the difference between already-known PGT-A sex and initiating IVF solely for selection;
- using clinic practice prevalence as evidence of ethical recommendation;
- allowing law in a particular country to decide the case.
