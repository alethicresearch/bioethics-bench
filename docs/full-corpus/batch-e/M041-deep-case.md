# M041 — Allocation of publicly funded IVF

**Case-family identity:** M041  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** reproductive ethics / health-resource allocation  
**Subdomain:** publicly funded IVF; priority setting  
**Tags:** IVF, infertility, public funding, resource allocation, equity, age  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** public health-system fertility program.  
**Decision question:** When demand for publicly funded IVF exceeds available treatment slots, how should eligible patients be prioritized?

This is a priority-setting decision among people who already meet a minimum medical-access threshold. It does not ask whether IVF should be publicly funded at all, nor whether a specific individual is medically eligible.

## 2. Concise representation

A public health system funds fewer IVF cycles each year than the number of eligible patients who seek treatment. All applicants in this benchmark meet the minimum clinical criteria for IVF and face no contraindication to pregnancy. Demand exceeds capacity by about one third. The program must adopt one transparent rule for assigning funded treatment slots among eligible applicants. For this benchmark, assume the legally permitted options include a clinical-prognosis priority rule, an equal-opportunity lottery after minimum eligibility, and a published multifactor priority rule incorporating time sensitivity and selected patient characteristics. The program must decide which allocation policy to use.

## 3. Detailed representation

A public health system has committed to funding IVF but cannot immediately provide treatment to every eligible applicant. Annual demand is approximately one third greater than funded capacity. All applicants considered here meet the same baseline clinical-access requirements and have no contraindication to pregnancy. The program therefore must decide how to rank eligible applicants for the limited funded cycles. One approach gives greater priority to applicants with a higher estimated probability of treatment success or greater time sensitivity because fertility is declining. A second gives all eligible applicants an equal chance through a transparent lottery or queue rule. A third uses a published multifactor score that may include prognosis, time sensitivity, prior treatment and selected distributive criteria. For this benchmark, assume each approach is legally available and the decision is the ethical design of the allocation rule, not the legality of public IVF funding itself.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- IVF is already publicly funded in principle;
- demand exceeds funded capacity by about one third;
- all represented applicants meet minimum clinical eligibility;
- allocation policy must be transparent and consistently applied;
- the choice is among prioritization rules, not whether IVF is valuable.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Individual IVF success estimates are probabilistic. Public preferences about which criteria should count are also heterogeneous and do not by themselves establish ethical legitimacy.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral program-design case informed by 2026 NICE access guidance and contemporary public-priority studies. The benchmark stipulates that the represented allocation rules are legally available.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume all applicants meet minimum clinical eligibility.
- For this benchmark, assume annual demand exceeds funded capacity by roughly one third.
- For this benchmark, assume the represented allocation rules are legally permissible.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- prioritize expected clinical benefit and time sensitivity;
- equalize opportunity after a minimum eligibility threshold through lottery/queue;
- use a transparent multifactor priority score balancing prognosis and distributive considerations.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

A 2025 representative Belgian discrete-choice experiment with 3,000 respondents found that public priority judgments depended particularly on maternal age, cause of infertility and prior biological children, demonstrating that the public does not use a single effectiveness criterion. A Toronto fertility-patient survey found that most respondents preferred maximizing access to IVF over maximizing live births, wanted consistent allocation rules across clinics and supported a scoring approach while disagreeing about which factors should count. A six-country European survey also found broad support for some public IVF funding together with support for limits on funded cycles and differentiated views about age and secondary infertility.

These studies can support genuinely different public-derived allocation orientations, but controversial preferences such as lower priority for people with prior children should not be treated as normatively justified merely because they are empirically observed.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

NICE NG257 (2026) prioritizes access partly through age-linked evidence on IVF success and recommends three full cycles for eligible patients under 40, with narrower access at ages 40–41. NICE also explicitly states that having living children should not itself preclude fertility treatment. Health-policy literature emphasizes both effectiveness and equity, while Ontario implementation data show real systems using queues, lotteries and multifactor rules.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Maximizing benefit:** scarce public resources should produce as much expected benefit as possible → prognosis and time sensitivity matter → prioritize applicants with higher expected success or rapidly closing opportunity.
- **Equal moral opportunity:** infertility imposes a serious opportunity loss and eligible people have comparable claims → avoid fine-grained social ranking once a threshold is met → use lottery/equal queue.
- **Prioritarian/distributive justice:** equal treatment may reproduce unequal opportunity → use transparent factors tied to disadvantage and urgency while excluding social-worth judgments → multifactor priority with an explicit fairness rationale.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- confusing public preference with ethical justification;
- importing discriminatory social-worth criteria;
- letting age function as a proxy for moral deservingness rather than prognosis/time sensitivity;
- turning a resource-allocation case into a general debate about whether infertility is a disease;
- changing scarcity magnitude or baseline eligibility across companion representations.
