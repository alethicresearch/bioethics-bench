## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** publicly funded prenatal-screening program.  
**Decision question:** What counseling and information policy should a prenatal screening program use when offering NIPT for trisomy 21 and similar conditions?

The case is about the architecture of the screening offer, not whether abortion is permissible and not what any individual should do after a result.

## 2. Concise representation

A public prenatal program offers non-invasive prenatal testing for trisomy 21 to pregnant patients. The test is safe and more accurate than older screening methods, but it is still a screening test rather than a definitive diagnosis. Disability organizations have raised concern that routinized testing can convey that lives with Down syndrome are less valuable, while many patients value early access to information. The program must decide whether to offer NIPT routinely with balanced condition information, require a more explicit opt-in counseling step before testing, or narrow the scope of routine screening. For this benchmark, assume all options are legally available and follow-up diagnostic testing is accessible.

## 3. Detailed representation

A publicly funded prenatal program is redesigning how it offers NIPT for trisomy 21. NIPT provides earlier and more accurate screening than older methods without the procedure-related miscarriage risk of invasive diagnostic tests, but positive results still require confirmation before irreversible decisions. Most patients value the opportunity to receive information, yet disability advocates and families of people with Down syndrome have raised concerns that highly routinized screening can create subtle pressure, present disability mainly through deficits, and contribute to stigma or reduced social support. The program can make NIPT a routine universal offer accompanied by balanced condition information, require an explicit opt-in counseling encounter emphasizing that testing is optional and presenting lived-experience information, or limit routine screening more narrowly where the social harms of routinization are judged substantial. The program must choose a policy that preserves meaningful informed choice without treating disability as a life not worth living.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- NIPT is screening, not definitive diagnosis;
- follow-up diagnostic testing is available;
- the program is deciding how testing is offered, not deciding pregnancy outcomes;
- patients value access to information;
- disability-related expressive, stigma and service concerns are documented;
- all represented policies remain voluntary.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Evidence about the population-level social effects of widespread NIPT is less certain than evidence about test performance. The benchmark should represent these concerns as normative/social risks rather than settled causal facts.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- routine universal offer with balanced, nondirective information;
- explicit opt-in after enhanced values and disability-informed counseling;
- narrower routine offer where expansion would undermine meaningful choice or intensify disability-related social harms.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

A US public survey found majority support both for the availability of prenatal genetic screening and for decisions to continue pregnancies after aneuploidy-positive findings, showing that support for screening does not imply a single view about disability or pregnancy continuation. A survey of mothers of children with Down syndrome found that 59% would consider NIPT themselves, while large majorities worried about increased termination, stigma and reduced services. These findings support a real tension between access to information and concern about the social meaning and consequences of routinized screening.

They do not support a crude disabled-community-versus-pregnant-patient binary; the same affected respondents can value testing and still worry about its social context.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

The Nuffield Council on Bioethics treats autonomous informed choice as central while explicitly requiring attention to how NIPT may affect disability, difference and the social environment in which reproductive choices are made. Mainstream prenatal-screening practice similarly emphasizes informed voluntary testing and accurate pretest counseling. The expert layer appears more convergent than the affected-public layer.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Reproductive autonomy:** information expands meaningful reproductive choice → routine access should not depend on clinicians' views of acceptable reasons for testing → offer NIPT broadly with accurate nondirective counseling.
- **Disability justice / expressivist concern:** institutional practices communicate social values as well as information → a routinized deficit-focused offer can stigmatize existing people with the screened condition → require disability-informed counseling and avoid default assumptions that testing or termination is expected.
- **Relational autonomy:** choice is shaped by framing, clinical routines and social support → formal voluntariness alone is insufficient → design an explicit opt-in process that surfaces values and alternatives.
