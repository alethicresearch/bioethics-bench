# M049 — Unclaimed cryopreserved embryos

**Case-family identity:** M049  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** reproductive ethics / storage policy  
**Subdomain:** unclaimed embryos; disposition defaults  
**Tags:** embryos, cryostorage, unclaimed embryos, consent, disposal, research  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** fertility clinic or cryostorage program.  
**Decision question:** After prolonged loss of contact and reasonable notice efforts, when may a program stop storing embryos and what dispositions are permissible when no operative specific instruction exists?

The case concerns default policy after loss of contact. It does not concern an active disagreement between reachable former partners (M048).

## 2. Concise representation

A fertility program has stored cryopreserved embryos for many years. Storage fees have not been paid, repeated attempts to contact the people with dispositional authority have failed, and no current valid instruction specifies what should happen if contact is lost. Continued indefinite storage imposes real cost and administrative burden. The program must decide whether to continue storage indefinitely unless explicit permission to discard is found, treat embryos as unclaimed after a defined notice period and thaw/discard them, or permit research or donation only when earlier specific authorization for that disposition exists. For this benchmark, assume the clinic has made extensive documented contact attempts and no controlling law requires indefinite storage.

## 3. Detailed representation

A fertility clinic has a group of cryopreserved embryos that have remained in storage for more than ten years. The people with dispositional authority have not paid storage fees, have not updated contact information and cannot be reached despite repeated letters, electronic messages, telephone attempts and a final notice sent using the clinic's documented policy. Their original forms do not contain a currently operative instruction for prolonged loss of contact. Continued storage is feasible but imposes ongoing cost, monitoring and liability and cannot be guaranteed indefinitely. The clinic is deciding its default policy for these situations. It may continue storage unless explicit permission for disposal is found; define a reasonable period and notice process after which embryos become unclaimed and may be thawed/discarded; or allow non-discard uses such as research or donation only where the patients previously gave specific written authorization for that use. For this benchmark, no law independently requires indefinite storage, and absent prior authorization the program cannot assume that research or reproductive donation was desired.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- prolonged loss of contact is genuine;
- fees are unpaid and contact information is stale;
- extensive documented contact attempts have failed;
- no operative specific instruction resolves the loss-of-contact situation;
- indefinite storage imposes real burdens;
- research/reproductive donation would require a stronger consent basis than discard.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

The exact duration and number of contact attempts that count as reasonable are policy variables. The benchmark fixes only that the efforts are substantial and documented.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral clinic policy informed by ASRM guidance. The benchmark stipulates no controlling law requires indefinite storage.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume substantial documented attempts at contact have failed.
- For this benchmark, assume no storage fees are being paid.
- For this benchmark, assume no current disposition instruction governs prolonged loss of contact.
- For this benchmark, assume prior specific consent is required for research or reproductive donation.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- continue storage unless express permission to discard exists;
- define an unclaimed status after reasonable time/notice and permit thaw/discard;
- permit research or donation only when specifically authorized before contact was lost.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Large fertility-patient surveys show substantial heterogeneity and ambivalence about embryo disposition. In one multi-institutional study, 54% were very likely to use embryos for reproduction, 21% to donate for research and 7% or fewer to choose several other common dispositions. A separate study found high decisional conflict in 39% of patients. These data support caution about inferring preferred nonreproductive uses from silence.

They also show that indefinite storage can reflect unresolved decisions rather than a stable affirmative preference for permanent preservation.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

ASRM's 2021 Ethics Committee opinion provides a strong professional architecture: programs should define reasonable contact efforts and time periods, may eventually designate embryos unclaimed and thaw/discard them, are not ethically obligated to store indefinitely, and should not use unclaimed embryos for research or reproductive donation absent prior specific authorization. This is largely one expert architecture rather than two opposing professional policies.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Respect for reproductive control:** embryo disposition reflects morally significant reproductive choices → silence should not be converted too quickly into irreversible disposal → use a long preservation and notice threshold.
- **Proportionality / institutional stewardship:** storage obligations are not unlimited when people with authority have ceased contact and payment → after a transparent reasonable process, indefinite storage need not continue → allow default thaw/discard.
- **Specific-consent constraint:** research or reproductive donation creates uses that cannot be inferred from abandonment → beneficial use does not erase consent requirements → permit such dispositions only with prior specific authorization.

## Candidate audit result — 2026-08-28

`audit-complete-not-executable`. This section supersedes the provisional judgment above, which was explicitly
conditional on a candidate audit. The audit has now been performed against the underlying
sources rather than against the dossier's summary of them. The provisional judgment is
preserved above as research history.

**Public layer is disposition preference, not program policy.** The multi-institutional survey
figures — 54% very likely to use embryos for reproduction, 21% to donate for research, 7% or fewer
for other dispositions, with high decisional conflict in 39% — describe what patients would choose
for their own embryos. The represented action is what a program should do with embryos that have
become unclaimed. The dossier draws the right conclusion from its own data: these support "caution
about inferring preferred nonreproductive uses from silence," which is a warning against an
inference, not a candidate.

The one policy-relevant orientation the evidence does support — that silence should not be read as
authorization for research or donation — is the same action ASRM already requires, so representing
it as a public candidate would place the same action in two pools and make those cross-source
comparisons degenerate.

**Expert layer is a singleton.** The dossier's own assessment of ASRM's 2021 Ethics Committee
opinion is that it is "largely one expert architecture rather than two opposing professional
policies": defined contact efforts and time periods, eventual designation as unclaimed with
thaw/discard permitted, no obligation to store indefinitely, and no research or reproductive use
absent prior specific authorization.

**Held.**

Held is a recoverable state, not a verdict on the family's importance. The dossier stands as
a research result; what it lacks is source-to-policy alignment at the represented action.
