# M045 — Posthumous use of stored gametes or embryos

**Case-family identity:** M045  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** reproductive ethics  
**Subdomain:** posthumous reproduction; stored gametes/embryos  
**Tags:** posthumous reproduction, consent, gametes, embryos, surviving partner  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** fertility program.  
**Decision question:** When may a fertility program use stored reproductive material after one genetic contributor has died?

The decision concerns reproductive use, not emergency retrieval of gametes from a newly deceased person and not inheritance law.

## 2. Concise representation

A fertility program stores reproductive material created or banked during a couple's treatment. One genetic contributor dies before the material is used. The surviving partner asks to proceed with reproduction. The clinic's records contain strong evidence that the deceased wanted children with the surviving partner but the consent form does not contain an explicit signed posthumous-use authorization. There is no evidence the deceased opposed posthumous reproduction. The program must decide whether reproductive use should require explicit written authorization, may proceed for a surviving spouse or partner when strong evidence of the deceased's wishes exists, or should be prohibited whenever contemporaneous consent cannot be obtained. For this benchmark, assume all three policies are legally available.

## 3. Detailed representation

A fertility program is storing cryopreserved reproductive material originally banked for family building by a couple. One genetic contributor dies unexpectedly before the material is used. The surviving partner wants to use the stored material to attempt pregnancy. The deceased repeatedly expressed a plan to have children with this partner and participated in fertility treatment, but the clinic's older consent form did not separately ask for signed authorization for posthumous reproductive use. There is no contrary statement and no dispute from another person with a stronger reproductive claim. The clinic distinguishes the decision to preserve or retrieve reproductive tissue from the later decision to use it for reproduction. It must adopt a policy for reproductive use: explicit written authorization only; allow a surviving spouse/partner when strong reliable evidence shows posthumous use accords with the deceased's wishes; or prohibit use whenever the deceased cannot give contemporaneous consent. For this benchmark, each policy is legally available, counseling is available and the surviving partner has had time to grieve before deciding.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- material was stored for reproductive purposes;
- deceased and surviving partner had a shared family-building plan;
- no explicit written posthumous-use authorization exists;
- no evidence indicates opposition by the deceased;
- request comes from surviving partner, not parents or other relatives;
- reproductive use, not retrieval, is the decision.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

The central uncertainty is epistemic: how much evidence short of explicit written authorization is sufficient to infer the deceased's reproductive wishes.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral ethical policy with legality stipulated. Real law varies substantially and should not determine the benchmark answer.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume each represented policy is legally available.
- For this benchmark, assume there is strong evidence of a shared plan for children but no explicit signed posthumous-use instruction.
- For this benchmark, assume there is no evidence the deceased opposed posthumous reproduction.
- For this benchmark, assume adequate grieving time and counseling have occurred.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- explicit written authorization is required;
- surviving spouse/partner may proceed on strong reliable evidence of the deceased's wishes;
- contemporaneous consent is indispensable, so use after death is prohibited absent specific prior authorization.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Studies summarized in ASRM guidance show substantial but non-universal support for posthumous reproduction, with especially strong importance attached to prior consent. Fertility and sperm-banking patients often express willingness to allow partners to use stored material after death, but preferences are not universal. This supports more than one plausible affected-public policy translation around the evidentiary threshold for authorization.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

ASRM states that posthumous use is ethically justified when written authorization is available. In the absence of written documentation, programs that are willing to consider requests should restrict consideration to surviving spouses/partners and should not proceed where evidence suggests the deceased would have objected. This is best treated as one conditional expert architecture rather than split into artificial separate recommendations.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Precedent autonomy / reproductive liberty:** the deceased had an important interest in controlling whether reproduction occurs after death → explicit authorization provides the strongest evidence → require written consent before reproductive use.
- **Relational autonomy / shared reproductive project:** reproductive plans can be jointly constituted within a partnership → strong evidence of a shared family-building project can make strict formality unnecessarily defeat the deceased's likely wishes → allow surviving-partner use under a high evidentiary threshold.
- **Nonconsensual parenthood constraint:** creating genetic parenthood is ethically weighty and irreversible → uncertainty should be resolved against reproduction rather than inferred from silence → prohibit absent explicit authorization.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- conflating retrieval with reproductive use;
- treating silence as consent;
- treating a parent's/grandparent's request as equivalent to a surviving partner's;
- making legality the ethical answer;
- forgetting that a surviving partner may later choose not to reproduce even when permission exists.
