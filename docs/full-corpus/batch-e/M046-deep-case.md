# M046 — Institutional abortion-service policy

**Case-family identity:** M046  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** reproductive ethics / institutional policy  
**Subdomain:** abortion-service scope  
**Tags:** abortion, institutional policy, access, referral, reproductive health  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** general hospital or health-system leadership.  
**Decision question:** Within a jurisdiction where several service models are legally available, what abortion services should a hospital provide directly and what access obligations remain for services it does not provide?

The case concerns institutional service scope. Individual clinician conscientious objection is M047; abstract legality or political permissibility of abortion is excluded.

## 2. Concise representation

A general hospital provides obstetric, gynecologic and emergency care in a region where abortion is lawful under the circumstances represented here. The hospital can staff abortion care directly but leadership is considering a narrower service policy because some clinicians and community stakeholders object. Other local providers exist, though referral can add travel and delay. The hospital must choose whether to provide abortion broadly within ordinary clinical/legal limits, provide only a narrower set of indications while guaranteeing timely referral or transfer for other lawful care, or ordinarily decline abortion provision while maintaining emergency treatment and an access pathway elsewhere. For this benchmark, assume all three institutional policies are lawful.

## 3. Detailed representation

A regional general hospital offers comprehensive obstetric and gynecologic care and has clinicians capable of providing medication and procedural abortion. Abortion is lawful for the patients and gestational ranges relevant to this benchmark. Hospital leaders are considering whether the institution should integrate abortion into ordinary reproductive care, restrict direct provision to a narrower set of gestational ages or clinical indications while guaranteeing a timely external pathway, or generally decline direct abortion services except urgent health-preserving care while directing patients elsewhere. Some staff and community members have moral objections; other patients and clinicians argue that exclusion from a comprehensive hospital creates stigma, delay and unequal access. Alternative providers are available but may require additional travel and several days of delay. The hospital must choose its standing service policy, not adjudicate the morality of an individual abortion.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- abortion is lawful within the represented choice set;
- hospital has technical capacity to provide abortion;
- alternative providers exist but impose additional travel/delay;
- emergency health-preserving care remains available under every policy;
- the object is institutional service scope, not individual conscience.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

The magnitude of access burden from referral varies by region. The benchmark fixes a meaningful but not catastrophic additional burden rather than assuming either effortless transfer or complete absence of alternatives.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral institutional ethics with legality explicitly stipulated. Evidence is dated because abortion law and policy change rapidly.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume all three institutional policies are lawful.
- For this benchmark, assume the hospital can safely provide abortion directly.
- For this benchmark, assume external access is available but entails meaningful travel and delay.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- integrate broad abortion access into ordinary hospital reproductive care;
- provide a narrower in-system service with guaranteed timely referral/transfer;
- generally decline direct provision while maintaining emergency care and a reliable external pathway.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

There is extensive public opinion research on abortion legality and morality, but much less direct evidence comparing these hospital service architectures. Patient-access research documents harms from delay, travel, stigma and fragmented referral, but translating those findings into two or more institutional public-policy candidates requires caution. Partisan polling should not be used as a substitute.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

ACOG's 2025 policy treats abortion as an essential component of comprehensive evidence-based health care and supports integration into mainstream medical care and removal of barriers. WHO's abortion-care guideline likewise emphasizes evidence-based, high-quality, accessible abortion services. These sources substantially converge toward an access-oriented architecture rather than providing multiple equivalent professional policies.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Reproductive autonomy / equal access:** hospitals that already provide reproductive care have strong reasons not to exclude a time-sensitive lawful service → integrate abortion within ordinary care.
- **Institutional pluralism with nonabandonment:** institutions can possess mission-based moral commitments, but those commitments should not strand patients → permit narrower direct provision only with an effective timely access pathway.
- **Conscience / moral complicity:** an institution may regard direct participation in abortion as inconsistent with its moral identity → decline routine provision while preserving urgent care, accurate information and nonobstructive access elsewhere.

- importing partisan labels;

- making the referral pathway unrealistically effortless or impossible;
- creating an expert anti-access candidate unsupported by professional guidance merely for symmetry.
