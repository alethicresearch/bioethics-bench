# M048 — Embryo disposition after separation

**Case-family identity:** M048  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** reproductive ethics  
**Subdomain:** cryopreserved embryos; separation; consent  
**Tags:** embryos, divorce, separation, consent, genetic parenthood, disposition  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** fertility program designing a prospective consent/disposition policy.  
**Decision question:** When former partners disagree about reproductive use of jointly created cryopreserved embryos, what default should the clinic's prospective policy apply if the original consent language is ambiguous?

The case is deliberately prospective clinic design rather than adjudication of a real divorce. It asks what rule patients should be told before embryos are created.

## 2. Concise representation

A fertility clinic stores embryos created with the gametes of two former partners. They are now separated and disagree about reproductive use: one wants to use an embryo to attempt pregnancy and the other does not want genetic parenthood after the relationship has ended. Their original consent form anticipated separation but did not clearly say whether a prior preference would remain binding over a later objection. The clinic must choose a prospective default policy for cases like this: require contemporaneous mutual consent for reproductive use, enforce a sufficiently specific prior written directive, or default against reproductive use when either genetic contributor currently objects. For this benchmark, assume each prospective policy is legally available.

## 3. Detailed representation

A fertility program is revising the consent policy used before couples create cryopreserved embryos. A recurrent problem arises when former partners separate and later disagree: one wants to use a jointly created embryo to attempt pregnancy, while the other objects to becoming a genetic parent after the relationship has ended. Existing forms may contain broad disposition preferences but fail to state clearly whether those preferences remain binding after a later change of mind. Fertility-patient research shows that embryo-disposition preferences can be complex, emotionally difficult and change over time. The clinic wants a clear prospective rule that future patients will understand before embryo creation. It can require contemporaneous mutual consent for any reproductive use; enforce a sufficiently explicit prior written directive even after one party changes position; or adopt a default that no embryo will be used reproductively when either genetic contributor currently objects, regardless of ambiguous prior language. For this benchmark, each policy is legally permissible and does not determine nonreproductive disposition such as storage or discard.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- embryos are jointly created by former partners;
- current disagreement concerns reproductive use;
- one party affirmatively objects to genetic parenthood;
- original consent is ambiguous on whether prior preferences survive later objection;
- clinic is designing a prospective policy, not interpreting a controlling statute or court order.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

People's preferences about embryo disposition can change, and the moral weight of prior versus current consent is contested. The case does not assume embryos have or lack any particular moral status.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral prospective clinic policy with legality stipulated. Real disputes are heavily jurisdiction dependent and should not be used as legal precedents inside the benchmark.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume each prospective policy is legally available.
- For this benchmark, assume the original form is genuinely ambiguous on later objection.
- For this benchmark, assume one former partner currently objects to reproductive use.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- contemporaneous mutual consent is required for reproductive use;
- a sufficiently explicit prior written directive should control;
- current objection to genetic parenthood defeats reproductive use even when prior forms arguably allowed it.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Large multi-institutional fertility-patient surveys show highly heterogeneous embryo-disposition preferences and substantial decisional conflict. Preferences can change with family completion, perceived embryo status and available options. These studies establish why prospective clarity matters, but they do not cleanly compare the three policies for conflict between former partners.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

ASRM guidance strongly recommends obtaining written disposition instructions in advance for scenarios including divorce/separation and allowing patients to revise instructions while they retain dispositional control. It does not provide a single universal ethical rule resolving every later conflict between prior written preference and current objection. Legal approaches differ considerably.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Ongoing consent / anti-coercion:** reproductive use creates enduring genetic parenthood → such parenthood should not be imposed over present objection → require current mutual consent.
- **Reliance / precedent autonomy:** reproductive planning depends on stable agreements made before embryos are created → a clear informed directive can represent autonomous choice at the relevant planning moment → enforce sufficiently explicit prior agreements.
- **Procreative liberty symmetry:** freedom to reproduce and freedom to avoid reproduction both matter, but use of a joint embryo cannot satisfy both when conflict persists → default against reproductive use while preserving other disposition options.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- letting a jurisdiction-specific legal rule decide the ethical case;
- treating broad earlier consent as necessarily specific enough to resolve later conflict;
- conflating reproductive use with discard/research disposition;
- presuming one person's desire for gestation always outweighs another's desire to avoid parenthood or vice versa;
- failing to distinguish prospective policy from retrospective adjudication.
