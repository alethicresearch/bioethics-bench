# M044 — Uterus transplantation

**Case-family identity:** M044  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** reproductive ethics / transplantation  
**Subdomain:** uterus transplantation; absolute uterine factor infertility  
**Tags:** uterus transplantation, infertility, transplantation, surgical risk, reproductive autonomy  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** specialized transplant/reproductive-medicine program.  
**Decision question:** Should a specialized program offer uterus transplantation to medically eligible patients with absolute uterine factor infertility as clinical care, restrict it to tightly governed prospective programs, or decline routine provision because of its burdens and risks?

The case asks whether and under what governance structure a program should provide UTx. It does not ask whether gestational parenthood is superior to adoption or surrogacy.

## 2. Concise representation

A specialized center can perform uterus transplantation for carefully selected patients with absolute uterine factor infertility. The procedure can enable gestation and live birth, but it requires major transplantation surgery, temporary immunosuppression and later graft removal; living donation can also expose a healthy donor to major surgery. The center has trained staff and outcome monitoring. A medically eligible patient understands that adoption and, where lawful, gestational surrogacy avoid transplantation risks but strongly values the possibility of carrying a pregnancy. The center must decide whether to offer UTx as selected clinical care, limit access to a prospective registry/research-style program, or decline routine provision because the intervention is non-life-saving and risk intensive.

## 3. Detailed representation

A tertiary transplant and reproductive-medicine center has the technical expertise to perform uterus transplantation for patients with absolute uterine factor infertility. Published programs have now produced live births, but UTx remains resource intensive and exposes the recipient to transplantation surgery, immunosuppression, pregnancy under transplant management and a later operation to remove the graft. Living-donor programs additionally expose a healthy donor to substantial surgery; deceased-donor grafts avoid that donor risk but introduce other limitations. A medically eligible patient has received detailed counseling and strongly prefers the possibility of gestating a pregnancy, while understanding that adoption and, where legally available, gestational surrogacy do not carry transplantation risks. The program must choose whether UTx should be available as carefully selected clinical treatment, only within a tightly governed prospective registry or research-like pathway, or not routinely offered because the reproductive benefit does not justify the aggregate donor, recipient, fetal and resource burdens.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- patient has absolute uterine factor infertility;
- program has genuine UTx expertise rather than offering an experimental procedure without infrastructure;
- live birth is possible, but success is not guaranteed;
- recipient faces major surgery, immunosuppression and later graft removal;
- living donation may impose risk on a healthy donor;
- the patient values gestational parenthood after informed counseling.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Long-term recipient, donor and offspring outcomes continue to mature as programs accumulate experience. The case should remain evidence-dated rather than implying a settled risk profile.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral specialized-program policy, explicitly evidence-dated to 2026. Any future executable version would need dated review because UTx is a moving technology.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume the center has the surgical and reproductive expertise to perform UTx responsibly.
- For this benchmark, assume the patient is medically eligible and has completed informed counseling.
- For this benchmark, assume adoption and gestational surrogacy are discussed as alternatives but are not treated as equivalent experiences.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- offer selected clinical access with rigorous informed consent and donor safeguards;
- restrict UTx to prospective registry/research-style governance while evidence matures;
- do not routinely provide a non-life-saving transplant with substantial recipient/donor burdens.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Women with absolute uterine factor infertility often express very strong interest in UTx. In one 2024 study, 71.5% of women with Rokitansky syndrome preferred UTx ahead of adoption or surrogacy. A UK interview study of informed self-referred patients found 97.5% would choose UTx ahead of safer alternatives. Public attitudes are less uniform: US polling has been broadly supportive, while a Japanese survey found only 36.5% supported approval. These findings establish real value pluralism but do not map cleanly onto institutional governance policies.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

Professional opinion remains mixed and has changed with accumulating clinical experience. A large US multidisciplinary survey found 45% considered UTx ethically justified and only 35.9% would introduce it to patients. Other professional surveys have been considerably more favorable conditional on adequate medical, surgical and ethical safeguards. This is genuine expert pluralism, but survey attitude is weaker than formal professional guidance.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Reproductive autonomy / capability:** gestating a pregnancy can be a deeply valued reproductive capability → medically eligible adults may reasonably choose meaningful risk for that capability → permit selected access with robust consent.
- **Nonmaleficence / proportionality:** transplantation exposes a healthy or otherwise stable recipient and potentially a living donor to substantial burdens for a non-life-saving benefit → evidence and safeguards must clear a high threshold → restrict to tightly governed programs while uncertainty remains.
- **Opportunity-cost justice:** UTx consumes scarce multidisciplinary transplant resources → reproductive benefit must be weighed against other uses and access inequities → routine expansion should wait for stronger outcome and resource evidence.
