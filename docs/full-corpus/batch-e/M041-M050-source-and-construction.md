# Bioethics Bench — Batch E source and construction packet (M041–M050)

**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27  
**Scope:** reproduction and family formation. This packet identifies the bounded policy object, strongest current source classes, and main construction risk before executable transcription. It does not itself authorize machine-readable records.

## M041 — Allocation of publicly funded IVF

**Decision object:** how a public health system should prioritize a fixed number of funded IVF cycles among people who already meet minimum clinical eligibility.

**Affected/public evidence:**
- Van Muylder et al. (2025), representative Belgian discrete-choice experiment, n=3,000: public priorities were influenced by maternal age, infertility cause and prior biological children; partner age, civil status and treatment cost were not important.
- Ontario fertility-patient survey, n=271: 90.3% supported public IVF funding; 62.7% preferred maximizing access rather than maximizing live births; 84.5% wanted a consistent allocation policy across clinics; respondents favored a scoring approach but differed on which factors should count.
- Six-country European survey, n=6,110: 93% supported at least some public IVF funding; respondents supported a finite number of funded cycles and expressed differential views about primary versus secondary infertility and age.

**Professional/policy evidence:**
- NICE NG257 (2026): initial 3 full cycles under 40 for people meeting clinical criteria; one cycle age 40–41 under specified conditions; prior self-funded treatment does not itself bar public access, though prior cycles count toward the total.
- NICE QS73 states that existing living children should not preclude fertility treatment and that age is used because it is a robust predictor of IVF success.
- Ontario clinic survey documents real implementation variation: first-come/first-served, lottery and multifactor priority approaches.

**Likely serious policies:** prognosis/effectiveness priority; equal-access/lottery after minimum criteria; multifactor priority including time sensitivity and distributive considerations.

**Construction risk:** do not let a discriminatory social-worth criterion enter merely because a public survey measured it; distinguish empirical preference from normatively acceptable allocation.

## M042 — Prenatal screening and disability justice

**Decision object:** what information/counseling architecture a prenatal screening program should use when routinely offering NIPT for conditions such as trisomy 21.

**Affected/public evidence:**
- US public survey on disability and NIPT: majority supported availability of screening and also supported decisions to continue pregnancies after aneuploidy-positive results; qualitative responses expressed disability-related concerns.
- Survey of mothers of children with Down syndrome: 59% would consider future NIPT, while 88% worried it could increase pregnancy terminations, 57% anticipated increased stigma and 64% worried about reduced services.

**Professional/normative-policy evidence:**
- Nuffield Council on Bioethics, *Non-invasive prenatal testing: ethical issues* (2017): emphasizes informed autonomous choice while explicitly addressing disability, difference and social consequences of routinized prenatal testing.
- Mainstream prenatal-screening ethics supports non-directive informed choice and accurate information about tested conditions; disability-justice literature adds the requirement that information not reduce life with disability to medical deficits alone.

**Likely serious policies:** routine universal offer with robust balanced condition/disability information; opt-in offer after explicit values-oriented counseling; narrower testing scope where routinization or nonmedical trait expansion would generate disproportionate social harms.

**Construction risk:** do not collapse screening into abortion policy; do not treat disability-community concern as opposition to all prenatal information.

## M043 — Multifetal pregnancy reduction

**Decision object:** how a fertility/MFM service should counsel and respond when a patient with a higher-order multifetal pregnancy is considering reduction.

**Affected evidence:**
- IVF patient survey: 77.4% would consider multifetal pregnancy reduction; 22.6% said they would never consider it; triplets were the common threshold at which reduction would be considered.

**Professional evidence:**
- ACOG Committee Opinion 719: higher-order multifetal pregnancy increases maternal and perinatal risk; all such patients should receive nondirective counseling covering continuation and reduction; clinicians should respect the patient's decision; conscience conflict requires timely nonjudgmental referral.

**Likely serious policies:** patient-choice/nondirective access; clinician recommendation for reduction above a specified risk threshold while leaving final choice with patient; restrictive institutional limits on non-medically motivated reductions.

**Construction risk:** professional evidence strongly converges on informed patient choice, so do not manufacture multiple expert candidates from the fact that individual clinicians may hold moral objections.

## M044 — Uterus transplantation

**Decision object:** whether a transplant/reproductive program should offer uterus transplantation to medically eligible patients with absolute uterine factor infertility outside an early research-only setting.

**Affected/public evidence:**
- 2024 survey: 71.5% of women with Rokitansky syndrome would choose UTx ahead of adoption or surrogacy.
- UK AUFI survey: 97.5% of 40 informed self-referred women preferred UTx over adoption/surrogacy despite understanding higher personal risk.
- US public survey found majority support for UTx as an ethical option; Japanese public survey found lower support (36.5%), showing sociocultural variation.

**Professional evidence:**
- Provider surveys remain divided. One large multidisciplinary US survey found 45% regarded UTx as ethically justified and 35.9% would introduce it to an AUFI patient. Earlier UK professional survey was much more favorable conditional on medical/surgical/ethical acceptability.
- Contemporary outcome literature now includes successful live births but recipient, donor and pregnancy risks remain material.

**Likely serious policies:** offer to carefully selected informed patients; restrict to specialized prospective registries/research programs; decline routine provision because the intervention imposes transplantation risks for a non-life-saving reproductive benefit.

**Construction risk:** rapidly evolving outcomes and program maturity; avoid treating adoption or surrogacy as ethically interchangeable substitutes or as reasons that gestational parenthood has trivial value.

## M045 — Posthumous use of stored reproductive material

**Decision object:** when a fertility program may use stored gametes or embryos after one genetic contributor has died.

**Affected/public evidence:**
- ASRM review notes general-population and fertility-patient studies showing substantial but not universal support for posthumous reproduction and particularly strong importance placed on prior consent.

**Professional evidence:**
- ASRM Ethics Committee: posthumous use is ethically justified with written authorization. Programs open to requests without written authorization should consider them only when initiated by a surviving spouse/partner and should not proceed where evidence indicates the deceased would have opposed use. Counseling and grieving time are important.
- ASRM 2026 fertility-preservation opinion reiterates that prior instructions to permit or prohibit use should be respected and that law varies.

**Likely serious policies:** written-consent-only; allow surviving spouse/partner under strong evidence of the deceased's wishes when no written directive exists; categorical non-use absent explicit authorization.

**Construction risk:** distinguish retrieval from reproductive use; do not treat surviving relatives other than a spouse/partner as equivalent claimants.

## M046 — Institutional abortion-service policy

**Decision object:** within a stipulated jurisdiction where multiple policies are lawful, what scope of abortion service a general hospital should provide.

**Professional/policy evidence:**
- ACOG 2025 policy regards abortion as an essential component of comprehensive evidence-based health care and supports integration into mainstream care and removal of access barriers.
- WHO 2022 abortion-care guideline recommends health-system and policy arrangements aimed at quality abortion care and evidence-based access.

**Public evidence:** broad public abortion attitudes are abundant but usually address legality or personal morality rather than the internal service architecture of a hospital. That is a source-granularity problem, not a reason to infer an institutional policy from partisan polling.

**Likely serious policies:** broad in-system provision within clinical/legal limits; narrower direct provision with guaranteed timely referral/transfer for services not offered; referral-centered non-provision except urgent medically necessary care.

**Construction risk:** high political salience; do not use partisan labels; avoid duplicating M047 and M007; likely non-executable until direct affected-service-user evidence supports distinct institutional policies.

## M047 — Conscientious objection in abortion care

**Decision object:** what disclosure, referral and emergency duties a hospital should impose on a clinician who objects to abortion.

**Professional evidence:**
- ACOG Committee Opinion 385: accurate unbiased information, advance disclosure of objections, timely referral, preservation of access, and emergency provision where referral is impossible or harmful.
- National OB/GYN vignette study showed professional disagreement: 43% considered conscientious refusal appropriate overall; approval was much higher with referral and very low when objection plus refusal to refer were combined.
- Primary-care survey: 69% believed physicians have an obligation to provide abortion referral despite personal objection.

**Affected evidence:** access literature shows refusal and poor referral can create delays, stigma and care barriers, but direct patient comparisons of alternative institutional conscience policies are limited.

**Likely serious policies:** objection with effective referral and advance disclosure; objection without direct referral but institution-managed transfer/access; require provision where refusal would materially impede timely access.

**Construction risk:** overlap with M007. Keep this family only if abortion-specific time sensitivity, stigma and institutional reproductive-access obligations add a distinct decision object.

## M048 — Embryo disposition after separation

**Decision object:** what prospective clinic policy should control reproductive use of jointly created cryopreserved embryos after former partners disagree and the original forms are ambiguous.

**Affected evidence:**
- Multi-institutional US fertility-patient studies show highly heterogeneous embryo-disposition preferences and high decisional conflict; disposition preferences often change over time.

**Professional/policy evidence:**
- ASRM unclaimed-embryo guidance strongly recommends written advance directives covering divorce/separation and permits parties to revise instructions while they retain dispositional control. The literature and law vary substantially on whether prior agreements or contemporaneous objection should control in disputes.

**Likely serious policies:** require contemporaneous mutual consent for reproductive use; enforce a sufficiently specific prior directive; default against imposing genetic parenthood when current consent is absent.

**Construction risk:** legal doctrine varies and can decide individual disputes. Frame as prospective clinic-consent design rather than adjudication of a named divorce case.

## M049 — Unclaimed cryopreserved embryos

**Decision object:** after prolonged loss of contact and reasonable notice efforts, what disposition policy a storage program may apply to embryos for which no operative specific disposition instruction exists.

**Affected evidence:**
- Large fertility-patient survey: 54% were very likely to use embryos for reproduction, 21% to donate for research and 7% or fewer for other common disposition options; many patients found existing options unacceptable.
- High decisional conflict is common in embryo disposition.

**Professional evidence:**
- ASRM 2021: programs should define reasonable time/contact efforts; unclaimed embryos may ultimately be thawed/discarded; absent prior specific consent they may not be donated for research or reproductive use; programs are not obligated to store indefinitely.

**Likely serious policies:** long/indefinite preservation absent express permission to discard; disposal after a defined notice/contact process; research/donation only when specific prior authorization exists.

**Construction risk:** a research-use candidate without prior consent would be a straw position against current ethics guidance; the real disagreement concerns storage duration, notice and default disposal.

## M050 — Donor anonymity and adult access to donor identity

**Decision object:** what identity-information policy a prospective gamete/embryo donation program should promise before donation occurs.

**Affected evidence:**
- Belgian donor-conceived adults: 82.8% wanted non-identifying information, 69% were interested in identifying information and 61.6% wanted donor contact.
- Donor-conceived adult research documents harms associated with late or accidental discovery and substantial interest in genetic origins.
- Donor evidence is genuinely different: in a Belgian anonymous-donor cohort only about 20% said they would continue donating if identity release became mandatory; other open-identity donor cohorts are substantially more accepting of future contact.
- ART-patient survey in Brazil found many recipients preferred continued donor anonymity, although views changed depending on whether the child actively sought origin information.

**Professional/policy evidence:**
- ASRM encourages disclosure of donor conception, permanent retention of medical/genetic information, and advance clarity about information release; it recognizes increasing open-identity practice but does not impose universal identity release in the US.
- UK HFEA policy provides a concrete open-identity architecture: most post-April-2005 donors are identifiable to donor-conceived adults at 18, while donors have no obligation to establish contact.

**Likely serious policies:** open identity to the donor-conceived adult at maturity; non-identifying information plus mutual-consent identity/contact; preserve promised anonymity while warning that DNA technology makes practical anonymity unreliable.

**Construction risk:** donor-conceived persons, donors and recipient parents are all affected communities with different interests. Do not label one stakeholder's preference as “the public” without provenance.
