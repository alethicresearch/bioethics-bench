# M050 — Donor anonymity and adult access to donor identity

**Case-family identity:** M050  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** reproductive ethics / donor conception  
**Subdomain:** donor identity; information rights  
**Tags:** donor conception, anonymity, identity release, gamete donation, genetic origins  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** prospective gamete/embryo donation program.  
**Decision question:** What donor-identity policy should a donation program promise to donors, recipient parents and donor-conceived people before donation occurs?

The case concerns prospective program design. It is not about retroactively breaking a historical anonymity promise without notice.

## 2. Concise representation

A fertility program is designing its policy for future gamete donations. Donor-conceived adults increasingly seek information about genetic origins, while some prospective donors and recipient parents value anonymity or controlled contact. Home DNA databases also make practical anonymity increasingly difficult to guarantee. The program must choose whether donors will be identifiable to donor-conceived adults at age 18, whether only non-identifying information will be released with identifying contact requiring mutual consent, or whether the program will promise anonymity while warning that genetic matching may defeat it in practice. Medical and genetic information will be retained under all policies.

## 3. Detailed representation

A fertility program is redesigning the consent terms for future sperm, oocyte and embryo donation. Donor-conceived adults often report a strong interest in medical, genealogical and identifying information, and consumer DNA databases can reveal donor identities even where clinics promise anonymity. At the same time, some donors say they would not have donated under mandatory identity release, and some recipient parents prefer an anonymous model. The program wants a rule that is transparent before anyone donates or receives treatment. It can adopt an open-identity policy in which a donor-conceived adult may obtain identifying information at age 18 but the donor has no obligation to communicate; a non-identifying-information policy with identifying contact only when both adult offspring and donor consent; or an anonymity-preserving policy that withholds identifying information through the program while explicitly warning all parties that external DNA matching makes practical anonymity impossible to guarantee. Under every policy, the program permanently retains relevant medical/genetic history and clearly explains the chosen disclosure rules before consent.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- policy applies prospectively, before donation;
- adult donor-conceived persons have interests in genetic-origin information;
- donors and recipients may have privacy/anonymity interests;
- genetic matching undermines any guarantee of practical anonymity;
- identity release does not entail a duty to form a relationship;
- medical/genetic information is preserved under all policies.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

The long-term effects of different identity regimes on donor supply, family relationships and donor-conceived well-being remain context dependent. The benchmark represents these as relevant uncertainties rather than settled harms or benefits.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral prospective design with law stipulated to permit all three models. HFEA policy provides a real-world open-identity comparator, not a legal answer to the benchmark.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume all three prospective information regimes are legally available.
- For this benchmark, assume all parties receive the policy before consent.
- For this benchmark, assume the program retains medical/genetic information permanently.
- For this benchmark, assume external consumer DNA matching may reveal identities regardless of program policy.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- open identity to donor-conceived adults at maturity;
- non-identifying information with mutual-consent identity/contact;
- preserve program-level anonymity while clearly warning it cannot be guaranteed outside the program.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Affected groups show genuine conflict rather than a single preference. In a recent Belgian donor-conceived adult survey, 82.8% wanted non-identifying donor information, 69% were interested in identifying data and 61.6% desired personal contact. Other donor-conceived research links late or accidental discovery with worse experiences and documents active searching for genetic connections.

Donor preferences can differ sharply. In one Belgian cohort of anonymous sperm donors, only about 20% said they would continue donating if identity release became mandatory. In contrast, open-identity and voluntarily registered donor cohorts often report neutral or positive attitudes toward potential contact. A Brazilian ART-patient survey found many recipient patients preferred anonymity. These are different affected stakeholders and should remain separately attributed rather than collapsed into one public voice.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

ASRM encourages disclosure to offspring of donor conception, permanent collection and storage of medical/genetic information, advance clarity about information release and counseling that technological changes make anonymity increasingly difficult to guarantee. ASRM recognizes open-identity programs but does not prescribe universal identity release in the United States. The UK HFEA supplies a mature alternative policy architecture: for most donations after April 2005, donor-conceived adults may request identifying information at 18, while donors are not required to respond or establish contact.

These are genuinely different policy architectures rather than mere wording differences.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Identity and informational interests of offspring:** genetic-origin information can matter to identity, family narrative and health → donor-conceived adults have a strong claim to information about their biological origins → provide identifying information at maturity.
- **Privacy and consent:** donation occurred under a defined informational bargain and donor privacy remains morally relevant → disclosure beyond agreed non-identifying information should require consent from both sides → use mutual-consent identity release.
- **Reliance / promise keeping under technological change:** programs should not promise what they cannot control → formal anonymity may be preserved in clinic records but cannot credibly guarantee real-world anonymity → if an anonymity model is offered, disclose the DNA-matching limitation explicitly.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- collapsing donors, recipients and donor-conceived adults into one affected-public group;
- treating desire for identity information as a demand for a social relationship;
- promising practical anonymity despite DNA databases;
- retroactively applying a prospective rule to donors who were promised anonymity;
- treating donor-supply effects as known with certainty.
