# M125 — HIV confidentiality and partner notification

**Case-family identity:** M125  
**Primary domain:** infectious-disease ethics / confidentiality  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture
**Decision-maker:** clinician/public-health service.  
**Decision question:** When a patient declines to tell an identifiable partner about a serious HIV transmission risk, what notification policy should the service use?

## Scenario pair
**Concise:** An adult with newly diagnosed HIV has an identifiable ongoing sexual partner who may have been exposed. The patient understands the situation but refuses to notify the partner because of fear of relationship consequences. The service can offer assisted anonymous partner notification and safety planning. The decision is whether confidentiality should remain under patient control, whether provider-assisted notification should proceed without identifying the patient, or whether a targeted warning can be made after a defined threshold when supported disclosure fails.

**Detailed:** An adult receives a confirmed HIV diagnosis and reports an ongoing partner who may have had a clinically meaningful exposure. The patient is not virally suppressed yet. They refuse to disclose the diagnosis because they fear abandonment and social harm; no immediate threat of violence has been identified, and the clinic can offer counseling, anonymous/provider-assisted notification and linkage to testing. The service must decide whether to preserve confidentiality unless the patient consents, actively initiate provider-assisted notification that does not name the source, or permit a targeted warning only after supported disclosure fails and legal/public-health criteria for serious preventable harm are met.

## Source architecture
WHO recommends voluntary assisted partner-notification services as an effective way to reach partners while protecting confidentiality and safety. Contemporary HIV practice emphasizes counseling, voluntary disclosure support, violence screening and provider-assisted notification rather than punitive or stigmatizing disclosure. The ethics of direct warning depends heavily on actual transmission risk, law, treatment/viral suppression and feasibility of anonymous notification.

## Serious policy families
- patient-controlled confidentiality with intensive disclosure support;
- provider-assisted anonymous notification;
- targeted warning after defined serious-risk threshold and failed supported disclosure.

## Normative bridges
**Confidentiality/trust:** HIV care depends on protection from stigma and misuse of sensitive information.  
**Harm prevention:** identifiable partners have a strong interest in timely testing and prevention.  
**Least disclosure:** anonymous/provider-assisted notification can often protect both interests better than direct breach.

## Construction risks
Do not use outdated assumptions about HIV transmissibility; state treatment/viral suppression; do not ignore intimate-partner violence risk; do not convert legal permission into an ethical requirement.

## Executable judgment
`needs-additional-evidence`. WHO supports one strong assisted-notification architecture; affected evidence does not yet cleanly support multiple institutional policies under the strict rule.

## Principal sources
WHO, *Assisted HIV partner notification services* policy guidance; contemporary HIV testing and confidentiality guidance; partner-notification ethics literature.
