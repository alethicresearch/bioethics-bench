# M128 — Compulsory feeding in severe anorexia nervosa

**Case-family identity:** M128  
**Primary domain:** mental-health ethics / capacity and life-sustaining treatment  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture
**Decision-maker:** specialist eating-disorder service.  
**Decision question:** When a patient with life-threatening anorexia nervosa refuses nutrition, when, if ever, should clinicians compel feeding?

## Scenario pair
**Concise:** An adult with severe anorexia nervosa has life-threatening malnutrition and refuses nasogastric feeding. The patient understands that death is possible but clinicians are uncertain whether illness-related values and impaired appreciation undermine decision-making capacity about nutrition. Prior treatment has included voluntary care, and the patient has expressed both a wish to live and a refusal of weight restoration. The service must decide whether to respect the refusal, use time-limited compulsory feeding to preserve life and recovery opportunity, or use a short rescue period with repeated capacity/values review before deciding whether coercion should continue.

**Detailed:** An adult with longstanding anorexia nervosa is admitted with severe malnutrition, cardiac risk and worsening biochemical abnormalities. The patient can repeat medical information and recognizes that refusing nutrition may be fatal, but interprets weight gain through intense illness-driven fears and has previously expressed ambivalence between wanting to survive and refusing restoration. Less intrusive oral and supported nutritional approaches have failed. The service can continue voluntary treatment and accept a capacitated refusal, institute compulsory enteral nutrition under applicable law to prevent imminent death, or use a narrowly time-limited rescue intervention while repeatedly reassessing decision-specific capacity, values, trauma burden and less restrictive alternatives.

## Source architecture
Clinical guidance and ethics literature recognize that anorexia can impair decision-making in ways not captured by simple factual understanding, while also warning against assuming incapacity from diagnosis. Compulsory treatment is used in some life-threatening situations, but patient/service-user accounts describe coercion as traumatic and emphasize relational, compassionate and least-restrictive care.

## Serious policy families
- respect a capacitated refusal even when risk is grave;
- compel nutrition when needed to prevent imminent death and preserve recovery opportunity;
- time-limited rescue with repeated capacity/values reassessment and a high threshold for continuation.

## Normative bridges
**Respect for refusal:** grave risk does not by itself erase capacity or bodily integrity.  
**Rescue/beneficence:** severe malnutrition can distort evaluative capacities and death can foreclose recovery.  
**Proportionality:** if coercion is used, duration, force and treatment burden should be minimized and repeatedly justified.

## Construction risks
Do not equate anorexia diagnosis with incapacity; do not describe forced feeding as benign; distinguish emergency stabilization from prolonged compulsory treatment; preserve prior expressed values and trauma history.

## Executable judgment
`needs-additional-evidence`. There is genuine affected experience and clinical disagreement, but current evidence has not yet been translated into two or more direct institutional policy orientations under the strict rule.

## Principal sources
NICE eating-disorder guidance; eating-disorder capacity and compulsory-treatment literature; qualitative studies of patient experiences of involuntary treatment.
