# M130 — Seclusion and restraint in psychiatric crisis

**Case-family identity:** M130  
**Primary domain:** mental-health ethics / emergency coercion  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture
**Decision-maker:** inpatient mental-health service.  
**Decision question:** Should a mental-health service ever use seclusion or restraint when de-escalation fails and immediate risk of serious injury remains?

## Scenario pair
**Concise:** An inpatient psychiatric patient becomes acutely agitated and is attempting to strike others. Verbal de-escalation, environmental modification and offered medication have failed. Staff can withdraw other patients from the area, but immediate injury risk remains. The service must decide whether coercive practices should be prohibited and managed only through noncoercive alternatives, whether brief emergency restraint may be used solely to prevent imminent injury with mandatory review, or whether a broader least-restrictive physical intervention policy should remain available.

**Detailed:** A psychiatric inpatient experiences an acute crisis and repeatedly attempts to strike staff and another patient. Trauma-informed verbal de-escalation, sensory/environmental modification, space, support-person involvement and offered medication have not reduced the immediate risk. Staff can clear most of the environment but cannot fully eliminate danger to the patient and others. WHO/OHCHR guidance calls for elimination of seclusion and restraint and development of noncoercive services; many existing clinical systems nevertheless retain emergency powers. The service must decide what ethical policy should govern this narrow residual-risk situation and what review, duration and debriefing requirements apply.

## Source architecture
WHO/OHCHR 2023 guidance calls for ending coercive practices including seclusion and restraint, emphasizing rights-based, person-centered alternatives. Existing clinical regimes sometimes permit emergency physical intervention to prevent immediate injury, usually under least-restrictive, time-limited and review conditions. Service-user evidence documents trauma, humiliation and loss of trust from coercive practices.

## Serious policy families
- prohibit seclusion/restraint and invest in noncoercive crisis alternatives;
- permit emergency-only restraint for imminent serious injury with continuous monitoring and mandatory review/debrief;
- permit a broader least-restrictive physical-intervention hierarchy where immediate safety cannot otherwise be maintained.

## Normative bridges
**Dignity/noncoercion:** restraint can itself cause serious physical and psychological harm.  
**Immediate protection:** staff and patients have claims against preventable imminent injury.  
**Accountability:** any emergency exception must be narrow, monitored, documented and reviewed to prevent normalization.

## Construction risks
Do not use convenience or staffing shortages as indications; do not treat seclusion/restraint as treatment; do not omit prior de-escalation; distinguish emergency physical interruption from prolonged coercive management.

## Executable judgment
`research-complete-not-executable`. The professional rights-based guidance is highly convergent and direct affected-policy evidence does not support multiple institutional rules without editorial construction.

## Principal sources
WHO/OHCHR, *Mental health, human rights and legislation* (2023); WHO QualityRights materials; service-user research on seclusion/restraint experiences.
