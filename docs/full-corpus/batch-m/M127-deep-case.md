# M127 — Involuntary psychiatric treatment

**Case-family identity:** M127  
**Primary domain:** mental-health ethics / coercion  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture
**Decision-maker:** mental-health service and legal/clinical review system.  
**Decision question:** When a person experiencing severe mental illness refuses hospitalization or medication, what threshold, if any, should permit coercive intervention?

## Scenario pair
**Concise:** An adult experiencing severe psychosis refuses admission and medication. The person can communicate a choice, but clinicians disagree about whether the person can appreciate the consequences and whether the current risk of serious harm is high enough to justify involuntary intervention. Community support and supported decision-making are available. The service must decide whether free and informed consent should remain controlling, whether a narrowly defined imminent-serious-harm threshold permits temporary coercion, or whether temporary intervention may instead depend on decision-specific incapacity plus necessity and repeated supported reassessment.

**Detailed:** An adult with severe psychotic symptoms refuses hospitalization and antipsychotic medication. The patient can state reasons and preferences but has impaired appreciation of some consequences; there is concerning but not certain risk of serious deterioration and injury. A trusted supporter, intensive community treatment and repeated capacity assessment are available. The legal system currently allows coercion under specified conditions, while WHO/OHCHR guidance advocates moving mental-health systems toward free and informed consent and eliminating coercive practices. The service must decide which ethical threshold should govern temporary intervention rather than treating diagnosis itself as incapacity.

## Source architecture
WHO/OHCHR 2023 guidance calls for ending involuntary detention/treatment, seclusion and restraint and making free and informed consent foundational. Existing jurisdictions retain harm-, capacity- and necessity-based powers, but those are legal/clinical alternatives rather than competing WHO recommendations. Service-user literature documents trauma, loss of trust and mixed retrospective views of coercion.

## Serious policy families
- free/informed consent with intensive voluntary and supported-decision alternatives;
- temporary intervention only for imminent serious harm;
- temporary capacity/necessity-based intervention with repeated supported reassessment.

## Normative bridges
**Autonomy/equality:** psychiatric diagnosis should not uniquely diminish ordinary consent rights.  
**Harm prevention:** temporary intervention may preserve life or recovery opportunity when risk is acute.  
**Supported agency:** capacity and will/preferences can fluctuate and should be strengthened rather than bypassed.

## Construction risks
Do not assume psychosis equals incapacity; do not treat existing legal permission as professional ethical endorsement; do not erase coercion harms; distinguish hospitalization from medication.

## Executable judgment
`research-complete-not-executable`. The normative dispute is strong, but contemporary professional human-rights guidance is convergent and the alternative thresholds are not supported as distinct expert policies by the same source class.

## Principal sources
WHO/OHCHR, *Mental health, human rights and legislation: guidance and practice* (2023); WHO mental-health human-rights materials; service-user/coercion literature.
