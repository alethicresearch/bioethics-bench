# M134 — Location monitoring for a person with dementia

**Case-family identity:** M134  
**Primary domain:** dementia ethics / surveillance and safety  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture
**Decision-maker:** dementia-care service and surrogate/support network.  
**Decision question:** Should caregivers or services use GPS/location monitoring to reduce wandering risk when the person with dementia resists surveillance?

## Scenario pair
**Concise:** A person with moderate dementia has twice become lost for several hours while walking independently. The person values unsupervised walks and objects to wearing a GPS device, saying it feels like being watched. A surrogate and care team believe tracking could preserve independent mobility while reducing danger. They must decide whether to use continuous monitoring despite resistance, use only triggered/minimal monitoring with repeated assent, or respect the refusal and rely on environmental and human-support alternatives.

**Detailed:** A person with moderate dementia continues to take daily neighborhood walks. Twice in the past month the person became disoriented and required assistance to return home. The person can express a stable dislike of location tracking but has difficulty appreciating the full risk of future wandering. Family and clinicians can use a GPS wearable with geofencing, arrange accompanied walks, modify routines and increase community support. Continuous tracking could preserve mobility but creates surveillance and dignity concerns. The care plan must determine whether surrogate-authorized continuous tracking is proportionate, whether monitoring should activate only during identified risk periods with repeated assent and minimal data retention, or whether the person's refusal should control while less intrusive supports are intensified.

## Source architecture
Dementia tracking literature identifies a recurring safety-versus-autonomy conflict. Stakeholder work emphasizes that acceptability depends on proportionality, transparency, consent/assent, data governance and whether tracking expands freedom rather than merely controls behavior. A 2024 qualitative study of device developers similarly stressed stakeholder inclusion and ethical design. The evidence does not support treating GPS as automatically justified by diagnosis.

## Serious policy families
- surrogate-authorized monitoring where wandering risk is substantial;
- minimal/triggered monitoring with assent, data minimization and periodic review;
- respect refusal and use environmental/human alternatives unless an emergency threshold is reached.

## Normative bridges
**Relational autonomy:** technology can support independence if it is used to expand rather than replace agency.  
**Safety:** repeated wandering can create serious preventable harm.  
**Privacy/dignity:** continuous location data can transform care into surveillance.

## Construction risks
Do not assume dementia equals incapacity; state actual wandering risk; distinguish monitoring from confinement; do not omit less intrusive alternatives.

## Executable judgment
`needs-additional-evidence`. Stakeholder evidence is rich but tends to support one proportional, individualized architecture rather than clean competing institutional policies.

## Principal sources
