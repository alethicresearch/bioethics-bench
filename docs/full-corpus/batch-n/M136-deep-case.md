# M136 — Deep-brain stimulation and personality change

**Case-family identity:** M136  
**Primary domain:** neuroethics / identity and treatment adjustment  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture
**Decision-maker:** DBS patient and clinical team.  
**Decision question:** When effective DBS substantially changes mood, preferences or personally valued characteristics, whose values should govern continuation or device adjustment?

## Scenario pair
**Concise:** A patient with Parkinson disease has major motor improvement after DBS but family members report that the patient is more impulsive and socially different. The patient has decision-making capacity, values the increased energy and independence, and wants the settings unchanged. Before surgery the patient had said preserving certain personality traits mattered greatly. The team must decide whether current competent preferences control, whether pre-treatment values should constrain adjustment, or whether an iterative shared process should reconcile current preferences, prior goals and relational effects.

**Detailed:** A patient receives DBS for advanced Parkinson disease and obtains substantial motor benefit. Months later, the patient reports feeling more energetic, socially engaged and independent, while a spouse describes increased impulsivity and a loss of familiar traits. Formal assessment finds the patient currently capable of understanding the benefits, risks and alternatives. Preoperative counseling documented that the patient feared losing several personally meaningful characteristics, but recent patient-centered studies suggest DBS often improves rather than degrades valued characteristics and severe unwanted personality-change narratives may be overstated. The team must decide whether present capable preferences are decisive, whether pre-treatment values/advance goals should constrain programming, or whether programming should proceed through repeated shared decision-making that recognizes both identity continuity and relational consequences.

## Source architecture
Recent patient-centered empirical work (JAMA Network Open 2024) found patients' individually valued personal characteristics generally improved after DBS. A 2023 adaptive DBS participant study similarly found mostly positive psychosocial changes and few transient unwanted effects. The ethics literature nevertheless identifies authenticity, identity, autonomy and family conflict as real concerns. This evidence cautions against treating family reports of “personality change” as automatically overriding the patient's own evaluation.

## Serious policy families
- current competent patient preference controls programming;
- pre-treatment values/advance goals receive special weight when treatment appears to alter the evaluative standpoint itself;
- iterative shared decision incorporating current preferences, prior values and relational consequences.

## Normative bridges
**Current autonomy:** a capable patient is the primary authority over present treatment.  
**Diachronic autonomy:** prior values matter when an intervention may transform the perspective from which present preferences arise.  
**Relational identity:** close others can provide evidence about changes without gaining veto authority.

## Construction risks
Do not assume DBS routinely causes personality damage; distinguish family discomfort from patient harm; preserve current capacity; do not frame identity as fixed or purely individual.

## Executable judgment
`needs-additional-evidence`. The empirical and normative case is strong, but direct patient/professional evidence does not yet support multiple institutional programming rules under the strict candidate standard.

## Principal sources
Merner et al., JAMA Network Open 2024; adaptive DBS participant psychosocial study, 2023; DBS neuroethics literature on identity, authenticity and autonomy.
