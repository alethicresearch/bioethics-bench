# M137 — Neuroenhancement in healthy adults

**Case-family identity:** M137  
**Primary domain:** neuroethics / enhancement  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture
**Decision-maker:** regulator/clinical provider.  
**Decision question:** Should clinicians or regulated providers offer neurotechnology primarily for cognitive enhancement rather than treatment?

## Scenario pair
**Concise:** A noninvasive neurostimulation device has, for this benchmark, a well-characterized safety profile and produces a modest improvement in sustained attention in healthy adults. The effect is temporary and does not treat disease. A regulated clinic wants to offer it to adults seeking performance enhancement. The regulator must decide whether to permit voluntary enhancement, restrict clinical neurotechnology to treatment/restoration, or permit enhancement only with safeguards addressing coercion, fairness and access.

**Detailed:** A future noninvasive neurostimulation technology has undergone extensive testing and, for this benchmark, produces a reproducible modest improvement in sustained attention in healthy adults with no known serious adverse effects. It is not required to treat illness and the benefit is temporary. Universities and employers cannot require its use under the benchmark, but competitive pressure may still emerge if access becomes common. Public-attitude research on neuroenhancement is context-sensitive rather than uniformly permissive or prohibitive. UNESCO's 2025 neurotechnology Recommendation is cautious about non-therapeutic use and emphasizes dignity, autonomy, mental integrity, explicit consent and protection against coercive or exploitative uses. The regulator must choose the permissible scope of enhancement services.

## Source architecture
Public research demonstrates real variation in willingness to allow neuroenhancement depending on benefit, risk, fairness and regulator. UNESCO's current normative framework strongly emphasizes human rights and cautions against non-therapeutic neurotechnology, especially for vulnerable groups. It does not provide a mature clinical permission architecture for safe adult enhancement.

## Serious policy families
- permit informed adult enhancement under ordinary safety regulation;
- clinical use only for treatment/restoration;
- conditional adult access with anti-coercion, equity and advertising safeguards.

## Normative bridges
**Cognitive liberty:** competent adults may shape their own mental capacities when risks are acceptable.  
**Fairness/coercion:** apparently voluntary enhancement can become socially compulsory in competitive settings.  
**Professional role:** medicine may have a defensible boundary around treatment rather than optimization.

## Construction risks
Do not smuggle safety objections back into a benchmark that stipulates safety; do not assume enhancement equals cheating; do not infer professional permission from public tolerance.

## Executable judgment
`research-complete-not-executable`. Public pluralism is real, but a safe-future adult enhancement policy would require substantial extrapolation from current professional guidance.

## Principal sources
UNESCO 2025 Recommendation on the Ethics of Neurotechnology; recent public-attitude research on neuroenhancement; neuroethics literature on cognitive liberty, fairness and professional boundaries.
