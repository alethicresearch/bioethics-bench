# M132 — Preventing suicide against a capacitated refusal

**Case-family identity:** M132  
**Primary domain:** emergency psychiatry / refusal  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture
**Decision-maker:** emergency medical/psychiatric team.  
**Decision question:** When an adult appears to understand and appreciate the consequences of refusing intervention after a suicide attempt, when may clinicians continue involuntary protection?

## Scenario pair
**Concise:** An adult is medically stabilized after an intentional overdose and asks to leave. The patient can explain the diagnosis, options, and possibility of death if another attempt occurs, and gives coherent reasons for refusing admission. Clinicians still believe short-term suicide risk is high. The team must decide whether a capacitated refusal should be honored, whether temporary protection is justified despite apparent capacity, or whether a brief observation/support period should precede a final capacity and refusal decision.

**Detailed:** An adult is treated successfully after an intentional overdose. Hours later the patient denies current intent, explains the medical and psychiatric risks, reasons consistently about options, and requests discharge. There is recent major psychosocial loss, continuing hopelessness and a high estimated short-term risk of another attempt, but no psychosis or intoxication. Clinicians can offer crisis planning, family/support involvement and voluntary admission. The service must decide whether decision-specific capacity alone should control discharge, whether suicide prevention can justify temporary involuntary protection even when capacity appears intact, or whether a short, predefined observation/support period is justified before a final refusal is honored.

## Source architecture
Self-harm guidance emphasizes psychosocial assessment, individualized risk formulation, collaborative safety planning and avoidance of simplistic risk scores. Ethical literature rejects the assumption that suicidality automatically equals incapacity but also recognizes the irreversibility of death and the difficulty of assessing preferences during acute crisis. Many legal systems authorize short emergency holds, but legal existence is not itself ethical endorsement.

## Serious policy families
- honor a decision-specific capacitated refusal;
- temporary protection despite apparent capacity when near-term lethal risk is exceptionally high;
- brief observation/support followed by renewed capacity and preference assessment.

## Normative bridges
**Autonomy:** competent adults normally retain authority even over high-risk choices.  
**Preservation of future agency:** temporary protection may keep open the possibility of later autonomous choice after an acute crisis passes.  
**Proportionality:** if protection is used, it should be brief, reviewable and paired with supportive care.

## Construction risks
Do not assume all suicidal patients lack capacity; do not treat risk score as a moral threshold; distinguish short emergency protection from indefinite involuntary treatment.

## Executable judgment
`research-complete-not-executable`. The most defensible professional approach is temporally staged and individualized rather than several clean competing source-grounded policies.

## Principal sources
NICE self-harm guidance; emergency psychiatry and capacity literature; suicide-prevention ethics literature.
