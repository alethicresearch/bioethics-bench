# M131 — Psychiatric advance directives during a later crisis

**Case-family identity:** M131  
**Primary domain:** mental-health ethics / advance planning  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture
**Decision-maker:** psychiatric service.  
**Decision question:** Should a prior psychiatric advance directive refusing a treatment govern during a later crisis when clinicians believe that treatment would rapidly restore functioning?

## Scenario pair
**Concise:** A patient completed a psychiatric advance directive while well, refusing a specific medication because of prior severe adverse effects and naming a preferred alternative. During a later psychotic crisis the patient lacks decision-making capacity about treatment. Clinicians believe the refused medication would work faster than the preferred alternative. The service must decide whether the prior directive controls, whether a narrow emergency override is permitted, or whether the appointed proxy and supported interpretation of the patient's prior values should govern.

**Detailed:** An adult with recurrent severe mental illness completed a detailed advance directive during a sustained period of capacity. It refuses one medication associated with prior intolerable effects, names an alternative, and designates a trusted proxy. During a later crisis, the patient lacks decision-specific capacity and cannot engage consistently in treatment decisions. The treating team believes the refused drug would likely restore functioning more rapidly, while the preferred drug remains clinically reasonable but may act more slowly. The service must decide how much authority the prior directive, current expressed wishes and proxy interpretation should have, and whether any emergency override should be limited to a defined serious-harm threshold.

## Source architecture
Psychiatric advance directives are designed to preserve autonomy and improve crisis planning, but implementation is uneven. Evidence suggests facilitated advance planning can improve completion and may reduce coercive crisis interventions. Legal systems vary on override authority; this variation is not itself a professional recommendation.

## Serious policy families
- prior competent directive controls absent a conventional emergency exception;
- narrow temporary override where serious imminent harm cannot otherwise be prevented;
- proxy/supported-decision process interpreting both prior directive and present will/preferences.

## Normative bridges
**Precedent autonomy:** choices made while capable can protect the person against predictable crisis-state loss of agency.  
**Current welfare:** rigid adherence may expose the patient to avoidable grave harm when circumstances differ.  
**Relational interpretation:** a proxy can help interpret rather than replace the person's enduring values.

## Construction risks
Do not turn an advance directive into a blanket refusal of all care; distinguish an ordinary directive from a deliberately self-binding “Ulysses” arrangement; do not assume current incapacity erases current preferences.

## Executable judgment
`needs-additional-evidence`. Strong policy object, but direct service-user evidence comparing override rules is not yet sufficiently policy-specific for executable construction.
