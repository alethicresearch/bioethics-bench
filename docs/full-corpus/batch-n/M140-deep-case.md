# M140 — Digital proximity/contact tracing

**Case-family identity:** M140  
**Primary domain:** infectious-disease governance / digital surveillance  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture
**Decision-maker:** public-health authority during a serious outbreak.  
**Decision question:** What privacy and participation rules should govern a digital proximity/contact-tracing system that is expected to improve outbreak control?

## Scenario pair
**Concise:** During a serious outbreak, a privacy-preserving Bluetooth system can identify recent close contacts faster than manual tracing and is expected to reduce transmission if uptake is high. The authority must decide whether participation should remain voluntary with strong privacy protections, whether use can be required in narrowly defined high-risk settings, or whether digital tracing should not be used beyond ordinary manual public-health methods.

**Detailed:** A novel respiratory outbreak is spreading rapidly. A decentralized Bluetooth contact-tracing tool has been independently tested and is expected to shorten notification delays relative to manual tracing. It does not collect GPS location and automatically deletes proximity data after a short period. Uptake matters to effectiveness. WHO's ethical guidance for digital proximity tracking emphasizes voluntariness, data minimization, proportionality, transparency, time limitation, privacy protection and prohibition of commercial/law-enforcement uses. Public acceptance studies show privacy, trust and perceived benefit strongly affect willingness. The authority must choose the participation/governance rule rather than assume maximum uptake automatically justifies compulsion.

## Source architecture
WHO specifically states digital proximity tracking should be voluntary and informed, privacy-preserving, time-limited and limited to public-health purposes. Later WHO contact-tracing guidance maintains strong privacy/data-protection requirements. Systematic reviews find public acceptance is heavily dependent on privacy, security, trust and perceived utility.

## Serious policy families
- voluntary privacy-preserving digital tracing;
- mandatory use in narrowly defined high-risk institutional contexts;
- no digital tracing beyond manual methods.

## Normative bridges
**Public-health benefit:** faster notification can reduce transmission.  
**Autonomy/privacy:** digital surveillance can outlast emergencies and chill behavior.  
**Trust/instrumental effectiveness:** coercive or opaque systems may reduce uptake and legitimacy.

## Construction risks
Do not use GPS when the benchmark says Bluetooth; do not assume mandatory adoption guarantees effectiveness; do not retain data indefinitely; distinguish general population from high-risk institutional settings.

## Executable judgment
`needs-additional-evidence`. The professional layer strongly converges on voluntary privacy-preserving use; public evidence is conditional but does not yet directly support two institution-level participation rules under the strict standard.

## Principal sources
WHO, *Ethical considerations to guide the use of digital proximity tracking technologies* (2020); WHO contact-tracing guidance; 2024–2025 systematic reviews of digital contact-tracing privacy and acceptance.
