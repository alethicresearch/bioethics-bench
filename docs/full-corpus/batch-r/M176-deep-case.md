# M176 — Patient requests an unnecessary diagnostic test

**Domain:** primary care / low-value care  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27  
**Research status:** deep case draft

## Decision
**Decision-maker:** primary-care clinician.  
**Question:** When a patient strongly requests a diagnostic test with very low expected clinical value but meaningful false-positive and downstream-harm risk, should the clinician order it?

## Scenario
A patient with uncomplicated mechanical low-back pain, no neurologic deficits and no red-flag symptoms asks for MRI because a friend had cancer discovered after imaging. Evidence suggests immediate MRI is very unlikely to improve outcome and carries a meaningful chance of incidental findings leading to additional tests, anxiety and cost. The patient says reassurance itself has value and is willing to pay their cost share. The clinician must choose whether to decline and explain, offer delayed/shared-decision imaging if symptoms persist or uncertainty changes, or order now because reassurance value may outweigh the modest downstream risks for this patient.

## Evidence layers
Low-value-care guidance and Choosing Wisely-type recommendations generally discourage routine imaging without red flags. Patient-request research shows requests can reflect reassurance, fear and expectations, but does not establish a professional duty to order low-value testing.

## Candidate families
1. decline low-value imaging and explain;
2. time-limited watchful waiting/shared decision with clear trigger for imaging;
3. order after informed discussion when reassurance value is substantial and harms remain within reasonable practice.

## Risks
Do not make the test literally useless; preserve a small chance of relevant information and a real reassurance value. Distinguish patient willingness to pay from absence of societal/downstream costs.

## Executable judgment
`needs-additional-evidence`. The professional direction is strong; direct patient policy evidence does not support two action-distinct clinician rules.
