# M027 — Advance directive versus current contentment in dementia

**Case-family identity:** M027  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** dementia ethics / advance care planning  
**Subdomain:** precedent autonomy; present interests; surrogate decision-making  
**Tags:** dementia, advance directive, precedent autonomy, current wellbeing, life-sustaining treatment  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** treating clinical team with legally authorized surrogate.  
**Decision question:** When a prior competent advance directive refuses a specified life-prolonging treatment, should that directive control if a person with advanced dementia now appears comfortable and engaged but cannot understand or revise the directive?

The case is intentionally narrower than “do advance directives matter in dementia?” It fixes one treatment decision and a conflict between prior autonomous instructions and current experiential interests.

## 2. Concise representation

An older adult with advanced dementia lacks capacity for complex medical decisions but is usually calm, recognizes familiar people emotionally, enjoys music and meals, and shows no persistent distress. Years earlier, while fully capable, the patient completed a clear advance directive stating that if severe dementia prevented recognition and independent decision-making, they would refuse tube feeding intended to prolong life. The patient now develops severe dysphagia after an acute illness and cannot maintain nutrition safely by mouth. The surrogate believes the patient seems content and asks for a feeding tube. The clinical team must decide what policy should govern the conflict between the prior directive and current wellbeing.

## 3. Detailed representation

An older adult has advanced dementia and no longer possesses capacity to understand or decide about complex medical treatment. The person is dependent for daily care but is usually calm, responds warmly to familiar family members, enjoys music and hand-feeding, and shows no persistent behavioral evidence of suffering. Ten years earlier, while fully decisionally capable and after caring for a relative with dementia, the patient completed a specific advance directive stating that if severe dementia left them unable to recognize family or make independent decisions, they would refuse medically administered nutrition intended primarily to prolong life. The current patient cannot understand the directive or meaningfully affirm or revoke it. After an acute illness, swallowing worsens and careful hand-feeding no longer provides enough nutrition; a feeding tube could prolong life but would not reverse the dementia and may require restraints or repeated procedures if removed. The surrogate believes the patient's current contentment shows that the earlier self misjudged life with dementia and requests tube feeding. The team must decide what policy should govern the conflict between the directive and the patient's present experiential wellbeing.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- clear prior directive made with capacity;
- directive specifically anticipates severe dementia and the treatment class at issue;
- current patient lacks capacity to revise it;
- current patient appears comfortable/engaged rather than persistently distressed;
- tube feeding may prolong life but will not reverse dementia;
- burdens of tube feeding are real but not assumed catastrophic;
- surrogate believes current wellbeing changes the moral picture;
- hand-feeding remains part of comfort care even if tube feeding is forgone.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

The patient's present subjective experience cannot be fully known, and current pleasant affect does not establish a global judgment about quality of life. Conversely, the prior directive may have been based on predictions about dementia that the current patient cannot now evaluate. The case preserves both uncertainties.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral ethical policy. The benchmark assumes the advance directive is legally valid and potentially applicable, but that clinicians have genuine interpretive discretion about how to integrate present interests rather than a statute mechanically resolving the case.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- **Directive stipulation:** prior directive was informed, competent and specifically addressed severe dementia and life-prolonging tube feeding.
- **Capacity stipulation:** current patient cannot understand or revise the directive.
- **Wellbeing stipulation:** current patient usually appears comfortable and positively engaged.
- **Treatment stipulation:** feeding tube can prolong life but cannot reverse dementia and carries moderate burdens.
- **Care stipulation:** comfort-focused hand feeding remains available.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- follow the prior directive as the clearest expression of the person's autonomous values;
- give current experiential wellbeing priority when following the directive would end a life the present person appears to value;
- use a hybrid rule that gives strong presumptive authority to the directive but permits departure when current interests are compelling and treatment burden is modest;
- require independent ethics review before overriding either a clear directive or a surrogate's current-welfare claim.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Empirical studies show that treatment preferences differ substantially across health states and by treatment burden. People often choose less life-sustaining treatment for hypothetical severe dementia than for their current health state (PMID 15859720). Work with people with early dementia and family carers demonstrates that patients can express preferences, while family agreement is imperfect (PMID 27410259). A nationally representative surrogate experiment found that dementia status changes recommendations and that surrogates may depart from patient preferences in both directions (PMID 38358920).

These data establish instability, projection and surrogate mismatch but do not directly support two policies about what to do when a specific prior directive conflicts with present contentment.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

Advance-care-planning practice generally gives significant weight to valid prior directives and substituted judgment, while dementia care emphasizes present comfort, personhood and individualized goals. There is no single professional rule that resolves the philosophical conflict represented here. Clinical guidance is usually process-oriented: interpret the directive carefully, clarify its applicability, assess current burdens/benefits and involve the surrogate and ethics support when conflict persists.

Because the key disagreement is fundamentally normative, the Bench should not relabel philosophical positions as distinct professional recommendations.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Precedent autonomy (Dworkin-style):** competent people shape their lives through enduring values and critical interests → a later loss of capacity should not erase those values → follow a specific prior directive even when current experiential interests appear positive.
- **Present interests / minimal autonomy (Jaworska-style family):** the current person remains a subject of experiences and attachments → current wellbeing has independent moral weight → do not end life-prolonging support solely because a former self anticipated a life the current person now appears to enjoy.
- **Integrative / diachronic personhood:** both prior values and current welfare belong to the same life → neither should automatically dominate → use a strong presumption for the directive tempered by current welfare, treatment burdens and specificity.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- treating current smiling/contentment as proof of a fully good life;
- treating a prior directive as context-free text without interpretation;
- assuming tube feeding has either no burden or extreme burden;
- conflating dementia diagnosis with absence of all agency or experience;
- making the surrogate selfish or irrational;
- changing directive specificity between representations.
