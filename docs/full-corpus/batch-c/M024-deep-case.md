# M024 — Adolescent request for gender-affirming medical treatment under contested evidence

**Case-family identity:** M024  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** adolescent medicine / gender-related care  
**Subdomain:** consent; developmental capacity; evidence uncertainty; family involvement  
**Tags:** adolescent, gender incongruence, puberty suppression, hormones, consent, shared decision-making  
**Research status:** deep case draft; moving-policy case  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** multidisciplinary adolescent specialty service.  
**Decision question:** What clinical policy should govern access to a puberty-related gender-affirming medical intervention for an adolescent when the young person requests treatment, parental views are mixed, and important benefits and long-term risks remain uncertain?

The case is deliberately about a service's threshold and decision process, not the moral or political status of transgender identity. It excludes surgery and focuses on a reversible or partly reversible medical intervention whose evidentiary and policy status is actively contested.

## 2. Concise representation

A 15-year-old with persistent gender incongruence and distress requests a puberty-related medical intervention after more than a year of specialist assessment and psychosocial support. For this benchmark, assume the adolescent demonstrates substantial understanding of the treatment's intended effects, uncertainties, fertility implications and alternatives; one parent supports treatment and the other remains concerned about long-term outcomes but is willing to continue deliberation. There is no acute suicidality or untreated condition that independently prevents informed decision-making. The service must decide what policy should govern access when the adolescent requests treatment but the evidence base and professional policies remain contested.

## 3. Detailed representation

A 15-year-old has experienced persistent gender incongruence and clinically significant distress through puberty and has been followed for more than a year by a multidisciplinary service. The adolescent requests a puberty-related medical intervention after psychosocial support and repeated discussion of nonmedical options. For this benchmark, assume the adolescent can explain the intended effects, known adverse effects, important evidence uncertainties, possible fertility consequences and the possibility that future preferences may change. Relevant mental-health conditions have been assessed and are being treated where needed; there is no acute crisis or cognitive impairment that independently defeats decision-making capacity. One parent supports the request. The other parent is worried about long-term physical and psychological outcomes and would prefer further observation but remains engaged rather than categorically refusing care. Current international professional policies differ materially in how they translate the evidence into access rules. The service must decide what policy should govern treatment access in this context.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- adolescent is 15 and has persistent, clinically significant gender-related distress;
- specialist multidisciplinary assessment has occurred over more than a year;
- adolescent demonstrates substantial treatment-specific understanding;
- no independent acute condition defeats capacity assessment;
- parents differ but both remain engaged;
- fertility and uncertainty have been discussed;
- professional policies and interpretations of the evidence differ across health systems;
- case excludes surgery and emergency psychiatric treatment.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Uncertainty is central rather than a defect to be eliminated. Systematic reviews identify important evidence limitations for puberty-suppressing and gender-affirming hormonal interventions in adolescents, while professional bodies differ in the clinical policies they infer from that evidence. The benchmark should not imply that either benefits or harms are fully known.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

This case cannot responsibly be treated as timeless or jurisdiction-neutral at the executable-policy level. It is dated to 2026 and explicitly records jurisdictional divergence. Any later executable version would need a named system or a carefully stipulated authority structure.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- **Persistence stipulation:** gender incongruence/distress has persisted through specialist follow-up.
- **Capacity stipulation:** adolescent demonstrates substantial understanding, appreciation and reasoning about the specific intervention.
- **Mental-health stipulation:** no untreated acute condition independently defeats treatment-specific decision-making.
- **Family stipulation:** one parent supports treatment; the other prefers delay but remains engaged.
- **Evidence stipulation:** important long-term uncertainty remains and is disclosed.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- permit treatment after multidisciplinary assessment when persistence, treatment-specific capacity and informed consent criteria are met;
- use a more restrictive pathway with extended observation and heightened evidentiary/age thresholds before puberty-related medical treatment;
- limit specific interventions to research or exceptional pathways while continuing comprehensive psychosocial and general healthcare;
- use staged shared decision-making that distinguishes reversible, partly reversible and irreversible interventions and revisits consent over time.

These are genuine contemporary policy families, but they are not interchangeable “expert candidates” because they arise from different jurisdictions, evidence assessments and commissioning frameworks.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Qualitative studies of transgender/gender-diverse adolescents and parents show extended information-seeking, differing timelines of readiness, system barriers and the importance of supportive relationships, open communication and adequate time. Youth often report clear goals for treatment while parents may focus more heavily on long-term uncertainty. Studies of puberty-suppression decision-making also show that adolescents, parents and clinicians can differ in how they understand treatment-specific competence and the purposes of treatment.

This evidence is important for process design but should not be converted mechanically into pro-treatment and anti-treatment public policies. Samples are usually treatment-seeking and context-specific.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

The Endocrine Society currently supports multidisciplinary adolescent assessment and, for appropriately assessed youth, puberty suppression and later gender-affirming hormones with informed-consent and fertility counseling requirements. In contrast, NHS England does not routinely commission puberty-suppressing hormones for children and young people with gender incongruence/dysphoria and in 2026 was separately consulting on revised policy for masculinising/feminising hormones. Systematic reviews published in 2024–2025 report important limitations in certainty for several outcomes.

The correct representation is therefore **professional-policy divergence**, not consensus and not a simple two-candidate pool detached from jurisdiction.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Developing autonomy:** adolescents can possess treatment-specific decisional capacity and have profound interests in bodily development → where understanding and persistence are strong, their informed preferences deserve substantial authority → permit access under safeguarded multidisciplinary consent.
- **Precaution under uncertainty:** developmental trajectories and long-term effects are difficult to predict → when intervention has enduring consequences and evidence remains uncertain, stronger evidentiary and maturity thresholds may be justified → favor delay or restricted access while maintaining support.
- **Relational autonomy / shared decision-making:** adolescent choices develop within family and clinical relationships → support, time and transparent discussion can improve agency without treating parents as absolute veto holders → use staged decision-making and reassessment.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- turning a contested clinical-policy environment into partisan caricature;
- describing identity itself as pathology;
- treating any one country's policy as universal medical consensus;
- overstating benefits or harms beyond evidence certainty;
- conflating access policy, diagnosis, social transition and surgery;
- failing to date the evidence;
- using suicidality as rhetorical leverage rather than a clinical fact;
- ignoring the adolescent's own decision-making process.
