# M021 — Parental request to continue burdensome life-sustaining treatment for a severely ill newborn

**Case-family identity:** M021  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** neonatal ethics / end-of-life  
**Subdomain:** parental authority; life-sustaining treatment; potentially nonbeneficial treatment  
**Tags:** newborn, NICU, life support, parental authority, best interests, fair process  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** NICU/hospital service.  
**Decision question:** When parents request continued invasive life-sustaining treatment for a severely ill newborn whose expected benefit is very small and treatment burdens are substantial, what process should govern whether treatment continues?

The bounded decision is the institutional response to persistent parental request after prognosis and treatment burdens have been explained. It is not a generic question about whether disabled newborns should receive treatment and must not use disability itself as evidence of low benefit.

## 2. Concise representation

A newborn has a catastrophic condition causing permanent severe neurologic injury and remains ventilator-dependent after several weeks of intensive care. For this benchmark, assume continued ventilation and vasoactive support may prolong life but is very unlikely to permit discharge from intensive care, and the infant has recurrent distress despite maximal symptom treatment. The parents understand the prognosis and ask that all life-sustaining treatment continue because they hope for an unexpected recovery and believe stopping treatment would abandon their child. The NICU must decide what process should govern continued treatment or redirection to comfort-focused care.

## 3. Detailed representation

A newborn with a catastrophic acquired condition has permanent severe neurologic injury and remains dependent on mechanical ventilation and vasoactive support after several weeks in the NICU. Multiple senior clinicians agree that additional intensive treatment is very unlikely to permit survival to discharge from intensive care, although short-term physiologic survival remains possible. The infant has repeated episodes of distress associated with invasive treatment despite aggressive pain and symptom management. The parents have participated in repeated family meetings, understand the clinical prognosis and burdens, and continue to request all available life-sustaining treatment. They describe continued treatment as an expression of parental duty, hope and protection and worry that withdrawal would mean causing or abandoning their child's death. For this benchmark, assume there is time for consultation, second opinion and transfer inquiry; the question is what fair institutional process should govern continued treatment or redirection to comfort-focused care.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- newborn cannot decide and parents are lawful surrogates;
- prognosis concerns benefit of intensive treatment, not the worth of life with disability;
- treatment can prolong physiologic life but is very unlikely to achieve ICU discharge;
- treatment causes recurrent burdens/distress despite palliation;
- parents understand the prognosis and continue to request treatment;
- disagreement persists after repeated communication;
- time permits second opinion, ethics review and transfer inquiry.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

The case does not stipulate zero probability of benefit. “Very unlikely” is deliberate: the ethical problem is how institutions should respond when expected benefit is very small but not literally impossible. Prognostic uncertainty remains part of the case and should not be converted into a claim that clinicians are infallible.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral institutional policy. The benchmark assumes the hospital has legal authority to use an established conflict-resolution process and that no jurisdiction-specific statute independently determines the outcome.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- **Benefit stipulation:** continued invasive treatment is very unlikely to permit discharge from intensive care.
- **Burden stipulation:** recurrent treatment-associated distress persists despite maximal symptom treatment.
- **Understanding stipulation:** parents have received clear prognostic communication and understand the stated facts.
- **Process stipulation:** time exists for second opinion, ethics review and transfer inquiry.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- continue requested treatment while any non-negligible possibility of meaningful benefit remains, giving substantial weight to parental authority;
- use time-limited treatment with explicit clinical goals and scheduled reassessment;
- permit redirection to comfort-focused care after independent review, appeal and transfer opportunity if burdens substantially outweigh likely benefit;
- in the most extreme cases, allow clinicians to forgo requested treatment after due process rather than requiring indefinite treatment.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Parents in NICU end-of-life decisions do not express one uniform preference about decisional control. Qualitative and mixed-methods studies show that some parents value active responsibility for the final decision, while others value clinicians taking greater responsibility and protecting them from the burden of feeling they caused the child's death. Recent bereaved-parent data likewise show high value placed on shared decision-making, sensitive communication, time and professional guidance.

This evidence strongly supports flexible, family-sensitive process. It does **not** cleanly support two opposing institutional policies about whether clinicians may ultimately stop treatment over parental objection. The Bench should therefore not convert differences in desired decisional role into fabricated support for “always continue” versus “always withdraw.”

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

AAP guidance on forgoing pediatric life-sustaining treatment centers the child's interests and benefit/burden balance, gives parents wide but not unlimited decisional latitude, and endorses collaborative communication and comfort care. The 2026 AAP clinical and technical reports on parental requests for potentially nonbeneficial treatment recommend a stepwise approach: understand goals and values, gather information, involve relevant disciplines, obtain second opinion, use impartial institutional review, offer transfer where feasible, and permit forgoing of treatment after an established process in rare unresolved cases.

Professional guidance therefore supports process safeguards more clearly than competing expert endpoints.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Parental authority / family integrity:** parents ordinarily know and protect their child's interests and bear the consequences of decisions → substantial uncertainty leaves legitimate room for parental values → continue treatment or use a time-limited trial while benefit remains plausible.
- **Best interests / nonmaleficence:** treatment should benefit the child rather than merely prolong biological function → persistent burden with very low chance of achieving the relevant clinical goal can become contrary to the child's interests → permit redirection to comfort after fair review.
- **Procedural justice:** prognosis and quality-of-life judgments can be biased or disputed → unilateral bedside authority risks error and mistrust → require independent review, appeal and meaningful transfer opportunity before limiting requested treatment.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- treating severe disability as synonymous with low benefit;
- using “futile” as a conclusion rather than stating outcome facts;
- making probability of benefit literally zero and trivializing the dispute;
- stereotyping parental hope or religion as irrational;
- confusing desired decisional role with desired treatment outcome;
- changing the burden or discharge prognosis across companion representations.
