# M063 — Human reproductive cloning

**Case-family identity:** M063  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** reproductive genetics / research governance  
**Subdomain:** reproductive cloning by nuclear transfer  
**Tags:** cloning, nuclear transfer, reproductive cloning, human dignity, governance  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** national biomedical regulator.  
**Decision question:** Should a regulator maintain a categorical prohibition on using nuclear-transfer-derived human embryos for reproduction, or create any pathway for future reproductive cloning?

The case concerns reproductive cloning only. Somatic cell nuclear transfer used to derive research cell lines without reproductive transfer is excluded.

## 2. Concise representation

A future reproductive laboratory claims it can create a human embryo by transferring the nucleus of an adult somatic cell into an enucleated oocyte and proposes transferring such an embryo to establish a pregnancy. For this benchmark, assume major technical risks have been substantially reduced so the decision is not determined solely by current animal-cloning failure rates. The regulator must decide whether reproductive cloning should remain categorically prohibited, whether any future use should require a separate democratic/international authorization beyond ordinary reproductive-medicine review, or whether technically safe reproductive cloning should be treated as a permissible form of reproductive choice.

## 3. Detailed representation

A national regulator is asked to reconsider its longstanding prohibition on human reproductive cloning. A laboratory proposes somatic cell nuclear transfer to create an embryo genetically very similar in nuclear DNA to an existing adult, followed by uterine transfer and pregnancy. For this benchmark, assume extensive future research has substantially reduced the developmental and obstetric safety concerns that make reproductive cloning unacceptable today, so the policy question cannot be resolved merely by pointing to present technical risk. The resulting child would still develop in a different environment, would not be psychologically or physically identical to the nuclear donor, and would be a distinct person. The regulator can maintain a categorical prohibition grounded in dignity, identity, social and intergenerational concerns; allow reconsideration only after a special democratic/international authorization process because reproductive cloning changes a fundamental boundary of human reproduction; or permit technically safe cloning under ordinary assisted-reproduction consent and child-welfare safeguards.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- reproductive use of nuclear-transfer embryos is the object;
- research cloning without uterine transfer is excluded;
- benchmark stipulates present safety objections substantially reduced;
- resulting child is not treated as an identical copy in personality/experience;
- policy concerns reproductive authorization, dignity, welfare and social consequences.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Even after technical risk falls, long-term family, identity, social and symbolic effects would remain uncertain. The moral content of “human dignity” is contested.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Future-facing jurisdiction-neutral ethics. Current international governance strongly prohibits reproductive cloning; that fact is preserved as the source baseline while technical safety is counterfactually stipulated to expose deeper arguments.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume developmental/obstetric safety is no longer by itself prohibitive.
- For this benchmark, assume the cloned child would be recognized as a full independent legal person.
- For this benchmark, assume the regulator has authority to maintain or reconsider the prohibition.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- categorical prohibition regardless of technical safety;
- special democratic/international authorization before any reproductive use can be considered;
- permit technically safe use under ordinary ART safeguards.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Public polling on cloning has historically shown strong opposition, but reproductive cloning is not a contemporary clinical option and direct modern deliberative evidence under a hypothetical safe-use assumption is sparse. Historical opposition is therefore context rather than a sufficient executable public policy pool.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

Current governance is highly convergent. ISSCR lists implantation of human embryos produced by nuclear reprogramming as prohibited research. UNESCO's Universal Declaration states that reproductive cloning of human beings is contrary to human dignity and should not be permitted. No current major professional body supplies a clinical permission pathway.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Human dignity / anti-instrumentalization:** deliberately replicating an existing nuclear genome can express control over a future person's identity and alter fundamental norms of procreation → technical safety does not answer that objection → retain categorical prohibition.
- **Democratic legitimacy / precaution:** even if cloning could benefit individuals, consequences are society-wide and intergenerational → ordinary clinic consent is not enough → require exceptional public/international authorization before any use.
- **Reproductive liberty / non-identity:** a cloned child would still be a distinct individual, and genetic similarity alone need not violate welfare or rights → if technically safe and nonexploitative, reproductive freedom can support permission.

## 12. References and provenance
