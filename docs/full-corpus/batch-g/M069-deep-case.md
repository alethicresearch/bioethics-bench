# M069 — Disclosure of errors involving gametes or embryos

**Case-family identity:** M069

**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** fertility clinic.  
**Decision question:** What disclosure policy should a fertility clinic use after discovering an error involving a patient's gametes or embryos?

The benchmark centers a consequential error affecting reproductive opportunity and separates it from a harmless near miss.

## 2. Concise representation

A fertility clinic discovers that a laboratory error damaged one of a patient's cryopreserved embryos and may reduce the patient's future reproductive options. The clinic knows the error occurred and can explain the known facts, but its full root cause is still being investigated. The patient has not yet been told. The clinic must decide whether to disclose the known error promptly and provide later causal updates, wait until the investigation is complete before giving a single full account, or disclose only errors judged likely to alter clinical outcome while handling clearly harmless near misses internally.

## 3. Detailed representation

A fertility clinic discovers through routine quality review that a laboratory handling error damaged one cryopreserved embryo belonging to a patient with a limited remaining embryo cohort. The event could materially reduce the patient's future reproductive opportunity. The clinic has confirmed what occurred and which embryo was affected, but the exact system cause and whether additional procedural failures contributed are still under investigation. There is no uncertainty that an adverse event occurred. The clinic is deciding its disclosure policy: tell the patient promptly what is known, acknowledge uncertainty and provide later root-cause/corrective-action updates; wait until the full investigation is complete before disclosure to avoid giving incomplete information; or use a materiality rule under which consequential errors are disclosed but clearly harmless near misses may be managed through internal quality improvement without patient notification.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- actual confirmed error occurred;
- it may materially reduce reproductive opportunity;
- immediate facts are known but root cause remains under investigation;
- patient has not yet been informed;
- no gamete/embryo misdirection or wrong-parentage event is involved in this version;
- clinic has a root-cause process.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

The final system cause is unknown at initial disclosure. The downstream reproductive impact may be uncertain even though the error itself is confirmed.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral professional ethics. Evidence-dated 2026 using current ASRM guidance.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume the adverse event itself is confirmed.
- For this benchmark, assume the event could materially affect reproductive opportunity.
- For this benchmark, assume root-cause analysis can continue after initial disclosure.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- prompt disclosure of known facts plus staged updates;
- delay disclosure until investigation is complete;
- materiality-based policy distinguishing consequential errors from harmless near misses.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Direct ART-specific patient evidence comparing disclosure timing policies is limited. General medical-error literature strongly supports patients' desire for disclosure, explanation and prevention of recurrence, but reproductive tissue errors carry distinctive identity and reproductive-opportunity implications. That broader evidence is useful context, not a basis for inventing two ART-specific public candidates.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

ASRM's 2024 Ethics Committee opinion strongly converges on disclosure of clinically significant errors and immediate disclosure of gamete/embryo misdirection. It recommends disclosure even for many apparently minor errors and near misses, while recognizing that disclosure of truly no-harm near misses may not be obligatory. Clinics should conduct root-cause analysis and maintain written policies. This guidance supports prompt known-fact disclosure rather than waiting for complete causal certainty.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Autonomy / truthfulness:** patients need timely knowledge of events affecting reproductive options → disclose confirmed facts promptly while clearly separating what remains unknown.
- **Epistemic responsibility:** premature causal claims can mislead and undermine trust → disclose the event now but stage causal explanation as investigation proceeds.
- **Proportionality / quality improvement:** patient notification should track material impact while every near miss still triggers internal learning → disclose consequential events and use a narrower rule for clearly harmless near misses.
