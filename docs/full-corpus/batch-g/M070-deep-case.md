# M070 — Equitable allocation of scarce curative gene-therapy capacity

**Case-family identity:** M070  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** allocation / gene therapy  
**Subdomain:** scarce gene-therapy slots; sickle cell disease  
**Tags:** gene therapy, sickle cell disease, scarcity, allocation, lottery, urgency, equity  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** specialized gene-therapy program.  
**Decision question:** When an approved potentially curative gene therapy has fewer treatment slots than clinically eligible patients, how should the program prioritize access?

The case focuses on capacity scarcity after clinical eligibility is established. Price coverage and whether the therapy should be publicly funded are separate questions.

## 2. Concise representation

A specialized program can provide an approved one-time gene therapy for sickle cell disease, but manufacturing and transplant-unit capacity allow only a few treatments this year. All patients being compared meet clinical eligibility and understand the therapy's substantial burdens and risks. Some have rapidly progressive organ disease and may soon become ineligible; others are currently healthier and may have better procedural safety or expected long-term benefit. The program must decide whether to prioritize patients at greatest risk of losing future eligibility, prioritize expected clinical benefit/safety, or use transparent clinical tiers followed by lottery among patients who remain comparably prioritized.

## 3. Detailed representation

A regional sickle-cell gene-therapy program has a licensed potentially curative therapy but faces severe manufacturing, apheresis, conditioning and transplant-unit capacity constraints. For the next allocation period, there are fewer treatment slots than clinically eligible patients. All patients under consideration have decision-making capacity, have completed counseling and meet the program's minimum safety/eligibility criteria. Some have progressive organ dysfunction that makes treatment more urgent because delay may render them permanently ineligible. Others have less advanced disease and therefore potentially lower procedural risk and longer expected time to benefit. Some have alternative allogeneic donor options while others do not. The program must adopt a transparent rule for sequencing access: prioritize those at greatest risk of losing future eligibility because of disease progression; prioritize those with the greatest expected net clinical benefit/safety; or establish transparent priority tiers using urgency, alternatives and disease factors and then use a lottery when patients within the highest tier cannot be ethically distinguished.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- therapy is approved and potentially curative;
- all represented patients already meet minimum clinical eligibility;
- scarcity is real and near-term;
- some patients may lose future eligibility through organ progression;
- patients differ in expected benefit/risk and availability of alternatives;
- program can use transparent review and lottery.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Expected durability, individual treatment risk and future capacity are uncertain. Disease severity can increase both need and treatment risk. Structural inequities in SCD care affect who reaches a specialty program and cannot be solved by a within-program queue alone.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Contemporary US-like specialty-program ethics, generalized from current SCD/TDT gene-therapy implementation. The benchmark does not encode a particular insurer or drug price.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume all compared patients satisfy baseline clinical eligibility.
- For this benchmark, assume treatment slots are insufficient for the current eligible cohort.
- For this benchmark, assume program decisions can be publicly stated, reviewed and updated.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- urgency / impending-loss-of-eligibility priority;
- maximize expected clinical benefit and treatment safety;
- multi-factor priority tiers followed by lottery among ethically comparable patients.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

SCD patient-preference evidence directly shows that treatment benefit-risk judgments vary with disease severity. In a discrete-choice experiment involving 174 adults with SCD and 109 parents/caregivers, participants became much more willing to accept gene-therapy risks when asked to consider worsened disease; adults with milder disease required higher efficacy to accept substantial treatment mortality risk. This supports severity/urgency as patient-relevant, but the study did not ask patients how scarce slots should be allocated among different people. Direct affected-community allocation preferences therefore remain a gap.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

A 2025 Blood Advances allocation framework developed through Accountability for Reasonableness for scarce SCD/TDT gene therapy prioritizes disease-prevalence proportionality, patients at risk of losing future eligibility because of organ failure, absence of an allogeneic donor, and lottery. Within the highest priority tier, treatment urgency is discussed and lottery is used when no ethically meaningful distinction remains. This provides one unusually concrete real-world expert allocation architecture.

It does not supply a separate pure benefit-maximization policy; that remains a serious allocation framework but would need distinct professional or health-economic sourcing if used executably.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Prioritarian urgency / rescue:** patients likely to lose the chance for cure if delayed face an irreversible disadvantage → prioritize impending loss of eligibility.
- **Maximizing benefit:** scarce high-burden treatment should produce as much health benefit as possible → prioritize expected net clinical benefit/safety among eligible patients.
- **Fair process / equal moral worth:** when morally relevant clinical criteria cannot distinguish patients, opaque clinician discretion is unjust → use transparent priority tiers and lottery among ties.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- treating cost, clinical eligibility and queue priority as the same question;
- assuming “sickest first” always maximizes benefit;
- ignoring structural racial inequity in SCD access;
- using patient risk tolerance as direct evidence for distributive allocation policy;
- presenting lottery as random from the outset rather than after ethically relevant tiers.
