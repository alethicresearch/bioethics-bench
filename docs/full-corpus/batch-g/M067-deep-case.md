# M067 — Fertility preservation for minors facing gonadotoxic treatment

**Case-family identity:** M067  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** pediatric oncology / reproductive ethics  
**Subdomain:** fertility preservation; minors  
**Tags:** fertility preservation, minors, assent, gonadotoxic therapy, ovarian tissue, testicular tissue  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** pediatric oncology and fertility-preservation team.  
**Decision question:** When should clinicians offer or perform fertility-preservation procedures for a minor before time-sensitive gonadotoxic therapy?

The case separates established low-burden options from experimental or invasive procedures and fixes treatment urgency.

## 2. Concise representation

A 12-year-old child needs gonadotoxic cancer therapy within several days. Standard counseling indicates a high risk of future infertility. The child understands that fertility may matter later but is anxious about additional procedures. A fertility-preservation procedure can be performed without materially delaying cancer treatment; it requires anesthesia and tissue retrieval and offers a plausible but uncertain future reproductive benefit. The parents want preservation performed. The team must decide whether to offer and proceed with parent-authorized preservation while seeking the child's assent, require the child's affirmative assent before an invasive procedure when there is no immediate clinical benefit, or defer an experimental/invasive option when the child objects or when expected reproductive benefit is too uncertain relative to burden.

## 3. Detailed representation

A 12-year-old with newly diagnosed cancer must begin highly gonadotoxic therapy within several days. The oncology team estimates a substantial risk of permanent infertility from treatment. Fertility specialists can perform gonadal-tissue cryopreservation under anesthesia without materially delaying cancer therapy. For this benchmark, assume the procedure's physical risk is low but nonzero and future reproductive benefit is plausible rather than guaranteed. The procedure would not create embryos and the stored tissue would remain under disposition rules revisited when the child becomes an adult. The child can understand the basic purpose and burdens and expresses anxiety and ambivalence rather than a settled desire for future biological parenthood. The parents strongly favor preservation. The team must decide whether parent permission based on preserving the child's future options is sufficient while seeking developmentally appropriate assent; whether an invasive nontherapeutic procedure should require the child's affirmative assent when the child is capable of meaningful participation; or whether experimental/invasive preservation should be deferred when the child objects or expected benefit is too uncertain relative to burden.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- child is 12 and capable of meaningful assent discussion;
- cancer treatment is time-sensitive but preservation does not materially delay it;
- gonadotoxic infertility risk is high;
- procedure has low but real burden and uncertain future benefit;
- no embryos are created;
- parents want preservation;
- future disposition will be revisited at adulthood.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

The child may never want genetically related children, the stored tissue may never be usable, and future reproductive technology may change. Current preferences at age 12 may not predict adult values.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral pediatric ethics. Evidence-dated 2026 using current ASRM guidance.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume the procedure does not materially delay cancer treatment.
- For this benchmark, assume physical risk is low but nonzero and reproductive benefit is plausible but uncertain.
- For this benchmark, assume the child can meaningfully assent or object.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- parent-authorized preservation with developmentally appropriate assent;
- require affirmative assent for an invasive fertility-only procedure when the minor can meaningfully decide;
- defer experimental/invasive preservation when the minor objects or the burden-benefit ratio is unfavorable.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Pediatric oncology literature shows future fertility is often important to survivors and families, but decision-making preferences vary by age, urgency, treatment burden and family values. Existing affected evidence supports early counseling and involvement of young people but does not cleanly supply multiple institutional policies.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

ASRM's 2026 Ethics Committee opinion strongly converges on informing patients before gonadotoxic therapy, parental permission based on the child's potential benefit, developmentally appropriate assent, and IRB oversight for experimental procedures. It recommends not creating embryos from minors' gametes and revisiting disposition at adulthood. The guidance supports meaningful minor involvement but does not create a general requirement that assent always control parent-authorized low-risk preservation.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Open future:** infertility treatment may be impossible to reverse later → parents may preserve a valuable future option now when burden is low → allow parent authorization with assent-sensitive counseling.
- **Developing autonomy:** an invasive procedure with no present medical benefit is performed on the child's body → a mature-enough child's refusal deserves substantial authority → require affirmative assent when feasible.
- **Proportionality / nonmaleficence:** preserving possibilities does not justify any burden → where procedure is experimental, invasive or unlikely to benefit, defer rather than impose it.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- treating fertility preservation as costless;
- assuming future biological parenthood is universally valued;
- ignoring meaningful child objection;
- conflating experimental tissue preservation with established sperm/oocyte cryopreservation;
- allowing fertility preservation to delay urgent cancer therapy without explicit stipulation.
