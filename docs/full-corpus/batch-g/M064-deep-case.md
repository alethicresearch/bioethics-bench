# M064 — Artificial-womb support for extreme prematurity

**Case-family identity:** M064  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** neonatal ethics / emerging technology  
**Subdomain:** artificial-womb technology; extreme prematurity  
**Tags:** artificial womb, ectogestation, extreme prematurity, neonatal intensive care, parental consent  
**Research status:** deep case draft

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** future neonatal research/clinical program.  
**Decision question:** If artificial-womb technology becomes clinically feasible for extreme prematurity, under what initial eligibility and consent policy should a neonatal program offer it?

The case concerns artificial-womb support as a rescue technology for extreme prematurity, not complete ectogenesis replacing an entire pregnancy.

## 2. Concise representation

A future neonatal center can transfer an extremely premature fetus/newborn to an artificial-womb system that continues fluid-based extrauterine support rather than conventional ventilation. For this benchmark, assume early clinical evidence suggests a meaningful chance of improving survival without severe morbidity compared with standard intensive care at a narrow gestational threshold, but long-term outcome evidence remains limited. Parents can receive counseling and conventional neonatal care remains available. The program must decide whether to offer artificial-womb support as a parental option for eligible patients, restrict initial use to a tightly monitored trial/registry at defined gestational thresholds, or require it when the expected benefit over conventional care is substantial and parental refusal would expose the child to markedly worse outcomes.

## 3. Detailed representation

A future tertiary neonatal center has an artificial-womb platform intended for fetuses/newborns delivered at the edge of viability. The system provides fluid-based extrauterine support intended to continue organ maturation while avoiding some harms of immediate air ventilation. For this benchmark, assume controlled early clinical evidence indicates that, within a defined gestational and physiologic window, the technology has a meaningful prospect of improving survival without severe morbidity compared with conventional neonatal intensive care, but long-term neurodevelopmental evidence is still limited. Conventional intensive care remains a medically reasonable alternative. Parents have time for urgent but meaningful counseling. The center must choose a standing policy: offer artificial-womb support as one parental option alongside conventional care; restrict use to a prospective trial/registry with narrow eligibility until longer-term evidence matures; or treat artificial-womb support as the recommended/required rescue pathway when its expected benefit over conventional treatment crosses a high threshold, subject to ordinary limits on parental refusal of clearly superior life-saving care.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- technology is for extreme prematurity, not complete ectogenesis;
- benchmark stipulates early evidence of meaningful clinical benefit;
- conventional NICU care remains a reasonable alternative;
- long-term outcomes remain uncertain;
- eligibility is limited to a defined gestational/physiologic window;
- parents can participate in urgent informed decision-making.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Long-term neurodevelopment, burdens of transfer, moral/legal classification and future access inequities remain uncertain. The threshold at which a novel rescue option becomes standard care is itself part of the ethical problem.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Future-facing, evidence-dated case. Current AWT is approaching translation but not routine clinical care; the benchmark's benefit assumption is constructed and must remain explicit.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume AWT has crossed a technical threshold for carefully monitored human use.
- For this benchmark, assume conventional NICU remains medically reasonable.
- For this benchmark, assume the AWT benefit is meaningful but long-term evidence remains incomplete.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- parental-option access alongside conventional neonatal care;
- initial trial/registry-only access under narrow eligibility;
- stronger clinician authority to use AWT once benefit over conventional care becomes sufficiently large.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Current direct stakeholder evidence is inadequate for an executable policy pool. The 2026 scoping review of 247 publications found the literature split between clinically focused extreme-prematurity uses and speculative complete ectogenesis, and explicitly identified comprehensive stakeholder engagement as necessary for ethical guidance. Older small public-attitude work is insufficient to ground contemporary policy candidates.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

There is not yet mature clinical professional guidance specifying parental-choice, mandatory-use or trial-only policies for AWT. Current ethics scholarship emphasizes benefits/harms, decision-making, moral/legal status, justice/access and research ethics. Clinical translation is close enough to make the case important but not mature enough to supply a stable professional candidate architecture.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Parental authority under uncertainty:** when two medically reasonable options exist, families should weigh uncertain long-term outcomes according to their values → offer AWT as an informed option.
- **Staged innovation / precaution:** first-in-human neonatal technologies affect highly vulnerable patients and generate long-term uncertainty → learn within a structured trial/registry before normalizing routine use → restrict initial access.
- **Child best interests / harm threshold:** parental discretion narrows when one intervention becomes substantially superior at preventing death or severe harm → above a high evidence threshold, clinicians may recommend strongly and potentially override refusal under ordinary pediatric standards.
