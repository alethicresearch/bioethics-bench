# M091 — Environmental footprint of a clinical trial

**Case-family identity:** M091  
**Primary domain:** research governance / environmental sustainability  
**Tags:** clinical trials, carbon emissions, sustainability, climate, trial design, justice  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** public research funder and trial ethics/governance body.  
**Decision question:** When two trial designs preserve scientific validity and participant safety, how strongly should funders require the lower-environmental-impact design if it costs more or is less operationally convenient?

## Scenario pair

**Concise:** A multicenter clinical trial can use either a conventional design with frequent participant travel, centralized shipping and high material use, or a lower-carbon design using validated remote visits, regional testing and more sustainable procurement. The lower-carbon plan preserves the trial's primary endpoints and participant safety but increases administrative cost by about 8% and requires more planning. The funder must decide whether lower-carbon design should be required whenever validity and safety are preserved, required only when environmental gains are substantial relative to added cost/burden, or encouraged rather than required while scientific and participant priorities remain decisive.

**Detailed:** A public funder is reviewing a large pragmatic clinical trial. Independent methods review finds two designs scientifically credible. The conventional design relies on frequent participant travel to central sites, long-distance sample shipping and resource-intensive procurement. An alternative uses validated decentralized follow-up for appropriate visits, regional laboratories, consolidated logistics and lower-waste procurement. For this benchmark, assume the alternative does not reduce endpoint validity, safety monitoring, inclusion or data quality, but increases administrative spending by about 8% and requires more complex site preparation. Environmental assessment indicates a materially lower carbon/resource footprint. The funder must decide whether environmental sustainability is a required design criterion when scientific/participant outcomes are preserved; whether it should be a proportional requirement only when avoided environmental harm justifies added financial/operational burden; or whether it should remain a voluntary optimization subordinate to scientific value, safety and conventional cost considerations.

## Evidence architecture

WHO's 2026 review explicitly positions environmental sustainability as part of clinical-trial design, conduct and oversight, identifying participant travel, site operations, supply chains, data management and waste as sources of environmental impact. It proposes lower-carbon/decentralized design, sustainable procurement and governance mechanisms while emphasizing that trial integrity, social justice and LMIC capabilities must not be compromised.

Direct participant/public evidence comparing mandatory versus proportional sustainability rules is currently limited.

## Frameworks

- **Climate/public-health responsibility:** research itself creates health-relevant environmental externalities → avoidable emissions should count in ethical trial design.
- **Proportionality:** environmental benefit is one legitimate value among scientific validity, participant burden, inclusion and cost → require changes when benefits are material and trade-offs reasonable.
- **Research mission priority:** scarce research resources should principally generate valid health knowledge and protect participants → sustainability should not displace materially more valuable science when benefits are small.

## Construction risks

Do not assume decentralized trials are always lower carbon or more equitable. Keep scientific validity and participant safety fixed. Include financial and LMIC/resource trade-offs rather than treating sustainability as costless.

## Executable judgment

`research-complete-not-executable`. Current professional guidance is emerging and largely supports proportional integration; direct affected-policy evidence is insufficient.
