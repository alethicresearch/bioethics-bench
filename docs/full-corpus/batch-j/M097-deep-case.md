# M097 — Supervised consumption services as overdose harm reduction

**Case-family identity:** M097  
**Primary domain:** public health / substance-use policy  
**Tags:** supervised consumption, overdose, harm reduction, substance use, community, pilot  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** city public-health authority.  
**Decision question:** Should a city establish a supervised consumption service for people at high risk of overdose, and if so under what implementation model?

## Scenario pair

**Concise:** A city has persistently high fatal overdose rates despite naloxone distribution, medications for opioid use disorder and outreach. A proposed supervised consumption service would provide monitored use of pre-obtained drugs, overdose response, sterile supplies and voluntary linkage to treatment and social services. Evidence indicates such sites prevent onsite overdose deaths and connect people to care, while effects on population-level overdose mortality remain uncertain. Residents disagree about neighborhood impacts. The city must decide whether to establish the service as an ongoing harm-reduction program, decline it and direct resources to other treatment/prevention services, or authorize a time-limited evaluated pilot with explicit neighborhood, access and outcome safeguards.

**Detailed:** A city faces a sustained overdose crisis with high mortality. Existing interventions include widespread naloxone, medications for opioid use disorder, outreach and emergency treatment, but many people continue to use drugs in unsafe settings and experience recurrent overdose. Public-health officials propose a supervised consumption service where adults would bring their own substances, use them under trained observation and receive immediate overdose response, sterile equipment, health care and voluntary referrals. Evidence from existing services strongly supports prevention of onsite overdose deaths and engagement with health/social services; a recent systematic review finds the effect on population-level overdose mortality remains uncertain. Some local residents and businesses worry about public disorder or concentration of drug activity, while advocates and people who use drugs emphasize safety, dignity and nonfatal-overdose prevention. The city must choose among establishing a permanent service, declining the service in favor of other interventions, or authorizing a time-limited pilot with independent evaluation of overdose, service engagement and neighborhood outcomes plus community and participant advisory governance.

## Evidence architecture

Current evidence strongly supports direct onsite safety and service linkage, but broader population mortality effects are uncertain. Community and people-who-use-drugs perspectives are plural and location-sensitive. A defensible policy comparison must keep the empirical assumptions balanced and cannot imply either that the service solves the overdose crisis or that it causes neighborhood harm without evidence.

## Frameworks

- **Harm reduction / respect:** people continue to use drugs despite abstinence-oriented services → preventing immediate death and creating voluntary links to care is valuable even without abstinence.
- **Opportunity cost/community impact:** public resources and neighborhood burdens matter → choose interventions with the best overall expected health/community effects rather than assuming one harm-reduction tool must be adopted.
- **Learning under controversy:** where benefits and local externalities are uncertain, a transparent pilot can produce context-specific evidence without making a permanent commitment.

## Construction risks

Do not include operational drug-use instructions. Keep “pre-obtained substances” generic. Do not overstate population-mortality evidence. Separate moral objection to drug use from empirical neighborhood claims.

## Executable judgment

Provisional candidate-audit target. The case has genuine affected-community pluralism and distinct policy options, but candidate audit must confirm direct community/user evidence rather than using polarized public rhetoric.
