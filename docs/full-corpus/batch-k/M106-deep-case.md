# M106 — Reciprocity priority for registered organ donors

**Case-family identity:** M106  
**Primary domain:** transplantation / allocation  
**Subdomain:** reciprocity; donor registration  
**Tags:** organ allocation, donor registration, reciprocity, priority, fairness  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** national deceased-donor allocation authority.  
**Decision question:** Should prior registration as an organ donor provide additional allocation priority if that person later needs a transplant?

## 2. Concise representation

A transplant authority is considering a reciprocity rule. All candidates would first need to meet the same medical eligibility, urgency and compatibility requirements. Under the proposed rule, a person who had registered as an organ donor before becoming seriously ill would receive additional priority over an otherwise similar candidate who had not registered. The authority must decide whether registration should carry no allocation weight, whether it should provide a modest bonus or tie-break priority, or whether prior donor registration should receive substantial priority after basic clinical criteria are met.

## 3. Detailed representation

A national organ-donation system depends on voluntary public participation and faces persistent organ scarcity. The allocation authority is considering whether people who previously registered their willingness to donate should receive reciprocal priority if they later become transplant candidates. The proposal would not override medical compatibility or minimum clinical eligibility, and accommodations could be developed for people who were unable to register because of age, disability, lack of access or other recognized barriers. Supporters argue reciprocity rewards contribution and may encourage registration; critics argue access to organs should be based on need and benefit rather than prior civic behavior and that priority systems can stigmatize people or groups perceived as non-donors. The authority must choose no reciprocity criterion, a modest registration bonus/tie-break after clinical criteria, or a stronger reciprocity priority among clinically suitable candidates.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- all candidates meet baseline clinical eligibility;
- registration occurred before the current transplant need;
- policy can exempt people unable to register fairly;
- reciprocity would operate after or within clinical criteria rather than replacing them;
- rule concerns donor registration, not actual prior living donation.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

It is uncertain how much priority would change donor-registration behavior and whether social groups differ in access, trust or willingness to register for reasons unrelated to reciprocity.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral allocation-policy case grounded in public preference studies and real reciprocity systems. Current US OPTN rules are context, not the stipulated answer.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume medically relevant criteria are applied before reciprocity priority.
- For this benchmark, assume the system can exempt candidates who lacked a fair opportunity to register.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- no registration-based reciprocity criterion;
- modest bonus/tie-break for prior registration;
- strong reciprocity priority after basic clinical criteria.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Research specifically on priority for previously registered donors finds that the public is sensitive to reciprocity but does not uniformly support priority systems; concerns include divisiveness and unfairness. German allocation DCE evidence found registered-donor status significantly influenced choices but less strongly than age and predicted quality of life. These sources may support modest-versus-no reciprocity orientations, but direct evidence for a strong-priority rule is weaker.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

Current OPTN ethical principles emphasize utility, justice, autonomy and transparent rules; reciprocity is not a general US deceased-donor allocation criterion. Real reciprocity policies in Israel and Singapore demonstrate regulatory feasibility but are jurisdictional policy examples rather than a plural professional consensus.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Need/equal access:** organs are public scarce resources and entitlement should not turn on past civic behavior → use clinical criteria without reciprocity.
- **Fair reciprocity:** willingness to contribute to the donor pool can justify a modest advantage when clinical claims are otherwise close → use a bounded bonus or tie-break.
- **Reciprocal cooperation:** a donation system depends on mutual willingness to give and receive → stronger priority for prior registrants can deter free-riding and sustain participation, with exemptions for inability to register.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- assuming reciprocity sensitivity equals support for strong priority;
- penalizing people without a fair opportunity to register;
- conflating registered intent with actual prior organ donation;
- treating Israeli/Singaporean policy as professional evidence for all settings.
