# M105 — Compensation for living kidney donation

**Case-family identity:** M105  
**Primary domain:** transplantation / living donation  
**Subdomain:** compensation; organ markets; financial neutrality  
**Tags:** kidney donation, compensation, incentives, organ markets, exploitation  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** national transplant regulator.  
**Decision question:** Should a regulated system permit financial compensation beyond reimbursement for living kidney donation?

## 2. Concise representation

A country has a persistent shortage of kidneys for transplantation despite reimbursement of donors' medical costs, travel and lost wages. A proposal would allow a public agency, rather than recipients, to provide a fixed substantial payment to medically suitable living kidney donors. Donors would still undergo independent evaluation, could withdraw at any time and could not bargain over price. The regulator must decide whether to retain financial neutrality with reimbursement only, allow a regulated fixed public payment as an incentive, or authorize only pilot research on compensation before changing the national rule.

## 3. Detailed representation

A national transplant authority is reviewing living kidney donation policy. The current system reimburses verified donation-related medical expenses, travel, caregiving costs and lost wages so donors should not be financially worse off, but it does not permit donors to receive financial gain. Kidney scarcity remains severe. A proposed reform would create a public monopsony: an independent public agency would offer every medically eligible living donor the same substantial fixed payment, with no bargaining, no direct recipient payment and continued donor advocacy, screening, consent and long-term follow-up. Proponents argue this could expand donation and reduce deaths on dialysis; critics argue even regulated payment can exploit economic vulnerability and convert organs into commodities. The authority must decide whether to preserve financial neutrality, permit the regulated payment system, or authorize only a time-limited ethically monitored compensation trial before any permanent change.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- current system already reimburses legitimate donation costs/lost wages;
- proposed payment creates financial gain beyond neutrality;
- public agency pays a fixed amount; recipients do not bargain directly;
- ordinary donor consent/safety protections remain;
- organ shortage is substantial.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

The effect of payment on donation rates, socioeconomic composition of donors, coercion, long-term donor welfare and public trust is uncertain. Regulated payment may reduce some market harms without eliminating exploitation concerns.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral policy case. The benchmark stipulates legal authority to choose among the represented regimes rather than letting existing statutory bans decide the case.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume reimbursement already achieves reasonable financial neutrality for ordinary donation expenses.
- For this benchmark, assume any payment is fixed and administered by a public agency rather than negotiated with recipients.
- For this benchmark, assume existing donor medical/psychosocial safeguards remain.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- financial neutrality only: reimbursement without financial gain;
- regulated fixed public compensation;
- compensation only within monitored pilot research before permanent authorization.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

A US voter survey of 1,011 respondents found substantial willingness to support payment and reported that a proposed $50,000 payment would increase many respondents' willingness to donate. This is direct evidence for a pro-compensation public orientation. It does not by itself establish support for the exact public-monopsony design or a compensation trial rather than permanent policy.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

The Declaration of Istanbul states organ donation should be financially neutral: donors should neither lose nor gain financially. It strongly supports comprehensive reimbursement of actual costs and protection from financial injury while rejecting financial incentives or valuable benefits in exchange for organs. Regulated-compensation proposals remain prominent in academic scholarship, but current international professional governance is substantially convergent on financial neutrality.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Anti-exploitation / noncommodification:** financial gain can target economic vulnerability and alter the meaning of donation → protect donors from loss but prohibit profit.
- **Autonomy / welfare expansion:** competent adults already accept donation risk altruistically and a regulated payment may benefit donors and recipients while reducing scarcity → permit fixed public compensation under safeguards.
- **Evidence-sensitive reform:** both benefits and distributional harms are uncertain → test compensation prospectively under public oversight before permanent authorization.
