# Batch K candidate audit — M102 and M106

**Purpose:** test the only M101–M110 cases whose source classes plausibly support action-distinct executable policy pools under the Full Corpus source-to-policy standard. No JSON should be created before independent review.

## M102 — Opt-out deceased donation and family veto

### Public / affected candidates

**pub1 — deceased/deemed choice prevails over family preference**  
`In an adequately publicized opt-out system, proceed with donation when the deceased did not opt out unless the family provides credible evidence that donation would conflict with the deceased person's actual wishes; family preference alone is not a veto.`

**Support:** European public-attitude evidence includes strong opposition to family veto where the deceased had expressed a donation choice; UK studies summarized in Molina-Pérez et al. report only 8–14% support for families vetoing expressed donor consent and 64–88% opposition. The benchmark scenario uses deemed rather than explicit consent, so the clause limiting override to evidence of contrary wishes is **Bench-authored policy completion** needed to translate the autonomy orientation into the opt-out case. The core action—family preference should not routinely overrule the deceased's donation choice—is directly supported.

**pub2 — family has final refusal authority**  
`Do not recover organs when close family members object, even when the deceased did not opt out, because the family should retain final authority over donation when no explicit donor authorization is available.`

**Support:** the same systematic review finds substantial public support for family decision-making, especially when wishes are unknown; surveys framed from the next-of-kin perspective found majorities wanting consultation/final permission. This candidate is bounded to the no-explicit-authorization opt-out case and does not claim public support for overriding a clear registered donor choice.

**Distinctness:** pass. The policies yield different outcomes when relatives object for their own reasons but provide no evidence of contrary deceased wishes.

### Expert candidate

**exp1 — family consultation as evidence of the deceased's wishes, not independent veto**  
`Consult relatives to identify the deceased person's known wishes and relevant circumstances, but treat the donation authorization framework—not the family's own preference—as the governing basis for recovery.`

**Support:** HRSA/OPTN deceased-donor recovery ethics distinguishes donor authorization from surrogate decision-making where wishes are unknown and emphasizes substituted judgment. Adapted to an opt-out system; not a claim about one jurisdiction's law.

### Framework candidates

**fw1 — autonomy/public commitment**  
`Honor the deceased person's participation in an adequately publicized opt-out system unless evidence shows they would have refused, because allowing family preference to cancel the scheme makes the individual's donation choice unreliable.`

**fw2 — relational/family authority**  
`Give close family members final authority when the deceased never explicitly opted in because postmortem donation is embedded in family relationships and deemed consent is too weak to override serious family objection.`

### Judgment

**Provisional profile: `full-corpus-2x1x2-mean-v1`.** Survives candidate audit, with one important provenance note: pub1's specific “credible evidence of contrary wishes” exception is narrow Bench-authored policy completion; the source-supported core is rejection of family preference as a routine override.

## M106 — Reciprocity priority for registered organ donors

### Public candidates

**pub1 — no registration-based priority**  
`Do not add donor-registration status to allocation priority; rank clinically eligible candidates by medical and ordinary fairness criteria without rewarding prior registration.`

**Support:** a representative Israeli public survey of 604 adults found donor status was ranked the least important allocation criterion by 43.9% of respondents, including 43.2% of registered donors; prognosis/maximum benefit and waiting time were much more highly valued. This directly supports low/no weight for registration in allocation rather than merely general concern about reciprocity.

**pub2 — bounded reciprocity priority**  
`Give candidates who registered as organ donors before becoming seriously ill a modest allocation bonus after core clinical criteria, with exemptions for people who lacked a fair opportunity to register.`

**Support:** the Chandler/Burkell/Shemie review documents public support for reciprocity priority in several settings and real policy adoption in Israel/Singapore; German DCE evidence finds registered-donor status significantly influences allocation choices. **Bench-authored policy completion:** the “modest bonus” and fair-opportunity exemptions operationalize the source-supported reciprocity-priority orientation without creating a dominant clinical override.

**Distinctness:** pass. One excludes donor status; the other deliberately assigns it positive weight.

### Expert candidate

**exp1 — transparent clinical/justice allocation without registration reward**  
`Use transparent allocation criteria grounded in medical utility, urgency, equitable opportunity and other justified clinical/fairness factors rather than prior donor registration as a general reward criterion.`

**Support:** current OPTN ethical allocation principles. The current US architecture does not generally reward ordinary prior donor registration; actual prior living donors are a separate category and are excluded from this case.

### Framework candidates

**fw1 — equal need / anti-desert**  
`Do not condition access to a lifesaving public resource on prior donor registration because clinical need and benefit, not civic deservingness, should govern scarce-organ allocation.`

**fw2 — reciprocal cooperation**  
`Give a bounded priority advantage to people who previously joined the donor pool because a reciprocal system can fairly recognize willingness to contribute while preserving core clinical criteria and exemptions for inability to register.`

### Judgment

**Provisional profile: `full-corpus-2x1x2-mean-v1`.** Survives candidate audit. This is a stronger executable case than initially expected because the public evidence includes both explicit low/no-weight preferences and pro-reciprocity evidence, while real national policy demonstrates the orientation is actionable.
