# M108 — Xenotransplant recipient obligations to third parties

**Case-family identity:** M108  
**Primary domain:** transplantation / xenotransplantation  
**Subdomain:** infection surveillance; right to withdraw  
**Tags:** xenotransplantation, biosurveillance, public health, withdrawal, privacy  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** xenotransplant clinical program / regulator.  
**Decision question:** May a xenotransplant program require lifelong infection surveillance and limits on withdrawal because of possible infection risk to third parties?

## 2. Concise representation

A patient with otherwise terminal organ failure is offered an experimental genetically modified pig-organ transplant. The patient has capacity and understands that a novel animal-derived infection could theoretically affect close contacts or the wider public. The program requires long-term blood sampling, clinical reporting and specimen archiving. The patient asks whether they could later stop surveillance if it becomes burdensome. The program must decide whether lifelong surveillance is a nonwaivable condition of xenotransplantation, whether the recipient retains an ordinary right to withdraw from follow-up, or whether a bounded surveillance obligation should apply only while evidence shows a material third-party infection risk and with strong privacy protections.

## 3. Detailed representation

A regulated xenotransplant program enrolls patients with end-stage organ failure for an experimental genetically modified pig-organ transplant. Because animal-to-human transplantation creates a theoretical risk of novel infectious transmission, current public-health guidance contemplates long-term recipient monitoring and archival specimens. A competent candidate is willing to undergo transplantation but objects to an indefinite loss of control over future medical participation. The program can explain surveillance requirements before consent, minimize data collection, protect confidentiality and periodically reassess the scientific basis of monitoring. The policy question is whether recipients must accept lifelong biosurveillance as a nonwaivable condition of transplantation; whether they retain the same right to withdraw from research/follow-up as other patients; or whether surveillance obligations may be mandatory only while an independently reviewed threshold of material third-party risk persists, with the least intrusive monitoring compatible with public safety.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- transplant is experimental xenotransplantation;
- third-party infectious risk is plausible but uncertain;
- long-term surveillance can generate public-health information;
- recipient has capacity at enrollment;
- privacy safeguards and periodic risk review are feasible.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

The probability and magnitude of xenozoonotic risk are uncertain and may change with technology and accumulated experience. It is also contested whether prior consent can validly bind a recipient to lifelong future participation.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Evidence-dated 2026 emerging-governance case, grounded in PHS/FDA infectious-disease guidance and current xenotransplant ethics.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume the recipient is fully informed before transplantation that long-term surveillance is proposed.
- For this benchmark, assume there is a nonzero but poorly quantified potential third-party infection risk.
- For this benchmark, assume monitoring can be performed with strong privacy safeguards.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- lifelong nonwaivable surveillance as a condition of xenotransplantation;
- ordinary recipient right to withdraw despite prior agreement;
- bounded mandatory surveillance only while a material public-risk threshold persists.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

A 2024 nationally representative US survey of 5,008 adults shows substantial public interest and concern regarding xenotransplantation, including complication and evidence concerns, but it does not directly test whether recipients should be bound to lifelong biosurveillance. General xenotransplant acceptance cannot be translated into surveillance policy.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

PHS/FDA infectious-disease guidance supports extensive long-term monitoring and specimen archiving for xenotransplant recipients. Contemporary bioethics scholarship directly challenges removal of the right to withdraw, arguing that if public risk is sufficiently high to justify irrevocable monitoring it may imply still stronger restrictions, whereas if not, ordinary withdrawal should remain. The expert/normative dispute is therefore real but not matched by public-policy evidence.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Reciprocity/nonmaleficence to third parties:** accepting a xenograft creates foreseeable public-health obligations that ordinary recipients do not impose → surveillance can be a condition of access.
- **Continuing autonomy:** consent to a lifesaving experimental transplant does not transfer indefinite control over a person's future body/data → preserve a meaningful right to withdraw.
- **Least-restrictive public health:** third-party protection can justify limits on autonomy only while risk is material and no less intrusive measure suffices → use bounded, reviewed surveillance obligations.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- overstating xenozoonotic risk as known/high;
- assuming consent at transplant can waive all future autonomy;
- treating public acceptance of xenotransplantation as support for surveillance coercion;
- ignoring privacy and least-restrictive alternatives.
