# M109 — Care after transplant tourism

**Case-family identity:** M109  
**Primary domain:** transplantation / global ethics  
**Subdomain:** transplant tourism; continuity of care  
**Tags:** transplant tourism, organ trafficking, continuity, nonabandonment, reporting  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** domestic transplant health system.  
**Decision question:** What obligations does a domestic health system have to a patient returning after an overseas transplant that may have involved organ trafficking or commercialism?

## 2. Concise representation

A resident returns home after buying an organ transplant through a poorly regulated overseas program. Records are incomplete, and clinicians are concerned that the organ may have been obtained through exploitative or illegal practices. The patient now needs urgent immunosuppression management and complication surveillance. The domestic transplant service must decide how to provide medically necessary follow-up while avoiding facilitation of future transplant tourism, preserving traceability and complying with any legitimate reporting duties.

## 3. Detailed representation

A patient who had previously been advised against purchasing an overseas transplant returns to the domestic health system after receiving a kidney through a commercial intermediary in a jurisdiction with weak donor protections and incomplete records. The source of the organ cannot be independently verified, and there is concern that trafficking or exploitation may have occurred. The patient has a functioning graft but requires prompt specialist evaluation, immunosuppression, infection screening and long-term follow-up. The health system also has obligations to maintain registries and may have a lawful pathway for reporting suspected transplant-related crime. It must determine a standing policy that preserves medically necessary care and nonabandonment while refusing to finance, arrange or medically facilitate future illicit transplantation and using proportionate traceability/reporting mechanisms.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- patient has already undergone transplantation;
- organ source is uncertain and may involve exploitation/trafficking;
- follow-up is medically necessary and interruption creates serious harm;
- care after return is distinct from facilitating travel or procurement;
- traceability/reporting can be separated from denial of care.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Clinicians may not know whether trafficking actually occurred, and reporting rules vary by jurisdiction. Patients may also have limited information about how their organ was obtained.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral continuity-of-care case grounded in Declaration of Istanbul Custodian Group recommendations. Specific criminal-reporting duties are stipulated rather than assumed.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume the patient requires ongoing transplant care to prevent serious harm.
- For this benchmark, assume the domestic system did not arrange or finance the overseas transplant.
- For this benchmark, assume a lawful confidential mechanism exists for documenting and, where warranted, reporting suspected trafficking.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- full nonjudgmental clinical care with ordinary confidentiality;
- necessary follow-up combined with explicit non-facilitation of future tourism and registry/traceability duties;
- necessary care plus proportionate reporting/ethics review when trafficking is reasonably suspected.

These are likely nested components of one professional architecture rather than truly competing expert policies.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Direct comparative evidence from returning transplant recipients or the public about these post-return institutional policies is limited. General attitudes toward organ markets or trafficking do not supply a policy for treating an already transplanted patient.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

The Declaration of Istanbul Custodian Group recommends prompt referral and appropriate care for returning patients, continuity and traceability, while opposing payment/facilitation of transplant tourism and supporting appropriate frameworks for reporting suspected transplant-related crimes. This is a mature integrated architecture: nonabandonment does not imply endorsement.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Nonabandonment:** clinicians should not impose medical harm as punishment for a patient's prior ethically problematic decision → provide necessary care.
- **Complicity avoidance:** care for complications is distinct from helping procure another illicit organ → refuse facilitation and financial support for trafficking-related transplantation.
- **Justice/accountability:** protecting vulnerable donors and transplant systems can justify proportionate traceability and lawful reporting without withholding medical treatment.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- treating suspected trafficking as proven;
- using denial of follow-up as punishment;
- confusing nonabandonment with facilitation;
- creating separate candidates from nested care/traceability/reporting components of one policy.
