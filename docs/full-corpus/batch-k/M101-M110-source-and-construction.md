# Bioethics Bench — Batch K source and construction packet (M101–M110)

**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27  
**Scope:** transplantation and donation ethics: age, family authority, living-donor risk, retransplantation/nonadherence, compensation, reciprocity, normothermic regional perfusion, xenotransplant surveillance, transplant tourism, and immigration status.

This packet applies the Full Corpus source-to-policy construction standard. Acceptability, moral concern, treatment-risk tolerance, or abstract philosophical disagreement are not translated into institutional rules unless the source already supports that policy direction.

## M101 — Age in organ allocation

**Decision object:** deceased-donor allocation policy when two candidates have comparable expected transplant benefit but substantially different ages.

**Professional/governance evidence:** OPTN ethical guidance says age alone should not determine transplant candidacy, while longevity and graft success may be considered; pediatric allocation guidance separately identifies fair-innings, prudential-lifespan, maximin and utility reasons for pediatric priority. Thus current professional ethics distinguishes age-as-proxy/organ-specific pediatric priority from simple age discrimination.

**Public evidence:** organ-allocation discrete-choice studies in Germany and Australia find younger age materially influences allocation preferences, but age competes with expected benefit, urgency, waiting time, quality of life and other attributes. These studies support public willingness to use age, not necessarily two distinct age policies.

**Likely judgment:** strong research case; candidate audit needed before executability. Avoid turning “younger preferred” into a freestanding life-cycle rule unless the public source supports the rule itself.

## M102 — Opt-out deceased donation and family veto

**Decision object:** policy in an opt-out system when the deceased did not register an objection and family members object to organ recovery.

**Public evidence:** a 2022 European systematic review found a majority supports a family role when the deceased's wishes are unknown, but views on family override of expressed/deemed consent are ambivalent and highly sensitive to perspective. UK studies cited in the review found only small minorities support veto of explicit donor consent, while next-of-kin framed questions produce more support for final family permission. A 2023 experimental study in an opt-out context found family approval differs depending on deceased wishes and regret/beliefs.

**Professional/governance evidence:** donor authorization ethics emphasizes the donor's autonomous gift and substituted judgment when wishes are unknown. Jurisdictions vary in whether family objection has legal or operational force, so the benchmark must stipulate legal authority and ask about program policy rather than treat current law as the answer.

**Likely policies:** deceased choice/deemed consent prevails; family veto; family consultation with rebuttable presumption favoring donation. M102 is a strong candidate-audit target.

## M103 — Acceptable risk to a competent living organ donor

**Decision object:** transplant-program risk ceiling for a fully informed competent living donor who seeks to accept unusually high medical risk to help a recipient.

**Professional evidence:** current HRSA/OPTN living-donor guidance requires voluntariness, independent advocacy, comprehension, individualized medical/psychosocial evaluation, disclosure of short- and long-term risks, and a judgment that benefits outweigh risks. OPTN has moved toward individualized lifetime-risk assessment in some exclusion criteria. Guidance does not treat donor autonomy as sufficient by itself.

**Affected evidence:** living-donor literature documents heterogeneous risk tolerance, motivations and psychosocial benefit, but direct comparative donor preferences about program risk ceilings are limited.

**Likely judgment:** needs additional evidence for executable public candidates; important case for autonomy versus professional nonmaleficence.

## M104 — Retransplantation after prior nonadherence

**Decision object:** whether past behavior contributing to graft loss should reduce access to retransplantation.

**Professional evidence:** OPTN candidacy ethics says adherence measures are imperfect, social and structural barriers must be assessed, and potentially injurious behavior should not be a sole exclusion criterion unless risk overwhelmingly outweighs benefit. Repeat transplantation raises justice concerns but graft failure has many causes beyond patient control.

**Public evidence:** organ-allocation preference studies often show public concern with perceived responsibility for organ failure, but this is not equivalent to a policy for retransplant after documented nonadherence.

**Likely policies:** no moralized penalty; evidence-based future-adherence assessment after barrier mitigation; lower priority only when a clinically meaningful modifiable recurrence risk remains. Under the strict standard, these may still collapse into one professional conditional architecture.

## M105 — Compensation for living kidney donation

**Decision object:** whether a regulated health system should allow financial compensation beyond reimbursement for living kidney donation.

**Public evidence:** a US voter survey (n=1,011) found substantial support for payment and reported that a proposed $50,000 payment would increase willingness to donate for many respondents. This supports a pro-compensation orientation.

**Professional/governance evidence:** the Declaration of Istanbul states organ donation should be financially neutral: donors should neither lose nor gain financially. It endorses comprehensive reimbursement of actual costs and protection from financial injury while rejecting financial gain/incentives. Current US law likewise prohibits valuable consideration for organs.

**Likely judgment:** research-complete, but professional source class strongly converges against payment beyond financial neutrality. A regulated-compensation position is prominent in scholarship but should not be mislabeled professional consensus.

## M106 — Reciprocity priority for registered organ donors

**Decision object:** whether prior donor registration should give an otherwise eligible transplant candidate additional allocation priority.

**Public evidence:** public-preference studies find reciprocity matters, but sensitivity to reciprocity does not uniformly translate into support for priority systems. German DCE evidence found registered-donor status influenced choices but less strongly than age and predicted quality of life. Real priority systems have existed in Israel and Singapore.

**Professional/governance evidence:** OPTN allocation ethics emphasizes utility, justice, respect for persons and transparent allocation; current US allocation does not generally give registration-based reciprocity priority. The professional source layer may remain singleton.

**Likely policies:** no reciprocity criterion; modest tie-break/bonus priority; stronger reciprocity priority after clinical criteria. Candidate audit should test whether public evidence actually supports at least two action-distinct rules rather than only a general reciprocity value.

## M107 — Normothermic regional perfusion after circulatory death

**Decision object:** whether and under what safeguards a transplant system should use NRP after declaration of death by circulatory criteria.

**Current governance:** HRSA's August 2026 NRP page describes rapid growth of DCD donation, ongoing OPTN/HRSA safety work, variation in protocols, and continuing development of safety standards focused on preventing unintended brain reperfusion, donor safety, family experience, monitoring, coordination and transparency. OPTN's 2024 ethics paper supports consistent transparent protocols and adequate informed consent but is not policy.

**Professional disagreement:** major organizations have issued differing positions; contemporary scholarship includes both defended implementation with safeguards and objections grounded in death determination/dead-donor-rule concerns. 2025–2026 ethics papers continue active disagreement.

**Public evidence:** direct informed-public policy evidence specific to NRP remains limited.

**Likely judgment:** research-complete-not-executable for now. Professional pluralism cannot substitute for missing affected/public policy evidence.

## M108 — Xenotransplant recipient obligations to third parties

**Decision object:** whether a xenotransplant program may require lifelong infection surveillance and restrictions on withdrawal from follow-up because of possible xenozoonotic risk.

**Governance evidence:** PHS/FDA xenotransplant guidance calls for long-term/lifelong recipient surveillance and specimen archiving. Xenotransplantation therefore creates public-health obligations unlike ordinary transplantation.

**Normative disagreement:** a 2025 bioethics analysis argues recipients should retain a right to withdraw from long-term biosurveillance unless third-party risk is high enough to justify much stronger restrictions such as quarantine. This is genuine normative disagreement about the limit of prior consent.

**Public evidence:** a 2024 nationally representative US survey (n=5,008) documents public attitudes to xenotransplantation generally, including concerns about complications and limited evidence, but does not directly compare surveillance/withdrawal policies.

**Likely judgment:** research-complete-not-executable until direct recipient/public evidence on surveillance obligations exists.

## M109 — Care after transplant tourism

**Decision object:** obligations of a domestic health system to a returning patient whose transplant abroad may have involved organ trafficking or transplant commercialism.

**Professional/governance evidence:** Declaration of Istanbul Custodian Group recommendations emphasize prompt referral for evaluation and care, continuity, traceability and registries while opposing financing/facilitation of illicit transplantation and encouraging reporting frameworks for suspected crimes. Non-abandonment is separated from endorsement.

**Affected/public evidence:** direct comparative patient/public policy evidence is limited.

**Likely judgment:** research-complete-not-executable; one mature professional architecture combines care with non-facilitation and accountability.

## M110 — Transplant eligibility for undocumented migrants

**Decision object:** whether immigration/residency status should affect eligibility for publicly supported transplantation when clinical benefit and follow-up feasibility are otherwise established.

**Professional/governance evidence:** OPTN states immigration status alone should not determine or exclude transplant candidacy; citizenship/residency status must not be considered in deceased-donor allocation. It emphasizes that noncitizens also participate as donors and that structural/financial barriers should be addressed directly. The 2018 Declaration of Istanbul defines residents by where they make their life, not citizenship, and says residents should have equitable access to transplantation.

**Public evidence:** allocation studies sometimes include citizenship or social-status attributes, but direct affected-migrant policy evidence is inadequate for an executable public pool.

**Likely judgment:** research-complete-not-executable or needs-additional-evidence. Do not construct an exclusionary affected-public candidate from generalized scarcity attitudes.

## Batch-K working hypothesis

Primary candidate-audit targets after deep-file construction: **M102 and M106**. M101 may be audited if the public age-preference data translate cleanly into distinct allocation rules. M105 has a real public/professional conflict but not necessarily two professional candidates. M107 has professional pluralism but lacks direct public-policy evidence. M103/M104/M108/M109/M110 are likely non-executable under the strict Full Corpus standard.
