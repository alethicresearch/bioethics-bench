# M103 — Acceptable risk to a living organ donor

**Case-family identity:** M103  
**Primary domain:** transplantation / living donation  
**Subdomain:** donor risk; autonomy; program duty  
**Tags:** living donation, donor risk, autonomy, nonmaleficence, informed consent  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** living-donor transplant program.  
**Decision question:** How much medical risk may a transplant program allow a fully informed competent living donor to accept for another person's benefit?

## 2. Concise representation

A competent adult wants to donate part of an organ to a close relative with a high likelihood of substantial recipient benefit. The donor understands the procedure and has independent advocacy, but an individualized evaluation estimates a materially higher-than-usual lifetime risk of serious donor morbidity. The donor repeatedly states that accepting that risk is consistent with their values. The transplant program must decide whether informed donor autonomy can justify proceeding within a broad risk ceiling, whether the program should apply a strict donor-risk ceiling regardless of the donor's willingness, or whether the acceptable ceiling may vary with individualized donor risk, psychosocial benefit and expected recipient benefit.

## 3. Detailed representation

A living-donor program evaluates a competent adult who wishes to donate to a close relative. The recipient is expected to receive a substantial survival and quality-of-life benefit. The donor has independent legal/psychosocial support and an independent donor advocate, understands that donation provides no direct medical benefit, and can withdraw at any time. Medical evaluation identifies a donor-specific condition that does not make surgery immediately unsafe but raises the projected lifetime probability of a serious donation-related health outcome above the program's usual range. The donor understands this and strongly prefers to proceed. The program must decide whether to allow a well-informed donor to accept the higher risk within a broad upper limit, enforce a fixed risk ceiling that cannot be waived by consent, or use a structured individualized balancing process that considers the donor's absolute risk, uncertainty, psychosocial interests and expected recipient benefit while retaining a program-level upper boundary.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- donor has capacity, voluntariness, independent advocacy and adequate comprehension;
- risk is materially above usual but not certain or immediately prohibitive;
- recipient benefit is substantial;
- donor receives no direct medical benefit;
- question concerns program authorization, not only donor consent.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Long-term living-donor risk prediction is imperfect. Psychosocial benefits are real for some donors but difficult to quantify and should not erase medical harm.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral program ethics informed by current HRSA/OPTN living-donor guidance. All represented policies are assumed lawful.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume donor capacity and voluntariness are secure.
- For this benchmark, assume the donor-specific medical risk estimate is evidence-based but uncertain.
- For this benchmark, assume the recipient benefit is substantial.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- broad autonomy-respecting permission within a high outer risk ceiling;
- strict program risk ceiling independent of donor preference;
- individualized balancing with a nonwaivable upper boundary.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Living donors display heterogeneous motivations and willingness to accept risk, especially for close relatives, but the current evidence base does not directly compare institutional risk-ceiling policies. Donor willingness is therefore relevant context, not a source for a program rule.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

HRSA/OPTN requires full risk disclosure, independent donor advocacy, voluntariness and medical/psychosocial suitability. Living-donation ethics weighs donor harm against recipient and psychosocial benefits and increasingly favors individualized risk assessment, but program teams retain a duty to minimize donor risk and may decline donation despite donor willingness. This is one broad conditional architecture rather than a clean expert split.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Autonomy:** competent adults may accept significant bodily risk for deeply held relational commitments → programs should not make ordinary risk aversion an absolute veto.
- **Professional nonmaleficence:** clinicians intentionally expose a healthy donor to harm without medical benefit → consent cannot make every risk ethically acceptable → enforce a nonwaivable ceiling.
- **Proportional relational beneficence:** donor risk, recipient benefit and donor psychosocial interests all matter → individualized balancing is justified, but a program retains an upper limit to avoid exploitation or preventable severe harm.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- treating informed consent as sufficient for any risk;
- assigning a precise numerical ceiling without source support;
- counting recipient benefit twice through donor psychosocial benefit;
- ignoring uncertainty in lifetime risk estimates.
