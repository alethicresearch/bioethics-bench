# M102 — Opt-out deceased donation and family veto

**Case-family identity:** M102  
**Primary domain:** transplantation / deceased donation  
**Subdomain:** opt-out consent; family authority  
**Tags:** organ donation, opt-out, presumed consent, family veto, donor autonomy  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** national organ-donation authority designing operational policy for an opt-out system.  
**Decision question:** When an adult did not register an objection to organ donation, should a family's objection prevent organ recovery after death?

The case is prospective policy design, not a legal question about one current jurisdiction.

## 2. Concise representation

A country uses an opt-out deceased-donation system. Adults are informed that they are deemed willing to donate unless they register an objection, and the deceased person in this case did not opt out or leave a separate written instruction. After death, close family members object to organ recovery because they believe donation would be distressing and say the family should make the final decision. The donation authority must choose whether deemed consent should ordinarily prevail despite family objection, whether family members should have a final veto, or whether families should be consulted and allowed to rebut a presumption favoring donation when they provide credible evidence that donation would conflict with the deceased person's actual wishes or other exceptional circumstances.

## 3. Detailed representation

A national health system has adopted opt-out deceased organ donation after extensive public information and an accessible process for registering refusal. An adult dies in circumstances allowing organ donation. The person had not opted out, had not registered an explicit donor authorization, and had not left a known written statement about donation. Close relatives strongly oppose recovery, saying organ donation would violate family beliefs and that the family should decide what happens to the body. The transplant system recognizes that family cooperation matters practically and emotionally, but also that routinely allowing veto may undermine the deceased's autonomous participation in an opt-out system and reduce donation. The authority must choose a standing policy: proceed on the basis of deemed consent unless evidence shows the deceased would have objected; allow the family to refuse donation for its own reasons; or consult the family under a rebuttable presumption favoring donation, permitting objection only when relatives provide credible evidence about the deceased's wishes or another narrowly defined exceptional reason.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- opt-out rules were publicly communicated and refusal was accessible;
- the deceased did not opt out;
- there is no explicit registered donor authorization beyond deemed consent;
- relatives object for their own beliefs/distress unless they can show the deceased's contrary wishes;
- the policy must distinguish family evidence about the deceased from family preference itself.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Silence in an opt-out system may reflect acceptance, indifference, lack of awareness or inertia. Family opposition can also affect trust and the quality of end-of-life care even where it lacks formal legal authority.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral policy-design case with legal authority stipulated. Evidence is drawn from European opt-out systems and donor-authorization ethics.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume the opt-out system was accompanied by adequate public notice and an accessible refusal mechanism.
- For this benchmark, assume the family has legal opportunity to be consulted but the law allows the authority to choose whether family objection is decisive.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- deemed consent/deceased choice prevails unless contrary wishes are evidenced;
- final family veto;
- family consultation with rebuttable presumption favoring donation.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

The 2022 European systematic review found that many people support a family role, especially when the deceased's wishes are unknown, while attitudes toward family veto are ambivalent and sensitive to whether respondents imagine themselves as donors or next-of-kin. In several UK studies only small minorities supported overriding an explicit donor's choice, while surveys framed from the relative's perspective showed much stronger demand to be consulted or give final permission. This supports real disagreement about family authority, although deemed consent is not identical to explicit donor authorization.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

Deceased-donation ethics emphasizes respect for donor authorization and substituted judgment when wishes are unknown. Professional practice often involves family consultation even where authorization does not legally require a second consent. Current sources do not yield two equally mature professional policies for the exact opt-out/no-explicit-choice case.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Donor autonomy / public commitment:** opt-out has moral force only if the system respects the choice structure it created → family preference should not routinely cancel deemed participation.
- **Relational/family authority:** death and organ recovery occur within relationships and affect grieving relatives → legitimate donation governance should give families decisive authority where the deceased did not explicitly opt in.
- **Epistemic substituted judgment:** relatives are valuable witnesses to the deceased's actual values but should not substitute their own preferences → consult them under a rebuttable presumption favoring donation.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- treating deemed consent as identical to explicit registered consent;
- treating family consultation and family veto as the same policy;
- inferring the deceased's wishes from relatives' own beliefs;
- assuming family distress is ethically irrelevant because donation is legally authorized.

The public layer is mixed by basis, and correctly so. `pub2` is **direct policy evidence**: studies
of the public and of next-of-kin document substantial support for family consultation and final
permission where the deceased registered no explicit choice, which is the represented action. `pub1`
is **source-informed inference**: evidence of resistance to routine family override concerns
override of a *registered* decision, so translating it to deemed participation required narrowing
the candidate to override on evidence of the deceased's own contrary wishes. That narrowing is the
inference bridge and the record states it.

The expert layer is two real institutional architectures, not one split in two. The Netherlands Donor
Act records no-registered-choice as 'no objection', with family able to stop donation only by
convincingly showing the deceased opposed it. Current NHS Organ Donation operational guidance starts
from deemed consent but states clinicians will never proceed over family objection. Those produce
different actions on the fixed facts — a family objecting without evidence of the deceased's wishes
is exactly where they diverge.

Three framework positions: donor-choice reliability; family authority under bereavement; and a
rebuttable presumption treating relatives as witnesses to the deceased's values rather than
substitute deciders. The third is action-distinct from both, not a compromise label.
