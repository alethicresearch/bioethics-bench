# M110 — Transplant eligibility for undocumented migrants

**Case-family identity:** M110  
**Primary domain:** transplantation / structural justice  
**Subdomain:** immigration status; residency; public financing  
**Tags:** undocumented migrants, transplant eligibility, immigration, residency, equity  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** publicly supported transplant program / health system.  
**Decision question:** Should immigration or citizenship status affect transplant eligibility when a long-term resident otherwise meets clinical criteria and a feasible follow-up/financing plan exists?

## 2. Concise representation

A long-term resident without lawful immigration status has end-stage organ failure and is medically eligible for transplantation. For this benchmark, assume the patient has a realistic plan for immunosuppression, follow-up and financing comparable to other candidates. The patient has lived and worked in the country for many years and participates in the same deceased-donation system as other residents. The program must decide whether clinical eligibility should be assessed without regard to immigration status, whether transplantation should require citizenship or lawful residency as a condition of publicly supported access, or whether eligibility should turn only on concrete continuity and financing feasibility rather than legal status itself.

## 3. Detailed representation

A publicly supported transplant program evaluates an adult who has lived in the country for more than a decade but lacks lawful immigration status. The patient has end-stage organ failure, satisfies medical criteria and is expected to receive substantial benefit. For this benchmark, assume a charitable/public funding arrangement and local support network can provide post-transplant medications, transportation and follow-up at a level comparable to that available to other listed candidates. The patient is not a visitor who entered the country to obtain transplantation and has participated in ordinary community life and the same deceased-organ donation system from which residents may become donors. The program must decide whether immigration/citizenship status should be excluded from candidacy decisions; whether publicly supported transplantation should require lawful residency/citizenship; or whether programs may consider only concrete financing and continuity constraints using the same standards for everyone, without treating immigration status as a proxy for those constraints.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- patient is a long-term resident, not a transplant tourist;
- clinical benefit is favorable;
- follow-up and medication continuity are feasible;
- financing is stipulated feasible rather than left as an immigration-status proxy;
- immigration status is therefore isolated as the disputed criterion.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Health systems face real financing and continuity constraints, but those constraints vary independently of immigration status. Public intuitions about citizenship and scarce resources may also be shaped by political attitudes rather than transplant-specific evidence.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral structural-justice case informed by current US OPTN ethics and the Declaration of Istanbul. The benchmark assumes legal authority to list the patient.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume the patient is a long-term resident rather than a visitor seeking transplantation.
- For this benchmark, assume post-transplant medication, follow-up and financing are realistically secured.
- For this benchmark, assume all represented policies are legally available.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- equal eligibility using clinical criteria regardless of immigration status;
- citizenship/lawful-residency requirement for publicly supported transplantation;
- status-neutral eligibility with transparent feasibility criteria for financing and follow-up applied equally to all candidates.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

General organ-allocation studies sometimes show public preferences related to citizenship, social contribution or deservingness, but these do not constitute direct affected-migrant policy evidence. Under the Full Corpus standard they cannot be translated into a citizenship-exclusion candidate for this case.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

OPTN states immigration status alone should not determine or exclude transplant candidacy and that citizenship/residency status must not be considered in deceased-donor organ allocation. It recommends addressing psychosocial and financial barriers directly rather than using status as a proxy. The Declaration of Istanbul defines residents by where they make their life rather than citizenship and states residents should have equitable access to donation/transplant services. Professional guidance therefore strongly converges on status-neutral access subject to genuine clinical/feasibility considerations.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Equal moral worth / reciprocity:** long-term residents share health risks, community obligations and donation systems → legal status alone should not exclude them from scarce lifesaving care.
- **Public-resource membership:** some theories hold that publicly financed scarce benefits may legitimately prioritize formal members/citizens → lawful residency can be treated as an entitlement boundary even when clinical benefit is equal.
- **Anti-proxy procedural justice:** legitimate concerns about follow-up and financing should be assessed directly and consistently rather than inferred from a legal category → use status-neutral feasibility criteria.

## 12. References and provenance

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- turning political immigration preferences into transplant-policy evidence;
- treating legal status as a proxy for medication access or follow-up;
- conflating long-term undocumented residents with transplant tourists;
- ignoring donor participation and reciprocity.
