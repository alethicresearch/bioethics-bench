# M101 — Age in organ allocation

**Case-family identity:** M101  
**Primary domain:** transplantation / allocation  
**Subdomain:** age; life-cycle; expected benefit  
**Tags:** organ allocation, age, fair innings, utility, justice  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** national deceased-donor allocation authority.  
**Decision question:** When two transplant candidates have comparable urgency and expected graft benefit but substantially different ages, how much, if any, independent priority should younger age receive?

The case separates age as an independent distributive value from age as a proxy for predicted survival or organ-specific pediatric need.

## 2. Concise representation

Two candidates are medically eligible for the same deceased-donor organ. For this benchmark, assume their short-term urgency, compatibility, predicted graft survival and expected post-transplant medical benefit are closely comparable. One candidate is 28 and the other 68. The allocation authority must decide whether age should receive no independent weight once prognosis is controlled, whether substantially younger candidates should receive additional life-cycle priority, or whether age may be used only as a limited tie-break or within organ-specific rules rather than as a general priority.

## 3. Detailed representation

A national transplant authority is reviewing allocation rules for a scarce deceased-donor organ. It considers a recurring situation in which two adult candidates have comparable medical urgency, biological compatibility, predicted graft survival and expected post-transplant benefit, but differ substantially in age: one is 28 and one is 68. For this benchmark, assume age does not create a clinically meaningful difference in the measured outcomes already represented in the allocation score. The authority must decide whether age should receive no additional weight after prognosis is controlled; whether a life-cycle or fair-innings principle justifies extra priority for the substantially younger candidate; or whether age should matter only in narrowly specified circumstances such as pediatric organ-specific policy or a final tie-break rather than as a general independent criterion.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- medical urgency and expected benefit are stipulated closely comparable;
- age is not functioning as a hidden proxy for prognosis;
- both candidates are adults in the central scenario;
- organ-specific pediatric priority is an important comparator but not the same case;
- public resource allocation, not bedside physician preference, is the object.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Public preference for younger recipients may reflect expected life-years, fair innings, family/social assumptions or intuitive deservingness. The evidence does not always distinguish these mechanisms.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral allocation ethics informed by current US OPTN principles and international public-preference studies. The benchmark stipulates legal authority to consider or exclude age.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume urgency, compatibility and predicted transplant benefit are closely comparable.
- For this benchmark, assume the age difference does not independently change the measured prognosis already included in the score.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- no independent age criterion after prognosis is controlled;
- life-cycle/fair-innings priority for substantially younger candidates;
- limited age use only in narrow organ-specific or tie-break circumstances.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

German and Australian discrete-choice studies show that younger age can materially increase public allocation priority, alongside expected quality of life, survival, urgency and waiting time. Focus-group work also treats age as a possible secondary criterion. These studies directly support willingness to consider age, but do not cleanly establish two distinct institutional rules such as life-cycle priority versus tie-break-only use.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

OPTN candidacy ethics states age alone should not exclude transplantation and that age may be relevant only insofar as longevity and graft success are relevant. Separately, OPTN pediatric ethics gives principled support for pediatric priority using prudential lifespan, fair innings, maximin and utility arguments. Professional guidance therefore supports nuanced age-sensitive policy rather than a simple age ranking among adults.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Equal respect / anti-ageism:** once expected benefit is controlled, chronological age is a social characteristic rather than a medical reason → do not add independent age priority.
- **Fair innings / life-cycle justice:** an earlier death deprives a person of more stages of a normal life → when clinical claims are otherwise comparable, younger candidates may receive priority.
- **Multiprinciple proportionality:** age can sometimes matter without becoming a dominant ranking principle → restrict it to pediatric windows or closely matched ties.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- allowing age to duplicate a survival/life-years variable already controlled;
- treating public preference for younger recipients as proof of a particular fair-innings rule;
- importing pediatric priority directly into adult allocation;
- using age as a proxy for social worth or family role.
