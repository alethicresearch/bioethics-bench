# M112 — Paired kidney exchange and chain obligations

**Case-family identity:** M112  
**Primary domain:** transplantation / living donation  
**Subdomain:** paired exchange; donor withdrawal  
**Tags:** paired kidney exchange, donor withdrawal, reciprocity, coercion, chains  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** paired-exchange program.  
**Decision question:** What commitments may a program ethically require from a living donor once a kidney-exchange chain has begun and other participants have relied on the planned donation?

## 2. Concise representation

A living kidney donor joins a paired-exchange chain so the donor's intended recipient can receive a compatible kidney from another participant. After another transplant in the chain has already occurred but before the donor's surgery, the donor develops serious doubts and wants to withdraw. The program must decide whether the donor retains an unconditional right to withdraw until surgery, whether participation can create a stronger enforceable commitment after others have relied on the chain, or whether the system should preserve withdrawal while designing sequencing and backup plans to minimize harmful reliance.

## 3. Detailed representation

A paired kidney exchange links several incompatible donor–recipient pairs. A competent donor entered voluntarily after counseling and knew their donation would trigger benefits for others. Because procedures could not all be simultaneous, another recipient has already received a kidney in reliance on the chain. Before the donor's own surgery, the donor becomes distressed and asks to withdraw without giving a medical reason. Their intended recipient has already benefited. Withdrawal may break the remaining chain and harm another waiting participant. The program must choose whether the donor's right to withdraw remains absolute until anesthesia/surgery; whether participation after chain initiation creates a stronger commitment that can ethically limit withdrawal; or whether no coercive enforcement is permissible but programs should sequence exchanges, use bridge-donor safeguards and communicate reliance explicitly to reduce the chance and harm of withdrawal.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- donor has capacity and entered voluntarily;
- donor has not yet undergone surgery;
- another participant has already relied on the chain;
- withdrawal can harm remaining participants;
- program design can reduce reliance without coercion.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Moral commitment may increase after others rely on the chain even if legal/ethical consent remains revocable. Social pressure can itself undermine voluntariness.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral paired-exchange ethics; legality of donor withdrawal is assumed consistent with current living-donor consent principles.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume the donor remains medically eligible and has capacity.
- For this benchmark, assume withdrawal would disrupt the remaining chain.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- unconditional withdrawal right until surgery;
- stronger binding commitment after reliance begins;
- preserve withdrawal but design the chain to minimize reliance harms.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Direct donor/recipient evidence comparing these institutional policies is limited. Experiences of obligation or gratitude do not directly support coercive program rules.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

OPTN living-donor guidance states donors may withdraw at any time before surgery and specifically warns paired-exchange programs against pressure not to back out. This supports one clear professional architecture: preserve revocability and reduce reliance through program design rather than coercion.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Bodily autonomy:** no reliance interest can make nontherapeutic organ removal permissible after valid consent is withdrawn → preserve withdrawal.
- **Reliance/fidelity:** voluntarily entering a chain can create serious obligations when others irreversibly act on the promise → moral reasons to continue increase after reliance.
- **Institutional noncoercion:** systems should not solve predictable coordination risk by controlling donors → engineer simultaneous operations, backup plans and transparent sequencing instead.
