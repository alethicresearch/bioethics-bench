# M120 — Global allocation of a curative but ultra-expensive gene therapy

**Case-family identity:** M120  
**Primary domain:** global health / priority setting  
**Subdomain:** high-cost gene therapy; opportunity cost  
**Tags:** gene therapy, global health, opportunity cost, severity, cost-effectiveness, rescue  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** international health-financing mechanism with a fixed marginal budget.  
**Decision question:** Should a fixed global-health budget fund a small number of curative ultra-expensive gene therapies or use the same resources for high-impact population health services?

## 2. Concise representation

An international health fund has a fixed one-year marginal budget. It could finance curative gene therapy for a small number of children with a severe fatal genetic disease, or use the same funds for vaccines, maternal care and treatment programs expected to prevent substantially more deaths and disability across a larger population. The fund must decide whether to maximize total expected health benefit, give special priority to severe rare disease and identifiable patients despite high opportunity cost, or reserve a defined portion of the budget for high-cost cures while allocating the remainder by population health impact.

## 3. Detailed representation

An international financing mechanism receives a fixed additional budget that cannot be expanded during the current cycle. One option is a one-time gene therapy that is expected to cure or substantially transform a severe childhood genetic disease, but the treatment is extremely expensive and only a limited number of children could be treated. The same budget could instead expand high-impact vaccination, maternal/newborn services and treatment for common infectious or chronic diseases, producing much larger aggregate health gains. For this benchmark, assume the gene therapy has strong evidence of clinical benefit and the population-health programs also have established effectiveness. The fund must choose an ordinary cost-effectiveness/total-health rule, a severity/rescue rule that gives extra weight to the small group facing catastrophic disease, or a portfolio rule reserving a limited share for high-cost transformative therapies while allocating most resources according to expected population health gain.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- marginal budget is fixed;
- gene therapy is effective rather than speculative;
- opportunity-cost programs are also effective and scalable;
- rare-disease patients face severe/fatal illness;
- choice is international financing, not an individual clinician decision.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Long-term durability of gene therapy may remain uncertain, and conventional health-gain measures may not capture severity, cure, equity or scientific spillovers. Portfolio rules also require controversial budget shares.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral global priority-setting case grounded in contemporary HTA/high-cost therapy debates, without naming one product price that will become stale.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume both the gene therapy and alternative population programs have credible effectiveness evidence.
- For this benchmark, assume the fixed marginal budget creates real displacement: funding one option reduces the other.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- maximize total expected health gain/cost-effectiveness;
- give special severity/rescue priority to curative treatment of catastrophic rare disease;
- reserve a limited portfolio share for high-cost transformative therapies while applying ordinary impact criteria to the remainder.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Public priority-setting studies often show that severity matters while rarity alone receives weaker support, and rare-disease communities strongly advocate access to transformative cures. These sources do not directly specify an international budget rule or portfolio share and therefore cannot yet support an executable public pool.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

Health technology assessment frameworks increasingly incorporate severity modifiers, managed entry, evidence development and opportunity cost, but no mature global professional body supplies a single allocation rule for the exact global portfolio choice represented here. The policy options are serious but partly constructed from broader priority-setting traditions.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Utilitarian opportunity cost:** every dollar committed to a very high-cost cure displaces other health gains → maximize total expected health benefit.
- **Priority to the worse off / rescue:** catastrophic severe disease can justify extra weight even when fewer total health units are produced → fund some curative rare-disease treatment.
- **Pluralist portfolio justice:** a legitimate health system need not reduce every value to one metric → reserve a transparent bounded share for transformative high-severity interventions while protecting population-health capacity.
