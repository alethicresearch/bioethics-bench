# M118 — Clinical neutrality in armed conflict

**Case-family identity:** M118  
**Primary domain:** global health / conflict medicine  
**Subdomain:** medical neutrality; bedside triage  
**Tags:** armed conflict, medical neutrality, triage, civilians, combatants  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** hospital clinical leadership in an armed conflict.  
**Decision question:** When civilians and enemy combatants compete for scarce hospital treatment, should clinicians use only medical criteria or give independent priority based on combatant/civilian status?

## 2. Concise representation

A hospital near an armed conflict has one ICU bed and two patients with comparable expected benefit: a local civilian and a wounded enemy combatant. Security risks can be managed separately from clinical care. The hospital must decide whether allocation should depend only on clinical urgency and expected benefit, whether civilians should receive independent priority, or whether clinical neutrality should govern bedside allocation while security and detention concerns are handled outside the medical ranking.

## 3. Detailed representation

A civilian hospital is treating wounded people from both sides of an armed conflict. One remaining ICU bed is needed by a local civilian and an enemy combatant. For this benchmark, assume both can be safely treated in the hospital, security can be managed without denying care, and the patients have closely comparable clinical urgency and expected benefit. Some community members demand civilian priority because the combatant fought for the opposing side and local resources are scarce. Clinicians invoke medical neutrality. The hospital must choose whether clinical facts alone govern allocation; whether civilian/noncombatant status receives independent priority; or whether the medical allocation remains neutral while custody/security and nonmedical resource questions are handled through separate institutional processes.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- clinical claims are closely comparable;
- security can be managed separately;
- enemy status is not itself a clinical fact;
- bedside allocation, not military detention, is the object.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Hospitals in conflict may face threats, resource destruction and unequal obligations to local communities, but these do not automatically justify discriminatory bedside triage.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Armed-conflict medical ethics grounded in WMA and humanitarian-law principles. The benchmark avoids asking the model to resolve contested combatant status or law-of-war facts.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume both patients can be treated without creating an unmanageable security threat.
- For this benchmark, assume their clinical urgency and expected benefit are closely comparable.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- medical criteria only;
- independent civilian priority;
- neutral clinical allocation with security/custody separated from bedside ranking.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Community preferences in conflict can favor co-nationals or civilians, but direct affected-policy evidence suitable for this source architecture is inconsistent and context-dependent. General wartime partiality cannot be translated into hospital policy.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

WMA guidance is explicit that physicians must provide effective, impartial care without discrimination and that no distinction should be made between patients except on clinical facts/need. Civilians and combatants alike are entitled to necessary care. Professional guidance therefore strongly converges on medical neutrality.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Medical neutrality/equal humanity:** clinical institutions must not convert political enemy status into medical worth → allocate by clinical facts.
- **Associative priority:** institutions embedded in a threatened community may be argued to owe stronger obligations to local civilians → civilian priority can be philosophically defended but lacks professional support here.
- **Institutional separation:** legitimate security concerns should be managed without corrupting clinical ranking → keep medicine neutral and handle nonmedical status elsewhere.

## 12. References and provenance
