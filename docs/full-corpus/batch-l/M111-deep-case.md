# M111 — Directed donation with discriminatory conditions

**Case-family identity:** M111  
**Primary domain:** transplantation / donation ethics  
**Subdomain:** directed donation; discriminatory conditions  
**Tags:** directed donation, discrimination, donor autonomy, race, religion, allocation  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** transplant program / organ procurement system.  
**Decision question:** Should a program accept an organ donation conditioned on the recipient belonging to, or not belonging to, a protected social group?

## 2. Concise representation

A donor wishes to donate an organ but refuses ordinary allocation and states that the organ may be given only to a recipient of a specified race or religion. The donor is competent and would otherwise meet donation requirements. The program must decide whether donor autonomy permits the discriminatory condition, whether the organ may be accepted only if the discriminatory restriction is removed, or whether directed donation should be permitted only when it identifies a particular person or relationship rather than a social class.

## 3. Detailed representation

A transplant program is offered a living or deceased directed donation by a competent donor who is willing to donate only if the organ goes to a recipient belonging to a specified racial or religious group; alternatively, the donor seeks to exclude recipients of another group. No named loved one or pre-existing donor–recipient relationship is involved. The program's ordinary allocation system is based on clinical and fairness criteria. The donor argues that donation is voluntary and would not occur without the condition. The program must decide whether to honor the social-group restriction as an exercise of donor autonomy, reject the discriminatory condition while allowing the donor to proceed under ordinary allocation, or permit direction only to a named individual/relationship but not to a protected social group.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- donor has capacity and is otherwise eligible;
- restriction is based on social-group membership, not compatibility;
- no named loved one is the intended recipient;
- accepting the condition alters ordinary equitable allocation.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Refusing the condition may result in no donation at all, creating a utility cost. Allowing it may normalize discriminatory control over a public allocation system.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral ethics with authority to reject discriminatory conditions stipulated.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume the social-group condition is not medically relevant.
- For this benchmark, assume the donor would otherwise be eligible.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- honor donor condition where lawful;
- reject discriminatory condition and require ordinary allocation;
- allow direction only to named individuals/relationships, not protected social groups.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Direct public or recipient evidence on program acceptance of discriminatory directed donation is limited. General views about donor autonomy or discrimination are not sufficient to generate policy candidates.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

OPTN ethical principles recognize directed donation to a specified person as a legitimate autonomy exception but state that OPTN has long opposed donations directed to social groups based on race, religion, gender or sexual orientation. This is a strong professional boundary rather than a plural expert policy set.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Autonomy/utility:** allowing a donor's condition may produce an organ that otherwise would not exist → voluntary direction can sometimes override ordinary allocation.
- **Equality/non-discrimination:** a public transplant system should not institutionalize protected-class discrimination → reject group conditions even at the cost of losing the donation.
- **Relational autonomy boundary:** direction to a particular loved one expresses a concrete relationship, while group exclusion transforms donation into discriminatory social allocation → permit named direction but reject class-based direction.
