# M116 — Pharmaceutical IP versus access during a major health need

**Case-family identity:** M116  
**Primary domain:** global health / access  
**Subdomain:** intellectual property; technology sharing; medicines access  
**Tags:** patents, compulsory licensing, medicines access, technology transfer, innovation  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** government/international health authority.  
**Decision question:** When patent exclusivity materially limits access to a highly effective health technology during a major health need, what access policy should public authorities pursue?

## 2. Concise representation

A patented health technology substantially reduces death from a major disease, but supply and price leave most patients in lower-income countries without timely access. Manufacturing capacity could expand if additional producers receive legal rights and technical know-how, although the patent holder argues that exclusivity supports innovation and coordinated quality control. Authorities must decide whether to preserve exclusivity while subsidizing procurement, use public-health licensing and technology-sharing tools to expand production, or negotiate tiered licensing and manufacturing transfer with the originator before using compulsory measures.

## 3. Detailed representation

A highly effective patented vaccine or therapeutic is central to controlling a major health threat. Demand greatly exceeds affordable supply in many lower-resource settings. Existing factories can expand output only gradually, while qualified manufacturers in additional regions could potentially produce the product if they receive legal authorization, detailed know-how and regulatory support. The originator argues that patent exclusivity and licensing control are needed to fund innovation, maintain quality and coordinate scale-up; access advocates argue that public investment and preventable deaths justify stronger sharing obligations. The authority must choose whether to preserve exclusivity and close the access gap through subsidized procurement; invoke or credibly threaten public-health licensing mechanisms together with technology transfer; or pursue negotiated tiered licensing/manufacturing transfer with defined access milestones and use compulsory measures only if the agreement fails.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- access gap is substantial and health consequences are serious;
- patent rights are one constraint but know-how/manufacturing capacity also matter;
- additional qualified manufacturing is feasible with transfer/support;
- public authority has lawful licensing options;
- product quality/regulatory capacity remain necessary.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

The effect of weakened exclusivity on future innovation is uncertain, as is the speed with which technology transfer can expand high-quality production. Patent reform alone may not solve manufacturing bottlenecks.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral global-access case grounded in WTO/TRIPS public-health flexibilities and WHO technology-access/local-production initiatives.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume additional qualified producers could materially expand access if legal rights, know-how and regulatory support are available.
- For this benchmark, assume subsidized procurement alone could improve access but would not fully close the shortage within the relevant period.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- preserve exclusivity with subsidized procurement;
- compulsory/public-health licensing plus technology transfer;
- negotiated tiered licensing/transfer with access milestones and compulsory fallback.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Patients and civil-society groups strongly advocate access and technology sharing in many settings, but direct representative comparisons of these specific IP policy architectures are limited. General support for affordable medicines cannot be converted into a particular licensing mechanism.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

International governance recognizes public-health flexibilities in IP rules and WHO actively supports technology transfer/local production. At the same time, professional and policy institutions use negotiated licensing, procurement, patent pooling and capacity building rather than a single universal compulsory-license rule. These mechanisms often operate together, so they should not be split into artificial expert candidates.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Innovation stewardship:** durable incentives and quality coordination support future health technology development → preserve rights while socializing access costs.
- **Right to health / necessity:** preventable deaths can justify limiting exclusionary property rights when they block feasible production → use licensing and sharing powers.
- **Proportional escalation:** voluntary agreements can protect innovation while setting measurable access duties → negotiate first with credible compulsory fallback.
