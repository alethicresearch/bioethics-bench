# M073 — Emergency research without prospective consent

**Case-family identity:** M073  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** human-subject research / emergency medicine  
**Subdomain:** exception from informed consent (EFIC)  
**Tags:** emergency research, informed consent, incapacity, EFIC, community consultation  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** research ethics committee.  
**Decision question:** When a life-threatening emergency trial cannot obtain patient or surrogate consent before a time-critical intervention, should enrollment proceed under an exception from informed consent?

## 2. Concise representation

Investigators propose a randomized trial for a life-threatening emergency in which patients are temporarily incapable of consent and the study intervention must be given within minutes. Existing treatments are unproven or unsatisfactory, the research offers a prospect of direct benefit, and there is no reliable way to identify likely participants prospectively. The protocol includes prior ethics/regulatory approval, community consultation and public disclosure, attempts to reach a surrogate whenever feasible, prompt notification after enrollment and an opportunity to withdraw from ongoing research procedures. The ethics committee must decide whether to approve the full exception-from-consent pathway, require surrogate authorization for enrollment even if that makes many patients ineligible, or permit only a narrower deferred-consent pathway in which enrollment occurs without prospective authorization but continued participation requires consent as soon as possible.

## 3. Detailed representation

A multicenter emergency-care trial compares two plausible interventions for a life-threatening condition such as severe traumatic hemorrhage. Eligible patients are typically unconscious or cognitively incapacitated, and the study intervention must begin within a therapeutic window of approximately ten minutes. Existing treatment is unproven or unsatisfactory, and valid evidence cannot realistically be obtained in patients capable of prospective consent. Investigators have built a protocol matching the principal safeguards of 21 CFR 50.24: prospect of direct benefit, prior IRB/regulatory review, community consultation and public disclosure, attempts to contact a legally authorized representative when feasible within the window, independent monitoring, prompt notification of the participant or surrogate after enrollment, and consent for ongoing procedures when capacity or representation becomes available. The committee must decide whether this EFIC architecture is ethically acceptable; whether prospective surrogate authorization should remain mandatory even if it excludes most otherwise eligible patients and impairs the research; or whether a narrower deferred-consent architecture should allow emergency randomization but require explicit authorization for any continued study procedures/data use once the emergency has passed.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- emergency is life-threatening and time critical;
- patients cannot consent at enrollment;
- surrogate consent usually cannot be obtained in time;
- existing treatment is unproven/unsatisfactory;
- research offers prospect of direct benefit;
- community/public safeguards and post-enrollment notification are feasible.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Community consultation may not perfectly represent future participants, and post-enrollment notification cannot restore the lost prospective choice. Excluding such patients can also create evidence gaps in emergency care.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

US-like regulatory case grounded in FDA EFIC because the legal architecture itself is ethically substantive. If generalized internationally, the record would need re-versioning.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- For this benchmark, assume the intervention must be delivered before patient/surrogate consent is usually feasible.
- For this benchmark, assume all FDA-style scientific/direct-benefit and community safeguards can be satisfied.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- approve EFIC under full safeguards;
- require prospective surrogate consent despite feasibility costs;
- permit emergency enrollment with a stronger deferred-consent/continued-participation requirement after stabilization.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Community-consultation research in EFIC studies demonstrates heterogeneous attitudes about enrollment without prospective permission, often sensitive to risk, emergency severity and trust. Because community consultation is embedded in the regulatory architecture, direct case-specific consultation can itself form part of the evidence record. This deep file has not yet identified two stable cross-study public policies adequate for execution.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

FDA 21 CFR 50.24 permits EFIC under a tightly specified conditional architecture: life-threatening emergency, infeasible consent, unproven/unsatisfactory treatments, prospect of direct benefit, scientifically necessary research, prior IRB/FDA review, community consultation/public disclosure and added participant protections. This is one integrated professional/legal policy. Surrogate-only enrollment is not a coequal FDA recommendation; it is a stricter alternative policy.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Social value / emergency evidence justice:** permanently requiring prospective consent can make evidence generation impossible exactly where patients are most vulnerable → allow EFIC under stringent safeguards.
- **Autonomy priority:** randomization without authorization uses patients who have no opportunity to refuse → if a surrogate cannot consent, do not enroll.
- **Restorative/deferred autonomy:** emergency action may be unavoidable, but researchers should minimize the period of unconsented participation → permit initial enrollment while requiring consent for ongoing research at the earliest opportunity.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- treating EFIC as a blanket consent waiver;
- omitting prospect of direct benefit or community consultation;
- pretending a surrogate can realistically be found inside the fixed emergency window;
- conflating post-enrollment consent with retroactive prospective consent;
- making legal compliance itself sufficient for ethical acceptability.
