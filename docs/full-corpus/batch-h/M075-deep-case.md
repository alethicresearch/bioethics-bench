# M075 — Broad consent for future biobank research

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** institutional biobank governance board.  
**Decision question:** What consent model should a biobank use when storing identifiable biospecimens and data for future studies that cannot be fully specified at collection?

## Concise representation

A hospital plans a longitudinal biobank linking donated blood/tissue with identifiable clinical data. Future studies will span several disease areas and some questions cannot yet be specified. Donors can withdraw future use, a governance board and research ethics committee review every secondary-use proposal, and uses outside the consented scope can trigger recontact. The biobank must decide whether to use bounded broad consent for a defined range of future research, require dynamic/recontact consent for materially new research categories, or obtain specific consent for each future study while using opt-out only for low-risk residual clinical materials under separate rules.

## Detailed representation

An academic health system is establishing a biobank of identifiable biospecimens and longitudinal health data. The collection is prospective: patients are being asked to donate material specifically for future research rather than merely leaving residual tissue after clinical care. The institution can explain the types of research likely to occur, storage duration, access rules, commercial collaboration possibilities, data linkage, governance, withdrawal, handling of unsolicited findings and circumstances requiring recontact. A research ethics committee will review each future project and cannot authorize uses outside the agreed scope without additional permission or another lawful ethical basis. The biobank must choose whether one bounded broad-consent process should authorize a defined range of future research under governance; whether donors should be recontacted dynamically whenever a materially new research category or sensitive use arises; or whether each identifiable future study should require specific consent, reserving opt-out approaches for separately governed residual clinical specimens.

## Decision-critical facts

- samples/data are prospectively collected for a biobank;
- future studies cannot all be specified;
- materials/data remain identifiable or re-identifiable;
- governance and REC review are robust;
- broad consent can specify scope and exclusions;
- recontact is feasible for some but not all future uses.

## Explicit uncertainty

Future research categories and social sensitivities cannot be fully predicted. Dynamic consent can increase control but also impose recontact burden and selection bias; broad consent can be administratively durable but may become too remote from future uses.

## Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral governance grounded in CIOMS Guideline 11 and Common Rule broad-consent concepts, without making US regulatory mechanics the answer.

## Serious policy / position families

- bounded broad consent plus governance;
- dynamic/recontact consent for materially new categories;
- specific consent for each identifiable future study, with separate opt-out rules only for residual material.

## Public / affected-community evidence

Biobank participants commonly accept broad consent when governance, transparency and withdrawal are credible, while other studies show meaningful preferences for greater control over sensitive or novel uses. This deep file needs a targeted empirical candidate audit before claiming two public policy rules; preference for “more control” does not by itself specify a recontact threshold.

## Expert / professional recommendations

CIOMS Guideline 11 explicitly permits broad informed consent but stresses that it is **not blanket consent**. Broad consent should specify scope, storage, access, foreseeable uses, commercialization, unsolicited findings and governance; future uses outside the authorized scope require re-consent. HHS also recognizes broad consent for future secondary use under defined conditions. This is one mature professional architecture. Dynamic consent is a governance alternative in scholarship/practice, not a universal CIOMS requirement.

## Normative / framework positions

- **Stewardship/trust:** future research can remain respectful when donors authorize a bounded domain and accountable institutions police that boundary → broad consent plus governance.
- **Ongoing autonomy:** new technologies and sensitive uses can change what participants reasonably understand themselves to have authorized → recontact for materially new categories.
- **Specific authorization:** identifiable biospecimens can reveal intimate information across time → consent should track each study rather than institutional promises alone → study-specific consent.

## Key sources

## Construction risks

Do not call broad consent blanket consent; do not conflate prospectively donated biobank samples with residual clinical specimens; do not imply recontact is always feasible; specify what counts as outside scope.
