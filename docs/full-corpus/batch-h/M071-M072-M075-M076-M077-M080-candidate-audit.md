# Batch H candidate audit — M071, M072, M075, M076, M077, M080

## M071 — Placebo with established effective treatment

**Result: `needs-additional-evidence`.** Helsinki and CIOMS provide a narrow conditional permission boundary, but direct participant/public evidence does not yet support two institution-level policies. The apparent expert difference between “no serious/irreversible harm” and CIOMS “no more than a minor increase above minimal risk” is important for scholarship but should not yet be turned into two candidates without confirming that it changes the decision in the fixed scenario. Rescue/short duration are mitigation conditions inside the permissive rule, not separate candidates.

## M072 — Controlled human infection study

**Result: `needs-additional-evidence`.** WHO supplies one integrated conditional approval architecture. Availability of curative rescue is a risk-reduction factor, not an absolute WHO requirement, so “WHO approval” versus “rescue required” cannot be coded as two expert candidates. Public/volunteer willingness to participate is not yet a direct policy preference about the ethics-committee risk ceiling.

## M075 — Broad consent for biobank research

### Public candidates

**pub1 — broad consent with accountable governance**  
`Ask donors to make an active initial choice and, if they agree, authorize a defined broad range of future biobank research under independent governance without study-by-study recontact.`

**Support:** representative US genomic-research survey of 4,659 adults found broad consent preferred by 52% versus study-by-study consent by 48%; another mixed focus-group/survey study found broad research-unspecific consent preferred over categorical/study-specific models after an active opt-in decision. Large eMERGE survey found substantial willingness under broad consent with controlled sharing. This is direct consent-model evidence.

**pub2 — study-by-study / renewed consent**  
`Recontact donors for permission before each materially distinct future study or use of identifiable biospecimens rather than relying on one broad authorization for the life of the biobank.`

**Support:** the same representative survey found 48% preferred study-by-study consent; national work on reservations about broad versus study-by-study consent documents substantial minorities who regard broad consent as unacceptable, especially when future morally sensitive uses accumulate. Dynamic-consent studies show donor preferences can change over time.

**Distinctness:** pass. The policies differ for future uses that remain inside an initial broad category but were not individually described.

### Expert candidate

**exp1 — bounded broad consent + governance**  
`Use broad informed consent that specifies the permitted range of future research, storage/access/governance rules, withdrawal and sensitive-use conditions; require ethics review for secondary use and re-consent when a proposed use falls outside the authorized scope.`

**Support:** CIOMS Guideline 11 directly. HHS broad-consent architecture is supporting context. One professional rule; dynamic consent is not a universal professional requirement.

### Framework candidates

**fw1 — stewardship/trust**  
`Permit bounded broad consent because participants can authorize trusted institutional stewardship over a defined research domain without repeatedly deciding each scientifically similar use.`

**fw2 — ongoing autonomy**  
`Require renewed permission for materially distinct future uses because changing technologies and moral implications can make an old general authorization an unreliable proxy for present preferences.`

**Judgment:** **provisional `full-corpus-2x1x2-mean-v1`.** Strong executable candidate. Public evidence directly compares consent models, expert guidance supplies one mature broad-consent architecture, framework rules are action-distinct.

## M076 — Payment to healthy volunteers

**Result: `needs-additional-evidence`.** Empirical research shows high acceptance of paying healthy volunteers and wide participant variation in what counts as appropriate compensation; CIOMS/FDA/SACHRP favor fair compensation with IRB scrutiny and reject conflating payment with coercion or risk benefit. But participant evidence does not directly produce “uncapped fair payment” versus “conservative cap” institutional policies. A cap candidate would currently be more inherited practice/theoretical concern than source-grounded participant policy.

---

## M077 — Pediatric no-direct-benefit research

**Result: `needs-additional-evidence`.** CIOMS explicitly permits a minor increase above minimal risk under compelling social-value/necessity conditions, so “minimal risk only” is not the complete professional rule. Parent/child evidence about willingness by procedure burden is relevant but not yet sufficient to construct two research-policy candidates. Do not split “minor increase” and “remove procedure” unless participant evidence supplies those action rules.

## M080 — Post-trial access

### Public / participant candidates

**pub1 — meaningful continued access**  
`Ensure participants who benefited and still medically need the intervention can continue receiving it after the trial until a sustainable clinical source or therapeutic equivalent is available.`

**Support:** EPIC focus groups with current/recent trial participants found many believed researchers, sponsors, insurers and others share obligations to facilitate post-trial access to a beneficial study drug or equivalent. Some specifically included transition care and short-term drug supply.

**pub2 — no indefinite special treatment entitlement**  
`Do not guarantee indefinite sponsor-funded post-trial treatment; provide trial information and a responsible transition plan, but allow ordinary clinical access rules to govern after the study's defined transition period.`

**Support:** EPIC also found participants who believed there were no post-trial drug/care obligations, alongside broad agreement about information obligations. The transition language is **Bench-authored policy completion** that operationalizes the no-indefinite-obligation orientation without creating abrupt abandonment inconsistent with current professional guidance. Provenance must say so explicitly.

**Distinctness:** pass. They differ over whether continuing medically needed treatment remains a special obligation after the transition period.

### Expert candidate

**exp1 — advance meaningful post-trial provision with REC-reviewed exceptions**  
`Before enrollment, arrange and disclose post-trial provision for participants who still need an intervention shown beneficial and reasonably safe, specifying duration, financing, delivery and transition; any exception requires research-ethics review.`

**Support:** 2024 Declaration of Helsinki paragraph 34 and CIOMS Guideline 6. One professional architecture.

### Framework candidates

**fw1 — reciprocity**  
`Continue access for participants who still medically need the beneficial intervention because they accepted research burdens that helped generate the evidence and should not be made worse off when the study ends.`

**fw2 — proportional transition responsibility**  
`Require the research enterprise to bridge participants to sustainable clinical care but not to assume an unlimited lifetime treatment obligation when a fair transition can be arranged.`

**Judgment:** **provisional `full-corpus-2x1x2-mean-v1`.** Strong executable candidate. The public layer is unusually direct about post-trial obligations, while expert guidance converges on advance meaningful provision.
