# M080 — Post-trial access to a beneficial intervention

**Case-family identity:** M080  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** human-subject research / post-trial obligations  
**Subdomain:** continued access; transition of care  
**Tags:** post-trial access, clinical trials, sponsor obligations, continued treatment, justice  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** trial sponsor and research ethics committee.  
**Decision question:** What post-trial access arrangement should be required when participants still need an intervention shown beneficial and reasonably safe during the trial but ordinary access is uncertain?

## Concise representation

A randomized trial of a chronic-disease therapy is ending. Some participants receiving the investigational drug have had substantial clinical benefit and their treating investigators believe abrupt discontinuation would likely worsen their condition. The intervention has been shown beneficial and reasonably safe in the trial, but regulatory approval and routine insurance or public-system access may take many months. The sponsor can continue supplying the drug, but indefinite access would create cost and operational burdens. The ethics committee must decide whether the sponsor should guarantee continued access while participants medically need the intervention, provide a defined transition period until another payer or health system can take responsibility, or use a pre-agreed post-trial plan that can limit sponsor access when a research ethics committee approves a justified exception.

## Detailed representation

A multicenter clinical trial for a serious chronic condition has demonstrated that the investigational intervention is beneficial and reasonably safe. Several participants assigned to the intervention have improved substantially and remain clinically dependent on continued treatment. The trial is ending, but full regulatory approval and routine reimbursement are expected to take approximately nine to twelve months. Without a post-trial arrangement, participants may lose access despite having accepted research burdens and helped generate the evidence supporting the product. The sponsor can supply the drug during a transition period, though indefinite lifetime provision would be financially and logistically difficult. Before enrollment, the consent materials disclosed that post-trial arrangements would be determined under an ethics-approved plan. The sponsor and committee must decide whether participants who still need the intervention should receive continued sponsor-funded access for as long as medically necessary; receive a defined transition supply while the sponsor, health system and payer arrange ordinary access; or receive access according to a pre-agreed fair-benefit plan that permits a research ethics committee to approve exceptions where indefinite continued supply is disproportionate or infeasible.

## Decision-critical facts

- intervention is shown beneficial and reasonably safe;
- specific participants still medically need it;
- abrupt discontinuation is likely to worsen health;
- ordinary access will be delayed;
- sponsor can provide at least temporary continued supply;
- post-trial planning was disclosed prospectively.

## Explicit uncertainty

Regulatory and payer timelines can change. Lifetime sponsor obligation may be disproportionate, but too-short transition can make participants bear the cost of research-system delay.

## Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral research ethics grounded in the 2024 Declaration of Helsinki and CIOMS Guideline 6.

## Serious policy / position families

- continued access while medically needed;
- time-limited sponsor-funded transition to ordinary health-system access;
- pre-agreed proportional access plan with REC-approved exceptions.

## Public / affected-community evidence

The EPIC study of 93 current/recent US clinical-trial participants found genuine pluralism. Many participants believed researchers, sponsors, insurers and others share obligations to facilitate post-trial access to a beneficial study drug or therapeutic equivalent; some emphasized transition care or short-term supply; others believed there was no post-trial drug/care obligation. Participants broadly agreed on obligations to provide information about the study and treatment. This is unusually direct participant evidence about post-trial obligation structure.

## Expert / professional recommendations

The 2024 Declaration of Helsinki requires sponsors and researchers to arrange post-trial provisions in advance for participants who still need an intervention shown beneficial and reasonably safe; REC-approved exceptions are allowed and the arrangement must be disclosed in consent. CIOMS Guideline 6 similarly requires advance planning of level, scope, duration, financing, delivery and monitoring of post-trial care/access. These sources converge on **some meaningful post-trial provision**, while leaving duration and responsibility context-sensitive.

## Normative / framework positions

- **Reciprocity:** participants accepted burdens that produced socially valuable evidence → those who benefit and still need treatment deserve continued access rather than abrupt abandonment.
- **Transition responsibility:** research creates a special but not unlimited obligation → sponsor responsibility should bridge participants to sustainable clinical access rather than become an indefinite substitute health system.
- **Proportional fair-benefit planning:** obligations depend on medical need, feasibility, trial context and host-system arrangements → specify a fair plan in advance and allow REC-reviewed exceptions.

## Construction risks

Do not create a “no obligation” expert candidate from participant dissent; distinguish sponsor obligation from health-system/government responsibility; specify medical need and demonstrated benefit; avoid assuming indefinite lifetime provision is always feasible.

## Executable-eligibility judgment

Provisional `executable-other-profile`, likely `2 public × 1 expert × 2 framework`, subject to candidate audit. The participant layer directly supports continued-access versus limited/no special-obligation orientations, while professional guidance converges on advance meaningful provision.
