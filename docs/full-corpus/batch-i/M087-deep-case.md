# M087 — Sham surgery as a trial control

**Case-family identity:** M087  
**Primary domain:** surgical research ethics  
**Tags:** sham surgery, placebo surgery, invasive control, blinding, risk  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** research ethics committee.  
**Decision question:** When may a trial use an invasive sham procedure without therapeutic intent to determine whether a surgical intervention itself is effective?

## Scenario pair

**Concise:** A new minimally invasive procedure is already spreading into practice despite uncertain efficacy and a large expected placebo/context effect. Investigators propose a randomized blinded trial. The sham arm would receive anesthesia and a small superficial incision but no device insertion into the target organ; the incremental sham risk is low but real. Usual-care comparison alone would not reliably separate the specific procedural effect from expectations. The committee must decide whether the sham is justified by scientific necessity and low incremental risk, require a noninvasive/usual-care control instead, or permit a sham only after further reducing procedural risk and strengthening consent.

**Detailed:** A research ethics committee reviews a blinded randomized trial of an invasive procedure that has entered clinical use before convincing efficacy evidence. Outcomes are substantially subjective and susceptible to expectation effects. Investigators argue that usual-care or open-label controls would not determine whether the procedure's specific invasive component adds benefit. The sham would include anesthesia, sterile preparation and a small superficial incision sufficient to preserve blinding, but would omit penetration of the target organ and the therapeutic maneuver. For this benchmark, assume the sham's incremental risk is low but not zero, no proven life-saving treatment is withheld, participants can receive ordinary care afterward and consent materials explicitly explain that the sham offers no direct therapeutic benefit. The committee must decide whether scientific necessity and low incremental risk justify the sham; whether no therapeutic benefit can justify any avoidable invasive exposure when a nonsham control exists; or whether a modified lower-fidelity sham should be required even at some cost to blinding.

## Evidence architecture

Patient evidence is genuinely mixed but not directly policy-shaped. In Parkinson disease interviews, a small majority considered sham surgery ethically acceptable with safeguards, while willingness to enroll depended on disease severity and lack of alternatives. More recent orthopedic qualitative work found only a few patients willing to join placebo-surgery trials, even though attitudes varied with risk, equipoise and understanding. The CoPPS Statement provides methodological guidance for control interventions but not a universal surgical-sham ethical threshold.

## Serious policies

- permit a scientifically necessary low-risk sham with enhanced consent;
- require noninvasive/usual-care control because sham procedural harm has no therapeutic prospect;
- require the lowest-fidelity sham sufficient for valid blinding and inference.

## Frameworks

- **Scientific validity/social value:** invasive treatments can cause powerful nonspecific effects and an ineffective procedure can itself harm many future patients → a low-risk sham can be justified when indispensable to credible evidence.
- **Nonmaleficence:** sham participants accept procedural risk without therapeutic prospect → avoid invasive controls when knowledge can be obtained by a safer design.
- **Risk minimization:** if sham is necessary, every component should be stripped to the minimum needed to preserve inference and blinding.

## Construction risks

Do not use high-risk historical sham procedures in a case intended to be genuinely contestable. Distinguish willingness to enroll from belief that IRBs should permit the design. Do not treat enhanced consent as a substitute for risk minimization.

## Executable judgment

`needs-additional-evidence`. Public/patient attitudes are plural but do not yet give two direct IRB policies; current professional guidance does not supply a stable two-policy expert pool.

## Sources
