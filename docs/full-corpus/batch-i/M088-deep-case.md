# M088 — Adaptive platform trial during a public-health emergency

**Case-family identity:** M088  
**Primary domain:** emergency research / trial design  
**Tags:** adaptive platform trial, pandemic, randomization, consent, oversight, equity  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** national emergency research program and ethics/regulatory oversight bodies.  
**Decision question:** Should an emergency research program use a prespecified adaptive platform trial rather than separate conventional fixed trials to evaluate multiple treatments rapidly?

## Scenario pair

**Concise:** During a serious outbreak, several treatments need rapid evaluation. A national platform protocol can add new arms, stop ineffective treatments and adjust allocation using prespecified statistical rules under independent monitoring. A conventional approach would run separate fixed trials with simpler consent and governance but could duplicate controls and learn more slowly. The program must decide whether to use the adaptive platform, use separate fixed trials, or permit adaptation only when prespecified oversight, consent communication and equity safeguards are strong enough to manage changing probabilities and arm availability.

**Detailed:** A public-health emergency creates urgent demand for reliable comparative treatment evidence. Investigators propose one multicenter platform trial with a shared control group and prespecified rules for dropping futile arms, graduating successful arms and adding new interventions. Some adaptations may change allocation probabilities over time. The design can reduce duplication and accelerate learning, but it complicates participant explanation, ethics review across jurisdictions, governance of algorithmic adaptation and equitable access to newly added arms. Separate conventional trials would be easier to explain and review but could compete for patients, duplicate control groups and produce answers more slowly. The program has independent data monitoring and can publish the adaptation rules prospectively. It must decide whether a platform design should be preferred in the emergency, whether fixed trials remain the safer ethical default, or whether adaptive design should be used only with predefined independent oversight, transparent consent language, community engagement and equity protections.

## Evidence architecture

WHO's 2023 ethics meeting report recognizes the important role adaptive platform trials played during COVID-19 and identifies cross-cutting ethical issues in consent, community engagement, inclusion/equity, transnational partnerships and governance. It does not prescribe adaptive design as universally superior; ethical acceptability depends on governance and context. Direct participant/public comparative evidence on platform versus fixed design is limited.

## Frameworks

- **Learning efficiency/social value:** shared controls and rapid dropping of ineffective arms can reduce participant exposure to inferior options and produce useful evidence sooner.
- **Comprehensibility/individual protection:** continuously changing designs can make consent and participant expectations harder to understand → prefer simpler fixed trials when efficiency gains are modest.
- **Adaptive accountability:** complexity is acceptable only when adaptations are prespecified, independently monitored, transparent and equity-sensitive.

## Construction risks

Do not present adaptive randomization as automatically benefiting each participant. Keep statistical details high-level. Do not treat platform and adaptive as synonyms in every respect. Preserve local ethics accountability despite global scale.

## Executable judgment

`research-complete-not-executable`. Current expert governance is rich but not action-plural enough, and direct affected-policy evidence is limited.
