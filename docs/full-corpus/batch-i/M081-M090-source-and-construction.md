# Bioethics Bench — Batch I source and construction packet (M081–M090)

**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27  
**Scope:** public reporting of null trials, cluster-randomized trials, return of individual research findings, sponsor publication control, first-in-human cell/gene therapy, scarce investigational therapy, sham surgery, adaptive platform trials, national research-priority setting, and Indigenous genomic governance.

## M081 — Publication of negative and inconclusive trial results

**Decision object:** institutional/sponsor policy for timely public reporting of completed human research whose findings are negative, null, inconclusive or commercially unfavorable.

**Professional evidence:** Declaration of Helsinki 2024 ¶36 requires negative and inconclusive as well as positive results to be published or otherwise made publicly available, with timeliness/completeness/accuracy. CIOMS Guideline 24 likewise requires timely public availability and says sponsors must not prevent publication of unwelcome findings. WHO/funder joint statements require prospective registration and timely results disclosure.

**Participant evidence:** trial participants strongly value receiving aggregate trial results; oncology participants report a right to know results even when negative. This supports transparency but is not directly a policy about journal publication versus registry summary.

**Likely policies:** timely public registry/result disclosure for all completed trials plus publication when appropriate; fuller journal publication obligations for all scientifically interpretable results; limited discretion over journal manuscript detail while never suppressing registry/public results. A “do not publish negative trials” candidate is not serious.

## M082 — Cluster-randomized public-health trial and community authorization

**Decision object:** combination of individual consent, gatekeeper permission and community engagement when a school/village/clinic-level intervention is assigned by cluster.

**Professional evidence:** Ottawa Statement says individual informed consent is required when participants are individually identifiable research participants unless a REC grants a waiver/alteration under applicable criteria; gatekeepers cannot give proxy consent for individuals. Gatekeeper permission can legitimately authorize cluster participation where cluster interests are substantially affected, and cluster consultation is recommended. Waivers are appropriate only where the research would be infeasible without them and intervention/data procedures meet low-risk criteria.

**Likely policies:** layered individual consent + gatekeeper/community governance; waiver/opt-out for low-risk cluster interventions when individual consent is infeasible; individual consent wherever feasible. These may be conditions inside one professional architecture rather than independent rules.

## M083 — Return of individual research findings

**Decision object:** when a research sequencing study finds a valid, potentially actionable result unrelated to the main study question, what should investigators offer the participant?

**Professional evidence:** National Academies 2018 recommends study-specific plans; urgent clinically actionable results can create an obligation to return, while uninterpretable or invalid results should not be returned. Validity, participant value/preferences and feasibility matter. NIH in 2026 is actively developing broader return-of-results policy and frames return as a transparency/trust/partnership goal.

**Participant/public evidence:** systematic review of 221 studies/118,874 stakeholders found overwhelming participant/public interest in return, especially clinically actionable/reliable results. Jackson/Framingham cohorts: 88–92% wanted secondary genomic results across vignettes. Project Baseline participants showed heterogeneity and a small opt-out minority; many wanted all data while others prioritized actionable results.

**Likely policies:** offer confirmed clinically actionable findings with informed choice; offer broader valid findings chosen by participants; restrict return to results meeting validity/actionability thresholds. A no-return policy for actionable findings is weak.

## M084 — Sponsor control over publication

**Decision object:** how much prepublication review/delay a commercial sponsor may contractually require while protecting IP/confidentiality.

**Professional evidence:** CIOMS Guideline 24 states sponsors must not prevent publication of unwelcome findings and researchers should not enter agreements unduly interfering with data access, independent analysis, manuscript preparation or publication. ICMJE requires authors to have access to supporting data and disclosure of sponsor restrictions. Academic-center survey found near-consensus against sponsor veto/revision power but broad acceptance of limited prepublication review (often 60–90 days) and additional patent delay. Germany's 2025 standard contractual clauses similarly allow sponsor first-publication/review rights with defined time limits while preserving statutory publication obligations.

**Likely policies:** investigator publication freedom with limited sponsor review for confidential/IP material; fixed short delay for patent/coordination; broader sponsor control is not professionally defensible.

## M085 — First-in-human high-risk gene/cell therapy

**Decision object:** whether an ethics committee should authorize a first-in-human cell/gene intervention for a severe disease when preclinical evidence is promising but irreversible/long-lived harms remain uncertain.

**Professional evidence:** ISSCR 2025 guidelines require compelling scientific rationale, systematic preclinical appraisal, rigorous manufacturing, risk minimization, independent review, appropriate subject selection, early-phase consent designed to counter therapeutic misconception and long-term monitoring where products persist. Trial initiation should be justified by unmet need and credible preclinical evidence, not mere technical ability.

**Likely policies:** proceed in severely affected/no-alternative adults under enhanced oversight and staged dosing; require stronger preclinical evidence before human exposure; restrict first exposure to narrow highest-need populations. These may be threshold variants within one professional architecture.

## M086 — Scarce trial slots versus expanded access

**Decision object:** allocation when an investigational therapy has too little supply to both fill a randomized efficacy trial and satisfy urgent expanded-access requests.

**Professional/regulatory evidence:** FDA says investigational products should be used in clinical trials whenever possible; expanded access is for serious/life-threatening disease when trial participation is not possible and no satisfactory alternative exists. Expanded access must not interfere with clinical trials, including enrollment or drug supply; FDA may impose a clinical hold when insufficient product exists for both controlled trials and non-controlled access. Manufacturers are not required to provide expanded access and sometimes use lotteries/case-by-case rules.

**Likely policies:** prioritize trial supply to generate reliable evidence; reserve a transparent fraction for expanded access to otherwise ineligible worst-off patients; lottery/independent allocation within any expanded-access pool. Need direct patient/public allocation evidence.

## M087 — Sham surgery as a trial control

**Decision object:** whether a randomized trial may expose participants to anesthesia/incision or another invasive sham procedure without therapeutic intent to establish whether an invasive intervention works.

**Professional/evidence base:** sham controls can be methodologically powerful but expose participants to real procedural risk. CoPPS 2023 gives design/reporting standards for control interventions. Patient studies in Parkinson disease and orthopedic surgery find some participants judge sham surgery ethically acceptable under conditions, but personal willingness is lower and strongly depends on disease severity, equipoise, absence of alternatives and sham risk.

**Likely policies:** allow sham only when scientifically necessary and incremental risk is low; require active/usual-care controls when sham adds more than minimal/low risk; allow with enhanced consent and risk mitigation. Need a current professional ethics anchor beyond general placebo rules before execution.

## M088 — Adaptive platform trial during a public-health emergency

**Decision object:** whether an emergency research program should use adaptive platform design (adding/dropping arms, response-adaptive features) rather than conventional separate fixed trials.

**Professional evidence:** WHO 2023 meeting report recognizes important advantages during COVID-19 but identifies ethical issues in consent, community engagement, equity, international partnerships and oversight. Governance must remain locally/globally accountable; adaptations should be prespecified and independently overseen.

**Likely policies:** adaptive platform with prespecified independent oversight; conventional fixed trials; adaptive only when emergency urgency/learning advantages justify complexity. Direct participant/public comparative evidence is limited.

## M089 — Setting a national health-research agenda

**Decision object:** how a public research funder should allocate a fixed national health-research budget across high-burden diseases, neglected/worst-off populations, near-term implementation and higher-risk transformative science.

**Professional/governance evidence:** WHO 2025 guidance explicitly frames research priority setting as distributive ethics because choosing one research investment displaces others and allocates future health benefits. WHO recommends transparent value judgments, broad stakeholder involvement, public-health need, equity/inclusion, explicit methodology, implementation and reevaluation. WHO's 2020 PIPE process similarly emphasizes transparent objectives, stakeholder consultation, prioritization and monitoring.

**Likely policies:** expected population health gain/burden; priority to neglected/worst-off populations; participatory multiprinciple portfolio with reserved exploratory funding. WHO process guidance does not endorse a single substantive priority metric.

## M090 — Community governance of genomic research

**Decision object:** when genomic data/specimens concern a sovereign Indigenous community, what authority should community governance retain over secondary use, sharing and future research beyond individual consent?

**Affected/community evidence:** 2023 US community-engaged interviews with 42 Tribal leaders, clinicians, researchers, policy makers and Tribal review-board members emphasize Indigenous governance, sovereignty, research approvals, agreements, ownership, new consent for future studies, data return and community control. Oklahoma Tribal genomic partnerships require explicit specification of disease/use, secondary uses, storage/security and separate agreements/IRB review for new researchers.

**Governance/framework evidence:** CARE Principles—Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics—were developed through Indigenous data-sovereignty networks and assert that Indigenous Peoples should determine governance protocols and participate in stewardship decisions. SING Consortium framework calls for tribal oversight/consultation, collaboration, transparency, capacity and benefit.

**Likely policies:** layered individual consent + Tribal/community governance for secondary use; community approval/control for specified uses with individual consent still required where applicable; individual consent alone is not supported by current Indigenous governance sources. Executability may therefore fail because the affected layer converges strongly on collective authority rather than splitting.

## Batch-I working hypothesis

Strongest audit targets: **M083, M086, M087, M089, M090**. M081/M084 are likely research-complete cases with strong publication-accountability consensus; M082 is likely one layered cluster-trial architecture; M085/M088 are current governance cases with strong conditional expert rules but limited affected-policy evidence.
