# M086 — Scarce trial slots versus expanded access

**Case-family identity:** M086  
**Primary domain:** research ethics / investigational access  
**Tags:** expanded access, clinical trial, scarce drug, compassionate use, allocation, evidence generation  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** investigational-therapy manufacturer with independent allocation oversight.  
**Decision question:** When an investigational therapy is too scarce to supply both an efficacy trial and all expanded-access requests, how should available doses be divided?

## Scenario pair

**Concise:** A promising investigational therapy for a rapidly fatal rare disease has enough manufactured doses for a randomized trial but little surplus. Trial enrollment is essential to establish whether the therapy works and could support future approval. Patients who are ineligible for the trial because of age or disease stage request expanded access and have no satisfactory alternatives. Supplying many expanded-access patients would delay or shrink the trial. The manufacturer must decide whether trial enrollment receives priority, whether a defined portion of supply should be reserved for expanded access to otherwise ineligible patients, or whether all nontrial supply should be allocated through transparent independent criteria or lottery without compromising the minimum supply needed for the trial.

**Detailed:** A biotechnology company has an investigational product for a rare, rapidly progressive and ultimately fatal disorder. Early evidence is promising but efficacy remains uncertain, making a controlled trial scientifically important for regulatory approval and future population access. Manufacturing is the binding constraint: enough doses exist to run the planned randomized trial, but broad expanded access would materially reduce enrollment speed and may leave the study unable to answer its primary question. Several patients who cannot enroll because of prespecified age, comorbidity or disease-stage criteria have no satisfactory approved therapy and request the drug through expanded access. FDA criteria could permit some such use if risk-benefit is favorable, but expanded access must not interfere with the controlled investigation. The company must decide whether all scarce supply above manufacturing reserve should first protect the trial; whether a transparent percentage should be reserved for otherwise ineligible expanded-access patients while preserving trial viability; or whether the trial receives a defined minimum supply and remaining doses are allocated independently by urgency/lottery rather than case-by-case advocacy.

## Evidence architecture

FDA states that investigational products should be used in clinical trials whenever possible; expanded access is appropriate for serious/life-threatening disease when trial enrollment is not possible and no satisfactory alternative exists, but access must not interfere with clinical trials or development. FDA clinical-hold criteria explicitly recognize inadequate supply for both controlled trials and non-controlled treatment as a reason to protect the trial. Manufacturers are not legally required to provide expanded access and may use lotteries or case-by-case allocation.

Direct patient/public evidence comparing “trial first,” “split supply,” and “expanded-access priority” is limited. Desperate-patient advocacy demonstrates demand for access but does not itself establish a distributive rule.

## Serious policies

- protect trial supply as first priority because reliable evidence is necessary for future access;
- reserve a fixed transparent fraction for expanded access while maintaining a viable trial;
- define a minimum trial supply and independently allocate remaining scarce doses by urgency/lottery.

## Frameworks

- **Social value/evidence generation:** consuming scarce product outside the trial can delay knowledge needed for all future patients → protect trial viability.
- **Rescue/worst-off:** people excluded from trials can face death without any alternative and should not be categorically sacrificed to future knowledge → reserve access.
- **Fair procedural compromise:** neither loud advocacy nor investigator discretion should control scarcity → establish transparent trial minimums and independent rules for residual supply.

## Construction risks

Do not imply expanded access is proven therapy. Distinguish ineligibility from unwillingness to enroll. Do not make lottery a substitute for protecting the minimum trial supply. Do not infer an allocation preference from patient desire for treatment.

## Executable judgment

`needs-additional-evidence`. Regulatory architecture is strong but largely trial-protective/conditional; direct affected-community evidence on scarce-supply allocation priorities is insufficient.
