# M090 — Community governance of genomic research

**Case-family identity:** M090
**Primary domain:** research governance / data sovereignty
**Tags:** genomic research, Indigenous data sovereignty, collective governance, secondary use, consent
**Research status:** deep case draft
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** a research institution and a sovereign Indigenous community negotiating the terms on which genomic data and specimens may be held and reused.

**Decision question:** When genomic data or specimens concern a sovereign Indigenous community, what authority should community governance retain over secondary use, sharing and future research, beyond the individual consent of the people who contributed samples?

## Scenario pair

**Concise:** A research institution holds genomic data and specimens contributed by members of a sovereign Indigenous community under a study that has now ended. New researchers, including some outside the original partnership, wish to use the collection for questions the original consent did not name. Every contributor gave valid individual consent to the original study. The community has its own review body and asserts continuing authority over how data concerning its members are used, stored and shared. The institution must decide what governance rule applies to the secondary use.

**Detailed:** A university biobank holds genomic samples and derived data gathered in partnership with a sovereign Indigenous community for a completed study of a specific condition. Individual participants consented to that study under a document that did not anticipate the secondary questions now proposed, some by researchers with no relationship to the community. The community's review board has previously required that new researchers enter separate agreements, that particular uses be named rather than described in general terms, and that data be returned or reported back. Institutional policy allows de-identified data to be shared through a controlled repository on the strength of the original consent alone. The community holds that its authority over data concerning its members does not lapse when a study ends, and that population-level inferences affect the community whether or not any individual is identifiable. The institution must decide what governance rule governs the secondary use.

## Evidence architecture

**Affected/community evidence.** 2023 US community-engaged interviews with 42 Tribal leaders, clinicians, researchers, policy makers and Tribal review-board members emphasise Indigenous governance, sovereignty, research approvals, agreements, ownership, new consent for future studies, data return and community control. Oklahoma Tribal genomic partnerships require explicit specification of disease and use, secondary uses, storage and security, and separate agreements and IRB review for new researchers.

**Governance/framework evidence.** The CARE Principles — Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics — were developed through Indigenous data-sovereignty networks and hold that Indigenous Peoples should determine governance protocols and participate in stewardship decisions. The SING Consortium framework calls for tribal oversight and consultation, collaboration, transparency, capacity and benefit.

## Serious policies

- layered governance: individual consent **and** continuing community authority over secondary use;
- community approval and control for specified uses, with individual consent still required where it applies.

Both are forms of continuing collective authority. They differ in mechanism rather than in whether community authority persists.

## Frameworks

- **Collective self-determination:** authority over data concerning a people is an aspect of that people's sovereignty and does not transfer with a sample.
- **Group harm and population inference:** genomic findings make claims about a population, so de-identification of individuals does not extinguish the community's stake.
- **Relational stewardship:** consent is an ongoing relationship with obligations of return, reporting and renegotiation, not a single permission at collection.

## Construction risks

The dominant risk is manufacturing an opposing candidate. The source layer does **not** support an affected-community position that individual consent alone is sufficient, and constructing one to produce a two-sided public pool would invent disagreement the evidence does not contain — the exact failure the near-entailment and paraphrase rules exist to prevent, in a case where the invention would also misrepresent the communities concerned.

The genuine tension in this case runs between community governance and *institutional* data-sharing policy. That is a real disagreement, but the institutional position is not an affected-community position and must not be filed as one.

## Executable judgment

`research-complete-not-executable`. The affected-community source layer converges strongly on continuing collective authority rather than splitting into competing community policies. Per the Batch-I audit: *"The important corpus result is convergence on layered/collective governance, not a forced intra-community split."*

This is a finding, not a gap. A case where the affected community speaks with one voice about its own authority is evidence about the domain, and the corpus records it as such rather than forcing it into an executable shape.
