# M089 — Setting a national health-research agenda

**Case-family identity:** M089  
**Primary domain:** research priority setting / distributive ethics  
**Tags:** research funding, priority setting, disease burden, neglected populations, participation, innovation  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** public national health-research funder.  
**Decision question:** How should a fixed research budget be allocated across high-burden diseases, neglected/worst-off populations, near-term implementation research and higher-risk transformative science?

## Scenario pair

**Concise:** A national public funder has a fixed five-year health-research budget that cannot fund every valuable program. One portfolio would direct most funding toward diseases responsible for the greatest current population health loss and projects with high expected near-term impact. A second would reserve substantial funding for neglected diseases and populations that have historically received little research investment despite severe disadvantage. A third would use a participatory multi-principle portfolio combining burden, equity, implementation needs and a protected share for higher-risk transformative research. The funder must choose the governing priority rule.

**Detailed:** A national public research agency is setting a five-year portfolio across prevention, clinical research, implementation science and discovery. Its budget is large but scarce relative to worthwhile opportunities. Analysts can estimate current disease burden and plausible expected health gains, but estimates favor conditions with strong existing evidence pipelines and may understate poorly measured or neglected problems. Patient groups and marginalized communities argue that historical underinvestment should count independently of total burden. Scientific leaders argue that a portfolio focused only on near-term expected gain will underfund transformative high-risk work that can create future capabilities. The agency can conduct a transparent stakeholder process and reserve shares of the budget for different goals. It must choose whether expected population health gain/burden should dominate; whether neglected/worst-off populations should receive priority even at some opportunity cost; or whether a participatory multiprinciple portfolio should explicitly balance burden, equity, implementation and exploratory science.

## Evidence architecture

WHO's 2025 ethics guidance explicitly frames research priority setting as distributive ethics: research resources are scarce, funding one project displaces another and value judgments about whose interests count are unavoidable. WHO recommends transparent goals and values, inclusive stakeholder participation, public-health need, equity, implementation and monitoring. WHO's priority-setting methodology does not prescribe one substantive metric such as burden maximization or worst-off priority.

Affected/public evidence varies by disease and process. Patients and publics often prioritize severity, unmet need, equity and lived impact differently from technical burden/cost-effectiveness metrics. For this generic national portfolio, those studies support participatory design more securely than any universal public ranking rule.

## Serious policies

- maximize expected population health gain using disease burden and research tractability;
- prioritize neglected/worst-off populations and historically underfunded health needs;
- use a participatory multiprinciple portfolio with explicit criteria and a reserved exploratory-science share.

## Frameworks

- **Utilitarian/health maximization:** public research should generate as much expected health improvement as possible.
- **Prioritarian justice:** research resources should correct neglect and give extra weight to those worst off, even if aggregate gains are smaller.
- **Procedural pluralism:** no single metric captures uncertainty, equity, public legitimacy and transformative potential → use transparent participatory balancing.

## Construction risks

Do not make disease burden synonymous with research tractability. Do not let participation become a substitute for substantive fairness. Explicitly state opportunity costs and the exploratory reserve. Do not invent exact percentage allocations as evidence.

## Executable judgment

`needs-additional-evidence`. WHO gives a strong ethical process architecture but does not supply competing substantive expert policies; direct national public evidence matching the three portfolio rules is too context-specific for confident execution.
