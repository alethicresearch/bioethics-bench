# The nonhuman-interest problem is already answered in the released corpus

**From:** independent reviewer
**Re:** the Batch-P finding on M156–M159, and what it implies for Featured v1 and Batch Q.

The Batch-P audit is right, and the checkpoint already states the general form: *"A computational
ethics architecture that requires every affected interest to arrive through surveyable human
testimony risks systematically underrepresenting beings who cannot supply such testimony."* That
is the deepest methodological finding the corpus has produced.

Two things follow that the checkpoint does not yet say.

---

## 1. Featured v1 has already answered this question, and answered it the other way

**F17 is M156.** `FEATURED_V1_SELECTION.md` records the crosswalk, and gives F17's selection
rationale as: *"Animal ethics is part of the Bench proper; directly tests whether SACRE can
represent nonhuman interests faithfully."*

F17 is released, public, `2×2×2`, and its public pool is human attitudes:

- `pub1` — *"Extracted from UK public-attitude surveys in which acceptance of medical animal
  research is conditional on purpose and on the absence of an adequate alternative…"*
- `pub2` — *"Adapted from the same attitude research… The sequencing rule — fund validation first
  — is Bench editorial translation of that interest."*

So the released corpus contains a case selected specifically to test whether SACRE can represent
nonhuman interests, which represents those interests by surveying humans about them. M156 — the
same family — is now withheld precisely because that move is not legitimate.

This is the second verified pair of its kind. F11 is M098, released and executable while M098 is
refused. Two further pairs follow from the crosswalk and the batch reports: F15 is M129, and F18
is M160, both released while their counterparts are non-executable.

I attempted an aggregate count across all twenty families and it was wrong — it misclassified
M056 and M094, which are executable — so no number is offered here. Four verified pairs are enough
to establish the pattern without one.

## 2. Batch Q walks straight into it

The next block is zoonotic culling and vaccination, invasive and feral animal control,
wild-animal disease intervention, institutional animal-product procurement, and then
climate and planetary health.

Every one of those has a primary bearer of harm who cannot be surveyed. Animals, in the first
four. Future generations, in the climate cases — and F19, released, is M165, which is in that
block.

So the architecture question is not a Batch-P retrospective. It determines whether the next ten
cases can be built at all, and the corpus is about to spend a batch discovering that again unless
the rule is settled first.

## The choice, stated plainly

The audit lists three options. The distinction that matters between them is not methodological
taste — it is **who the `public` pool is claiming to speak for**.

- **A separate nonhuman affected-interest class.** Honest, and it changes the profile registry:
  a case with four source classes is not `2×2×2` and would need a registered shape. This is the
  only option that lets the corpus say "these are the animal's interests" without the word
  meaning "what humans said about them".
- **Welfare candidates from ethology and veterinary science.** Defensible, and note what it is:
  expert testimony about an interest, which is a *third-party account*, not the bearer's own. It
  should be labelled as such rather than filed in the affected-community pool.
- **Restrict executable animal cases to questions human policy evidence can legitimately answer.**
  Cleanest, and it makes the corpus smaller in a principled way — consistent with every other
  refusal the strict standard has produced.

**What I would not do is leave it implicit.** The current architecture does not distinguish
"the affected community's own view" from "others' views about the affected party", and wherever
the bearer cannot testify, the `public` pool silently becomes the second. That is true of F17 and
F18 today, and will be true of every climate case.

## Why this is a finding rather than a defect

A benchmark that cannot represent the interests of beings who cannot be surveyed has discovered
something about computational ethics, not just about itself. Every method that aggregates elicited
preferences inherits this limit; SACRE makes it visible because the source class is named on the
record and can be inspected.

That is worth reporting in P1 or P2 in its own right — and it is a much stronger result if the
corpus states the limit deliberately than if a reader later notices that the animal-ethics case
polls humans.

## Recommendation

Settle the rule before M161, not after Batch Q. And whichever option is taken, add a line to the
construction-standard record saying what the `public` pool means when the bearer of harm cannot
supply testimony — because Featured v1 already made that choice by default, and the corpus should
say so rather than leave two answers standing.
