# M015 — Pediatric practice policy after persistent refusal of routine childhood vaccination

**Case-family identity:** M015  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** pediatric ethics / public health  
**Subdomain:** vaccine refusal; practice dismissal; continuity of care  
**Tags:** vaccination, pediatrics, refusal, dismissal, medical home, stewardship  
**Research status:** deep case draft; professional policy pluralism strong, direct parent-policy evidence limited  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** pediatric practice / health-system outpatient service.  
**Decision question:** After repeated evidence-based counseling fails and parents continue to refuse all routine childhood vaccines for a healthy child, what ongoing-care policy should the pediatric practice use?

The represented decision is specifically about the clinician–practice relationship: continue care and counseling, or dismiss/decline routine enrollment under defined safeguards. It deliberately excludes a state vaccine mandate, school-entry requirement, acute outbreak exclusion or emergency treatment decision.

## Concise representation

The parents of a healthy 2-year-old child refuse all routine childhood vaccines after several visits of evidence-based counseling. There is no current local outbreak and no medical contraindication to vaccination. The practice also cares for newborns and immunocompromised children, but routine infection-control precautions are available. For this benchmark, assume that if the family is dismissed, another reputable pediatric practice within reasonable travel distance is accepting new patients, the family will receive appropriate notice and records transfer, and urgent care will not be abandoned during transition. The parents remain willing to bring the child for preventive care and treatment but do not intend to vaccinate. The practice must decide what policy should govern continued routine care for persistently vaccine-refusing families.

## Detailed representation

The parents of a healthy 2-year-old child have declined all routinely recommended childhood vaccinations since infancy. The pediatrician has discussed vaccine-preventable disease, vaccine safety, the recommended schedule and the parents’ specific concerns over multiple visits using evidence-based communication strategies. The parents understand the recommendation and do not report a medical or religious contraindication; they remain opposed to vaccination for the foreseeable future. No vaccine-preventable outbreak is currently occurring in the community. The practice serves newborns, children receiving immunosuppressive treatment and other medically vulnerable patients, although standard scheduling and infection-control measures can reduce waiting-room exposure. The parents value the child’s current pediatrician, attend preventive visits reliably and agree to seek prompt evaluation if the child becomes ill. For this benchmark, assume that if dismissal occurs, the practice will provide written notice, emergency/urgent care during an appropriate transition interval, complete records transfer, and information about another reputable pediatric practice within reasonable travel distance that is accepting new patients. The practice must decide whether to continue routine care and repeated counseling, or use a policy allowing dismissal after persistent refusal under these safeguards.

## Decision-critical facts

- healthy child whose parents persistently refuse all routine recommended vaccines;
- repeated evidence-based counseling has already occurred;
- no current outbreak and no vaccine contraindication;
- child continues to need ordinary preventive and acute pediatric care;
- practice includes medically vulnerable patients but has ordinary infection-control options;
- alternative reputable pediatric care is reasonably available if dismissal occurs;
- dismissal, if chosen, includes notice, transition care and records transfer;
- decision concerns practice enrollment/continuity, not a government mandate.

## Explicit uncertainty

The case does not assign a precise incremental infection risk caused by keeping this one unvaccinated child in the practice. Such risk varies by disease prevalence, community vaccination levels, clinic design and outbreak status. It also does not assume that dismissal will change parental vaccine beliefs; empirical evidence on that effect is limited.

The underlying literature is clearer about professional disagreement and the value of communication than about parents’ preferences concerning dismissal policies. That asymmetry is part of the case construction rather than a gap to be filled with invented public candidates.

## Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral outpatient policy. For this benchmark, assume dismissal for nonemergency routine care is legally permissible if the practice follows the stipulated notice, transition and nonabandonment safeguards. School/daycare vaccine laws are outside the represented decision.

## Benchmark stipulations

- **Counseling stipulation:** repeated evidence-based vaccine counseling has occurred without changing the parents’ decision.
- **Outbreak stipulation:** no current local outbreak is occurring.
- **Access stipulation:** another reputable pediatric practice is reasonably available if dismissal occurs.
- **Nonabandonment stipulation:** notice, transition coverage and records transfer will be provided.
- **Medical stipulation:** the child has no contraindication to vaccination.

All appear in both representations.

## Serious policy / position families

- continue providing routine pediatric care while revisiting vaccination respectfully and using infection-control precautions;
- allow dismissal only after repeated counseling fails and defined access, notice, equity and nonabandonment safeguards are satisfied;
- decline new routine enrollment of families committed to total vaccine refusal while maintaining existing patients or emergency obligations;
- use a graduated practice policy rather than treating every delayed or selectively accepted vaccine schedule as equivalent to complete persistent refusal.

## Public / affected-community evidence

The vaccine-hesitancy literature documents diverse parental reasons for delay/refusal, including safety concerns, mistrust, perceived disease risk, prior experiences, information environments and a desire for control. It strongly supports respectful communication and warns that trust can be harmed by stigmatizing or dismissive interactions.

The current packet does **not** contain strong direct parent evidence comparing the institutional policies represented here—continued care versus dismissal after safeguards. Parents’ refusal of vaccination itself cannot be translated into support for continued enrollment, and parental support for vaccination cannot be translated into support for dismissing another family. This source-class gap should remain explicit.

## Expert / professional recommendations

Current American Academy of Pediatrics guidance prioritizes evidence-based communication and continued efforts to improve vaccine uptake. It also recognizes dismissal as a possible practice response after repeated unsuccessful efforts, but emphasizes consideration of less drastic alternatives, potential effects on the child’s access to care, transparency, equity and avoidance of abandonment.

Pediatrician surveys show real professional-practice variation: some clinicians never dismiss for vaccine refusal, while others do so under defined conditions. This variation is relevant because it reflects genuine alternative professional architectures, not an artificially generated expert disagreement.

The existence of school vaccination requirements is not evidence for dismissal from primary care. Those are different decision-makers, purposes and procedural safeguards.

## Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Child welfare / continuity of care:** the child is not the decision-maker responsible for parental refusal and still benefits from a stable medical home → dismissal can reduce preventive care and continuity without reliably increasing vaccination → continue care while counseling and protecting vulnerable patients through proportionate precautions.
- **Nonmaleficence / protection of third parties:** vaccine refusal increases avoidable exposure risk to the child and others → practices have responsibilities to protect medically vulnerable patients and sustain evidence-based norms → permit dismissal when persistent refusal continues despite counseling and safe transfer is available.
- **Relational trust / public-health engagement:** durable vaccine confidence often depends on trusted clinician relationships → severing care may deepen mistrust and push families toward less evidence-based environments → prefer continued engagement unless concrete safety circumstances make it untenable.
- **Professional integrity / institutional consistency:** clinicians may reasonably decline to structure a practice around persistent rejection of a core preventive standard → a transparent policy can preserve professional integrity and fairness across families → allow dismissal only under prospective, consistently applied, nonabandoning criteria.

## Construction and representation risks

- conflating practice dismissal with a school or government vaccine mandate;
- making an outbreak active and thereby changing the decision into immediate infection control;
- assuming dismissal improves vaccine uptake without evidence;
- punishing the child rhetorically for the parents’ decision;
- assuming vaccine-refusing parents are uninformed after the benchmark has stipulated repeated informed counseling;
- treating professional disagreement as public opinion;
- making alternative pediatric access absent in only one representation;
- exaggerating waiting-room transmission into a guaranteed harm.
