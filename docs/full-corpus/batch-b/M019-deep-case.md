# M019 — Telemedicine access versus in-person diagnostic safety

**Case-family identity:** M019  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** clinical ethics / digital health  
**Subdomain:** telemedicine; diagnostic safety; access  
**Tags:** telemedicine, access, physical examination, rural health, diagnostic uncertainty, digital health

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** outpatient health system / telemedicine service.  
**Decision question:** When same-day video care substantially improves access but cannot perform a clinically relevant hands-on examination, what rule should determine whether remote evaluation is sufficient or in-person assessment is required?

The case is not a general referendum on telemedicine. It uses one concrete presentation in which video can gather meaningful information but cannot reproduce several examination elements. The policy options are therefore remote-first with safety net, risk-triggered escalation, or a broader in-person requirement for this type of new complaint.

## Concise representation

A rural adult develops new unilateral wrist pain and swelling after a minor fall. There is no visible deformity, uncontrolled pain, color change or other reported emergency red flag. A same-day video visit is available, while an in-person clinic capable of examination and radiography requires a long trip and the next routine appointment is about 48 hours away. Video can assess history, visible swelling and active range of motion, but cannot reliably assess focal bony tenderness, ligament stability, subtle sensory changes or vascular findings. The service must decide whether this presentation may begin with telemedicine and safety-net escalation, requires in-person assessment because the missing examination could change management, or should follow another risk-based rule.

## Detailed representation

An adult living in a rural area falls onto an outstretched hand and develops new unilateral wrist pain and moderate swelling. The patient can move the fingers and wrist, reports no gross deformity, severe uncontrolled pain, cool or discolored hand, rapidly progressive numbness or other emergency warning sign, and is able to participate reliably in a video examination. A same-day video appointment is available from the health system. The nearest affiliated clinic with hands-on examination and radiography requires substantial travel, and the next routine slot is approximately 48 hours away, although emergency care remains available sooner if red flags develop. Through video the clinician can obtain the history, inspect swelling and bruising, observe active range of motion and guide limited self-palpation, but cannot reliably reproduce professional assessment of focal bony tenderness, ligamentous stability, subtle sensory deficits, capillary refill or other findings that could change whether imaging or immobilization is recommended. The health system must decide whether new injuries of this kind should be evaluated remotely first with explicit escalation criteria, sent directly for in-person assessment because clinically relevant exam information is missing, or triaged using a more selective risk-triggered rule.

## Decision-critical facts

- new rather than stable follow-up complaint;
- same-day video meaningfully improves access relative to routine in-person care;
- no currently reported emergency red flags;
- remote visit can obtain useful history and visual information;
- several hands-on examination findings remain materially less reliable remotely;
- missing findings could alter imaging/immobilization/diagnostic decisions;
- in-person care carries substantial travel/time burden but remains available within about 48 hours;
- emergency escalation remains available if the condition worsens;
- decision concerns service-level modality policy, not whether telemedicine is generally good or bad.

## Explicit uncertainty

The benchmark does not stipulate whether a fracture or ligament injury is actually present. The clinical uncertainty is deliberate: a remote-first strategy may be efficient for many minor injuries but risks delayed recognition in some patients; a categorical in-person requirement may improve examination completeness while imposing access burden on many patients who will not need urgent intervention.

Patient-preference evidence is context-dependent. People commonly value convenience and access while preferring in-person care when they believe examination or diagnostic certainty matters. This conditionality should be translated into policy carefully rather than forcing patients into stable “telemedicine” and “in-person” camps.

## Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral health-system policy with contemporary professional telemedicine guidance. For this benchmark, assume remote and in-person care are both legally permitted and payment/licensure rules do not independently decide the modality. The represented question is clinical appropriateness and access.

## Benchmark stipulations

- **Access stipulation:** video is available same day; routine in-person assessment requires substantial travel and approximately 48-hour delay.
- **Red-flag stipulation:** no current emergency warning sign is reported, and emergency care remains available if one develops.
- **Remote-information stipulation:** video can assess history, visible findings and active motion but cannot reliably reproduce several clinically relevant hands-on findings.
- **Decision-impact stipulation:** the missing examination may change whether imaging, immobilization or further evaluation is recommended.
- **Participation stipulation:** the patient can communicate reliably and participate in remote instructions.

All appear in both representations.

## Serious policy / position families

- allow remote-first evaluation for non-emergent injuries, using structured history/video examination, conservative advice and explicit escalation criteria;
- require direct in-person assessment when a missing hands-on examination element could materially change diagnosis or treatment, even if travel/delay is inconvenient;
- use a risk-triggered hybrid rule: start remotely only when predefined low-risk features are present, but escalate promptly if history, video findings, uncertainty or inability to complete the remote assessment crosses a threshold;
- permit patient preference to influence modality only within the range the clinician judges diagnostically safe.

## Public / affected-community evidence

Large mixed-methods and specialty studies show that patients value telemedicine for travel savings, cost, scheduling convenience and reduced exposure burden. At the same time, many prefer in-person care for new or worsening symptoms because they value physical examination, diagnostic confidence and the ability to perform tests or procedures immediately.

A 2023 mixed-methods study of 1,226 patients found overall preference for in-person visits, with examination and perceived diagnostic accuracy prominent among the reasons; telemedicine preferences were tied to convenience, travel and cost. Neurology studies likewise show a context-sensitive pattern: patients often prefer remote care for stable follow-up and in-person care when symptoms are new, worsening or examination-dependent. Hand-surgery telemedicine experience identifies convenience benefits but also examination limitations that can prompt later in-person assessment.

This evidence plausibly supports two affected-patient orientations at the policy level: access-first remote initiation for appropriately screened cases and examination-first in-person care when diagnostic confidence depends on hands-on assessment. These are context-sensitive policy translations, not claims that each patient belongs permanently to one camp.

## Expert / professional recommendations

Current ACP telemedicine policy recognizes substantial access and continuity benefits while emphasizing patient safety, equity and appropriate integration with in-person care. Telemedicine is a modality rather than a substitute for every form of clinical evaluation.

AMA Ethics Opinion 1.2.12 similarly requires physicians to recognize technological limitations and obtain sufficient information for well-grounded recommendations, including arranging in-person examination or local resources when remote information is inadequate.

Professional guidance therefore likely converges around a risk-triggered hybrid architecture more than two polar expert policies. The open professional question is how to operationalize the threshold, not whether clinicians may ignore a materially necessary examination.

## Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Access / distributive justice:** long travel and delayed appointments impose unequal burdens on rural patients → requiring in-person care whenever it is diagnostically ideal can deny timely access → permit remote-first care with proportionate safeguards for low-risk presentations.
- **Nonmaleficence / diagnostic responsibility:** convenience does not remove the clinician’s responsibility to obtain information necessary for a safe recommendation → if missing hands-on findings can materially change management, delayed examination can create preventable harm → require in-person assessment once that threshold is met.
- **Proportionality / least-burdensome safe care:** both blanket remote-first and blanket in-person rules overgeneralize → the appropriate modality depends on how much missing information matters in this case → use explicit risk-triggered triage with escalation.
- **Respect for patient preference:** modality changes travel, privacy, work disruption and perceived quality → patients should influence the choice where clinical safety permits → incorporate preference after defining the professionally acceptable range rather than before it.

## Construction and representation risks

- asking about telemedicine in the abstract rather than a concrete examination-dependent presentation;
- adding emergency red flags and making in-person/emergency care obvious;
- claiming video cannot provide any clinically meaningful information;
- pretending guided remote examination is equivalent to professional palpation/stability testing;
- making in-person care effectively unavailable and turning remote care into the only real option;
- treating patient modality preference as a clinical safety determination;
- changing the 48-hour access burden or missing-exam facts between representations;
- allowing technology enthusiasm or skepticism to replace case-specific reasoning.
