## M011 — Expanded access to an investigational intervention

### Bounded decision
A seriously ill adult with no satisfactory approved options cannot enroll in the relevant trial. The treating institution and manufacturer must decide whether to pursue individual-patient expanded access to an investigational drug with completed early safety testing and a plausible but uncertain efficacy signal.

### Primary sources
- US FDA. *Expanded Access*. Current program page, updated 2025–2026. FDA states that expanded access may be appropriate when the patient has a serious or immediately life-threatening condition, no comparable satisfactory alternative exists, trial enrollment is not possible, potential benefit justifies potential risk, and access will not interfere with trials or product development.
  - https://www.fda.gov/news-events/public-health-focus/expanded-access
- US FDA. *IND Applications for Clinical Treatment (Expanded Access): Overview* (current 2026 page).
  - https://www.fda.gov/drugs/investigational-new-drug-ind-application/ind-applications-clinical-treatment-expanded-access-overview
- US FDA. *Expanded Access to Investigational Drugs for Treatment Use: Questions and Answers* / 2026 educational materials.
- Bunnik EM, Aarts N, van de Vathorst S. *What do patients with unmet medical needs want? A qualitative study of patients’ views and experiences with expanded access to unapproved, investigational treatments in the Netherlands.* BMC Med Ethics. 2019;20:80. PMID 31706313.
- Moerdler S et al. / related oncology literature on physician experience with expanded access; qualitative academic-oncology studies include PMID 34919568 and PMID 36318206.
- Caplan A et al. *A Pilot Experiment in Responding to Individual Patient Requests for Compassionate Use of an Unapproved Drug: The Compassionate Use Advisory Committee (CompAC).* Ther Innov Regul Sci. 2019;53:243–248. PMID 29714573.

### Source-class read
**Affected/public:** usable. Patients with unmet needs report wanting clinicians to discuss non-standard options, weigh safety/efficacy and quality of life, and worry about unequal access. That evidence supports a patient-centered access architecture but does not cleanly supply two opposed institutional policies.

**Expert/professional:** strong but conditional rather than polar. FDA criteria support access when stringent conditions are met and also expressly protect clinical-trial development. Experienced oncologists tend to prefer approved treatment or trial participation where available but view expanded access as an important option when those routes fail.

**Framework:** strong. Individual rescue/beneficence, procedural justice/equal access, and social value/evidence generation produce genuine tensions.

### Construction judgment
Do not frame the case as “give a dying person hope versus deny treatment.” The intervention must have enough early evidence to make access plausible, but efficacy must remain unproven. Trial participation must genuinely be unavailable. The benchmark should not stipulate that access would materially deplete trial supply, because that would make FDA expanded-access eligibility fail by construction. A better conflict is whether the institution/manufacturer should pursue access when criteria can be met but evidence remains highly uncertain and administrative/manufacturing burdens are real.

**Tentative eligibility:** `needs-additional-evidence` for a stable executable profile. Direct patient evidence is good but not yet two-policy granular.

## M012 — Clinical response to a patient seeking an unproven commercial stem-cell intervention abroad

### Bounded decision
A competent adult with a serious progressive neurologic disease plans to travel to a commercial clinic for an intrathecal mesenchymal stromal-cell intervention. The intervention has a biologically plausible rationale and small early studies in related settings but no credible evidence establishing clinical efficacy for this patient’s condition, is not being provided in a regulated clinical trial, and carries procedure/infection/financial risks. The treating neurologist must decide how far to assist.

### Primary sources
- International Society for Stem Cell Research (ISSCR). *Guidelines for Stem Cell Research and Clinical Translation*, version 1.2, August 2025. Sections 3.5 and 4.3. ISSCR condemns routine/commercial provision of unproven cell-based interventions outside compliant research or tightly constrained medical innovation and emphasizes evidence-based, non-promotional patient communication.
  - https://www.isscr.org/guidelines
  - https://www.isscr.org/guidelines/clinical-translation-of-stem-cell-based-interventions
- US FDA. *Consumer Alert on Regenerative Medicine Products Including Stem Cells and Exosomes* and current patient warnings on unapproved human-cell/tissue products.
  - https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/consumers-biologics/consumer-alert-regenerative-medicine-products-including-stem-cells-and-exosomes
- Master Z et al. *Factors that influence unproven stem cell intervention seeking behavior: A qualitative analysis of U.S. patients considering or having undertaken unproven stem cell interventions.* 2025. PMID 40744777.
- Master Z et al. *(Mis)trust among patients seeking unproven stem cell therapies: a qualitative analysis.* 2026. PMID 42210947.
- Bowman M, Racke M, Kissel J, Imitola J. *Responsibilities of Health Care Professionals in Counseling and Educating Patients With Incurable Neurological Diseases Regarding “Stem Cell Tourism”: Caveat Emptor.* JAMA Neurol. 2015;72:1342–1345.
- Snyder J et al. / medical-tourism literature; web-based clinic analyses include PMID 25449045.

### Source-class read
**Affected/public:** strong enough to represent heterogeneous orientations toward risk. Recent qualitative work distinguishes high seekers, who often perceive substantial possible benefit and may discount risk under desperation, from low seekers, who place greater weight on uncertainty and harm. This can support two patient-derived orientations if translated carefully and never described as population prevalence.

**Expert/professional:** converges strongly. Professional guidance opposes endorsement of commercial unproven interventions, while good clinical practice still supports truthful counseling, records continuity, and harm reduction. The real professional architecture is therefore “do not endorse or facilitate as treatment, but do not abandon the patient.”

**Framework:** strong. Respect for autonomous risk-taking and continuity of care can support harm-reduction assistance; nonmaleficence and epistemic integrity support refusing actions that lend professional endorsement to an inadequately supported commercial intervention.

### Construction judgment
Avoid a clinic that is obviously fraudulent or claims impossible cures. Use a plausible but unproven intervention in a serious disease for which legitimate research exists. Distinguish sending ordinary medical records and discussing risks from actively arranging treatment, certifying efficacy, or referring as if the clinic were a validated provider.

**Tentative eligibility:** likely `executable-other-profile`, probably two affected/public orientations × one professional architecture × two frameworks, if candidate-level audit confirms the public translations are genuinely actionable.

## M013 — Consent to material trainee participation

### Bounded decision
For an elective procedure in a teaching hospital, what disclosure and permission should be required before a trainee performs a material part of care under direct supervision?

### Primary sources
- AMA Code of Medical Ethics Opinion 9.2.1, *Medical Student Involvement in Patient Care*. Patients should be aware students may participate, know training status, be informed before procedures when they will be incapacitated, and have their willingness confirmed.
  - https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/medical-student-involvement-patient-care-0
- ACOG Committee Opinion, *Sexual Misconduct* (2020; current page): trainees should be introduced and patients given an opportunity to agree; educational breast/genital/rectal examinations require specific informed consent.
- Santen SA et al. *“Sorry, it’s my first time!” Will patients consent to medical students learning procedures?* Med Educ. 2005;39:365–369. PMID 15813758. Most patients consented to minor procedures after explicit disclosure of student inexperience, while two-thirds thought first-time status should be disclosed.
- Graber MA et al. *Patient opinions and attitudes toward medical student procedures in the emergency department.* Acad Emerg Med. 2003;10:1329–1333. PMID 14644784. Willingness varied markedly by procedure and prior student experience.
- Porta CR et al. *Training surgeons and the informed consent process: routine disclosure of trainee participation and its effect on patient willingness and consent rates.* Arch Surg. 2012. PMID 21930977. Patients broadly supported training but consent rates changed sharply with the trainee’s actual operative role and most wanted role information.
- Langerman A et al. *Patient Perceptions of Resident Involvement in Surgery: A Qualitative Study Using Surgical Video.* 2022. PMID 35396187.
- Milad HC et al. *The impact of obtaining explicit informed consent for medical student participation in the pelvic examination under anesthesia.* Int J Gynaecol Obstet. 2025. PMID 39905599.

### Source-class read
**Affected/public:** strong on the proposition that willingness depends on role, risk, supervision, and level of trainee experience, and that many patients want meaningful disclosure. The evidence is heterogeneous in willingness to permit trainee performance but less clearly divided over whether institutional policy should require explicit opt-in versus general team consent.

**Expert/professional:** strong and disclosure-dominant. AMA and specialty guidance clearly protect opportunity to decline student participation; intimate examinations have especially stringent consent expectations.

**Framework:** strong: autonomy/authorization versus the social-professional obligation to train future clinicians, with proportionality based on invasiveness and supervision.

### Construction judgment
Use a material but not intimate procedure to avoid making specific consent legally/ethically obvious. A good case is an elective laparoscopic procedure in which a resident, clearly identified as a trainee, will perform defined operative steps under direct attending supervision. Exclude mere observation and exclude an unsupervised trainee.

**Tentative eligibility:** `needs-additional-evidence` for current profile vocabulary. Strong patient evidence may ultimately support a smaller asymmetric profile, but an institutional “general consent is enough” public candidate should not be inferred merely from willingness to have trainees participate.

## M014 — Pregnant patient refuses recommended treatment intended primarily to benefit the fetus

### Bounded decision
A decisionally capable pregnant adult refuses a recommended intervention expected to materially improve fetal outcome while imposing nontrivial bodily burdens and risks on her. What should the care team do after informed, noncoercive counseling fails to change the decision?

### Primary sources
- American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists. Committee Opinion No. 664, *Refusal of Medically Recommended Treatment During Pregnancy* (2016; current ACOG ethics guidance). ACOG states that pregnancy is not an exception to a capable patient’s right to refuse treatment and strongly opposes coercion and court-ordered interventions.
  - https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2016/06/refusal-of-medically-recommended-treatment-during-pregnancy
- General informed-consent and reproductive-justice literature on coercion, bodily integrity, and the historical unequal use of compelled obstetric intervention.

### Source-class read
**Affected/public:** currently limited for this precise institutional-policy question. There is abundant literature on pregnant patients’ experiences of respectful maternity care and coercion, but the present packet does not yet justify two affected-public policies about override versus refusal.

**Expert/professional:** very strong and highly convergent: respect informed refusal, use directive counseling where appropriate, avoid coercion and courts.

**Framework:** genuinely plural. Bodily autonomy and equality support honoring refusal. Fetal-beneficence approaches can support intensive persuasion and, in some philosophical accounts, exceptional intervention, but that latter direction must be labeled a framework construction rather than a professional recommendation.

### Construction judgment
Do not make the intervention risk-free or the fetal benefit certain. Do not use incapacity, substance use, or psychiatric diagnosis as a shortcut. A plausible benchmark is a recommended cesarean delivery in a time-sensitive but not seconds-to-act setting, with a substantial expected fetal benefit and a small but meaningful maternal surgical risk, after the patient demonstrates understanding and continues to refuse.

**Tentative eligibility:** `needs-additional-evidence`; likely non-executable unless direct affected-patient policy evidence improves.

## M015 — Pediatric practice policy after persistent refusal of routine childhood vaccination

### Bounded decision
After repeated counseling, parents continue to refuse all routine childhood vaccination for a healthy child. What ongoing-care policy should a pediatric practice use: continue care and counseling, or dismiss/decline routine enrollment under defined safeguards?

### Primary sources
- American Academy of Pediatrics. *Strategies for Improving Vaccine Communication and Uptake.* Pediatrics. 2024;153:e2023065483. The AAP emphasizes evidence-based communication and allows dismissal as an acceptable option after repeated unsuccessful efforts, consideration of less drastic alternatives, access effects, transparency, equity, and nonabandonment safeguards.
- AAP Red Book 2024–2027. *Policies for Families Who Refuse or Delay Vaccination.*
- Flanagan-Klygis EA, Sharp L, Frader JE. *Dismissing the family who refuses vaccines: a study of pediatrician attitudes.* Arch Pediatr Adolesc Med. 2005;159:929–934. PMID 16203937.
- O’Leary ST et al. / national survey literature; *Characteristics of Physicians Who Dismiss Families for Refusing Vaccines.* Pediatrics. 2015. PMID 26527552.
- Kempe A et al. / JAMA 2020 survey: *Policies Among US Pediatricians for Dismissing Patients for Delaying or Refusing Vaccination.*
- Lantos JD et al. *Should Pediatric Practices Have Policies to Not Care for Children With Vaccine-Hesitant Parents?* Pediatrics. 2016;138:e20161597. PMID 27590900.
- CDC. *State Vaccination Requirements.* Updated 2024. School/daycare mandates are a separate policy domain and should not be used as the answer to this practice-level case.

### Source-class read
**Affected/public:** parental reasons for hesitancy and trust are well studied, but direct parent evidence comparing practice dismissal versus continued care is limited. Do not turn vaccine refusal itself into support for a practice-dismissal policy.

**Expert/professional:** genuinely plural at the level of professional practice architecture. Current AAP guidance allows continued engagement and, under specified conditions, dismissal; pediatricians themselves remain divided in practice. This is not the same as a public split.

**Framework:** strong. Child welfare and public-risk reduction can support restrictive practice policies; relational continuity, access, and the child’s independent interest in having a pediatric medical home support continued care and counseling.

### Construction judgment
Separate the practice relationship from school mandates and outbreak exclusion. State that no acute outbreak is occurring, the child is otherwise healthy, repeated evidence-based counseling has failed, and alternative reputable pediatric care is reasonably available if dismissal occurs. The presence of high-risk patients in the waiting room may be relevant but should not be exaggerated into inevitable transmission.

**Tentative eligibility:** likely `needs-additional-evidence` because the public/affected class is not directly policy-granular even though professional pluralism is strong.

## M016 — Parents refuse high-benefit curative cancer therapy for a child

### Bounded decision
Parents of a young child refuse standard treatment for a newly diagnosed cancer with a high probability of cure and a very poor outcome without therapy. When should clinicians move from negotiation to state intervention?

### Primary sources
- American Academy of Pediatrics. *Pediatric Decision Making: Consensus Recommendations.* Pediatrics. 2023;152:e2023061832. State intervention may be appropriate as a last resort when refusal creates significant risk of serious imminent harm or fails basic interests and the proposed treatment is necessary, effective, benefits outweigh burdens, and less intrusive alternatives are exhausted.
- Diekema DS. *Parental refusals of medical treatment: the harm principle as threshold for state intervention.* Theor Med Bioeth. 2004. PMID 15637945.
- Weaver MS et al. *Family Refusal of Chemotherapy for Pediatric Cancer Patients: A National Survey of Oncologists.* J Pediatr Hematol Oncol. 2015. PMID 25411868. Professional acceptance of refusal strongly tracks cure probability and patient age; refusal of therapies with moderate/good cure rates was rarely supported.
- Johnston EE et al. *Landscape of pediatric cancer treatment refusal and abandonment in the US: A qualitative study.* Front Pediatr. 2023. PMID 36699305.
- Mack JW et al. / related work on resolving refusal without legal action; case-series and qualitative studies include PMID 37638811 and PMID 40751325.
- Robertson EG et al. *Decision-making in childhood cancer: parents’ and adolescents’ views and perceptions.* Support Care Cancer. 2019. PMID 30880372.

### Source-class read
**Affected/public:** usable for how families experience pediatric oncology decision making—parents often want involvement, information, trust, and deliberation, but many also rely heavily on physician recommendations. This does not directly support two policies about court intervention.

**Expert/professional:** strong. There is broad support for persistent negotiation and support first, but state intervention becomes justified at a sufficiently high harm/high-benefit threshold. Empirical practitioner data also show threshold sensitivity rather than categorical thinking.

**Framework:** strong. Harm principle / child’s basic interests support override; constrained parental authority and family integrity support exhausting negotiation and using state coercion only at a high threshold.

### Construction judgment
Use an illness with a high but not certain cure rate and materially burdensome therapy. For this benchmark, stipulate approximately 85–90% long-term disease-free survival with standard therapy versus a high likelihood of death or irreversible progression without it; state major burdens and known treatment risks. Do not characterize parents as ignorant or malicious. Give a short but clinically acceptable window for intensive negotiation before delay materially lowers cure probability.

**Tentative eligibility:** `needs-additional-evidence` for an executable public pool; keep as a high-value scholarly case regardless.

## M017 — Reserve antimicrobial for present-patient benefit versus stewardship

### Bounded decision
A hospitalized patient has a serious multidrug-resistant infection. A reserve antimicrobial is expected to improve the patient’s chance of cure compared with a less-preferred alternative, but stewardship policy restricts the drug to preserve effectiveness and reduce unnecessary selective pressure. Should the institution permit immediate use, require specialist authorization, or prohibit use below a defined threshold?

### Primary sources
- WHO. *AWaRe classification of antibiotics for evaluation and monitoring of use*, 2025 update. Reserve antibiotics are stewardship targets intended for highly specific patients/settings and last-resort use when alternatives have failed or are unsuitable.
  - https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/B09489
- WHO AWaRe system and 2019–2025 supporting guidance.
- IDSA/SHEA. *Clinical Practice Guidelines for Implementing an Antibiotic Stewardship Program* (2016). Strong recommendation for preauthorization and/or prospective audit and feedback as core stewardship interventions.
  - https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/implementing-an-ASP/
- Public/patient AMR preference literature:
  - Roope LSJ et al. *Preferences regarding antibiotic treatment and the role of antibiotic resistance: A discrete choice experiment.* 2020. PMID 33080314. Contribution to resistance materially influenced lay treatment preferences and was the most important attribute for a majority class.
  - McNulty CAM et al. public-attitudes surveys on antibiotics and resistance, including PMID 35387816 and later England longitudinal data.
  - Moore JE et al. *Evaluating patient attitudes to increased patient engagement with antimicrobial stewardship.* JAC Antimicrob Resist. 2020;2:dlaa046.

### Source-class read
**Affected/public:** usable and potentially strong. Lay people can place substantial value on reducing resistance, but some patient groups prioritize immediate treatment benefit and trust clinicians to decide. Direct evidence on denying a reserve drug to a patient who personally has a meaningful expected benefit remains thinner than general AMR preferences.

**Expert/professional:** strong. Stewardship programs support restriction/authorization, not blanket denial of indicated reserve therapy. WHO expressly describes Reserve drugs as last-resort options that should remain accessible for appropriate patients.

**Framework:** excellent. Identifiable-patient beneficence, rule-consequential stewardship for future patients, and procedural justice/authorization can generate distinct actionable directions.

### Construction judgment
Do not claim that one dose measurably causes a future resistant infection. State the stewardship concern qualitatively and institutionally. To keep the case nontrivial, the reserve drug should have a meaningful but not overwhelming expected individual advantage; the alternative should remain clinically defensible. A pure “reserve is the only active drug” scenario would make access too obvious.

**Tentative eligibility:** likely `needs-additional-evidence` pending candidate-level audit of whether public evidence can support two policy directions without overtranslation. Professional layer may itself be one strong conditional-authorization architecture.

## M018 — Patient-requested antibiotics under genuine diagnostic uncertainty

### Bounded decision
An adult with an uncomplicated respiratory syndrome strongly prefers antibiotics. Bacterial infection is possible but not likely; immediate antibiotics offer low expected benefit, and close follow-up is feasible. What prescribing strategy should the clinician use?

### Primary sources
- CDC. *Core Elements of Outpatient Antibiotic Stewardship*, updated July 2026, plus the 2016 MMWR framework. CDC supports evidence-based standards and delayed prescribing/watchful waiting when appropriate.
  - https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/65/rr/rr6506a1.htm
- Spurling GK et al. *Immediate versus delayed versus no antibiotics for respiratory infections.* Cochrane Database Syst Rev. 2023. PMID 37791590 (updates earlier review PMID 28881007). Delayed prescribing substantially reduces antibiotic consumption compared with immediate antibiotics while preserving similar satisfaction and complication outcomes in many uncomplicated RTIs.
- Little P et al. *Delayed antibiotic prescribing strategies for respiratory tract infections in primary care.* BMJ. 2014. PMID 24603565.
- de la Poza Abad M et al. *Prescription Strategies in Acute Uncomplicated Respiratory Infections: A Randomized Clinical Trial.* JAMA Intern Med. 2016. PMID 26719947.
- Tan R et al. *Antibiotic expectations of patients attending an emergency department with upper respiratory tract infections.* Int J Antimicrob Agents. 2022. PMID 34971727.
- Laytner L et al. *Lack of Knowledge of Antibiotic Risks Contributes to Primary Care Patients’ Expectations of Antibiotics for Common Symptoms.* Ann Fam Med. 2024. PMID 39313338.
- McNulty CAM et al. England public-attitude studies; PMID 35387816.

### Source-class read
**Affected/public:** strong and heterogeneous. Some patients expect antibiotics and value immediate treatment; many others are comfortable when clinicians explain that antibiotics are not needed. Delayed prescriptions are particularly valuable because they can preserve reassurance and autonomy while greatly reducing use.

**Expert/professional:** strong. Stewardship supports no antibiotic when criteria do not justify it and delayed/back-up strategies when genuine uncertainty plus safe follow-up make them appropriate. Immediate prescribing driven only by patient pressure is not a professional standard.

**Framework:** strong. Nonmaleficence/collective stewardship supports no immediate antibiotic; shared decision and option value support delayed prescription under uncertainty; patient-preference deference is limited by professional duties not to prescribe low-value harmful care.

### Construction judgment
Do not use an obviously viral common cold. Fix a modest probability of bacterial infection, stable vital signs, no red flags, and reliable follow-up. The decision should be between no immediate antibiotic with return precautions, a delayed prescription, and immediate antibiotics after shared decision—not between “follow evidence” and blatant malpractice.

**Tentative eligibility:** promising but probably asymmetric. Two public orientations are supportable; professional evidence likely supports two distinct conditional strategies (no antibiotic versus delayed prescription) depending on how the uncertainty threshold is stipulated. Candidate audit required before any executable record.

## M019 — Telemedicine access versus in-person diagnostic safety

### Bounded decision
A service can provide a same-day video visit to a patient for a new, non-emergent problem, while in-person assessment requires substantial travel and delay. The remote encounter can obtain history and visual information but cannot perform a clinically relevant hands-on examination. What rule should govern whether remote care is sufficient or in-person assessment is required?

### Primary sources
- American College of Physicians. *Telemedicine Policy and Practice: A Position Paper From the American College of Physicians.* Ann Intern Med. 2026;179:1030–1032. PMID 42114091. ACP emphasizes telemedicine’s access and continuity benefits alongside safety, equity, and deliberate integration into clinical practice.
- AMA Code of Medical Ethics Opinion 1.2.12, *Ethical Practice in Telemedicine*. Physicians should recognize technological limits and obtain needed examination information through in-person or remote means when necessary for well-grounded recommendations.
- Patient-preference literature:
  - *Patients’ perspectives and preferences toward telemedicine versus in-person visits: a mixed-methods study on 1226 patients.* 2023. PMID 37968639. Most preferred in-person care, with examination/diagnostic accuracy prominent, while telemedicine offered travel, cost and infection-avoidance benefits.
  - Hand-surgery telemedicine study, PMID 34515550: convenience was valued, but inadequate physical examination frequently drove subsequent in-person visits.
  - Neurology mixed-methods comparison, PMCID PMC9068349: patients preferentially chose in-person care when examination was needed, symptoms were worsening, or diagnosis was uncertain, and telemedicine for stable follow-up/no in-person service needs.

### Source-class read
**Affected/public:** strong and genuinely plural by context. Patients value both access/convenience and diagnostic confidence from physical examination; preferences shift with clinical situation rather than forming fixed pro- versus anti-telemedicine camps.

**Expert/professional:** strong and conditional. Professional guidance supports telemedicine when clinically appropriate and requires overcoming modality limitations when the missing examination could matter.

**Framework:** strong. Access/equity and patient burden support remote-first care; nonmaleficence/diagnostic responsibility support risk-triggered in-person assessment; proportionality supports escalation rules rather than categorical bans.

### Construction judgment
The scenario must identify the missing exam element and its plausible consequence. A good candidate is a new unilateral hand/wrist complaint after minor injury in a rural patient: video can show range of motion/swelling but cannot reliably assess focal tenderness, stability, sensation, or vascular findings. Same-day video is available; in-person examination requires a long trip and 48-hour delay; no emergency red flags are currently reported. This creates a real access/safety trade-off without making remote care obviously unsafe.

**Tentative eligibility:** likely `executable-other-profile` after candidate audit; patient evidence may support two orientations, while professional guidance may converge on one risk-triggered hybrid architecture.

## M020 — Clinician duty to work during a dangerous infectious outbreak

### Bounded decision
During a severe infectious outbreak, a hospital faces staffing shortages. What occupational-risk threshold may it require clinicians to accept when appropriate PPE and infection-control measures are available but residual risk remains, and some clinicians have compelling family or personal vulnerability concerns?

### Primary sources
- AMA Code of Medical Ethics Opinion 8.3 / current pandemic-duty guidance. The duty to treat is foundational but not absolute; institutions share duties to protect staff and distribute burdens fairly, while clinicians’ obligation strengthens when their expertise is uniquely needed and alternatives are scarce.
  - https://www.ama-assn.org/councils/council-ethical-judicial-affairs-ceja/what-physicians-duty-treat-during-pandemics
- WHO/ILO. *COVID-19: Occupational health and safety for health workers*, interim guidance 2021. Health systems have responsibilities to control occupational hazards, provide PPE and safe conditions, and recognize worker rights and responsibilities.
  - https://www.who.int/publications/i/WHO-2019-nCoV-HCW_advice-2021-1
- WHO. *Keep health workers safe to keep patients safe* (2020): emphasizes continuous appropriate PPE, infection-control standards, training and worker protection.
- Aitken P et al. *Willingness to Work amongst Australian Frontline Healthcare Workers during Australia’s First Wave of Covid-19 Community Transmission.* 2021. PMID 34496995. PPE availability, family concerns and exposure were major determinants of willingness; many workers did not regard all occupational infection risk as automatically acceptable.
- *Exploring U.S. Nurses’ Perceived Duty to Care During the COVID-19 Pandemic.* 2024. PMID 38598257.
- Ebola-duty survey literature, including PMID 34780288: many nurses endorsed a duty to help despite risk while also reporting competing family obligations.

### Source-class read
**Affected/public:** strong when the affected population is the healthcare workforce itself. Clinicians and nurses express real heterogeneity in perceived duty, acceptable risk, family obligation, and the importance of PPE/organizational reciprocity.

**Expert/professional:** strong but nonabsolute. AMA recognizes a professional duty with limits and reciprocal institutional obligations; WHO/ILO makes worker protection an essential counterpart to service expectations.

**Framework:** strong. Professional-role duty/solidarity supports requiring some elevated risk; reciprocity and occupational justice condition that duty on protection/support; special-obligations and family-duty approaches support exemptions where personal vulnerability or caregiving burden is compelling.

### Construction judgment
Avoid either no PPE (too easy: institution has failed reciprocity) or near-zero residual risk (too easy: ordinary duty). Stipulate appropriate fitted PPE, training and infection-control measures, but a materially elevated residual infection risk compared with ordinary practice. Include one clinician with a high-risk household member or personal medical vulnerability and a staffing shortage that makes exemptions consequential. The decision-maker should be the health system setting a general staffing/exemption policy, not judging one worker’s moral character.

**Tentative eligibility:** strong candidate for `executable-2x2x2` or a recurring asymmetric profile. Affected-worker evidence is plural, professional guidance contains at least two distinguishable architectures (strong duty with reciprocity versus conditional exemption under competing obligations), and framework diversity is genuine. Candidate-level distinctness audit is still required before record construction.

## Batch-B source-first snapshot

### Most promising for later executable construction
- **M012** — affected seekers are genuinely heterogeneous; professional layer probably converges on one non-endorsement/harm-reduction architecture; likely asymmetric.
- **M018** — strong empirical patient and stewardship evidence; delayed prescribing gives a real middle architecture rather than a synthetic compromise.
- **M019** — strong context-sensitive patient evidence plus current ACP/AMA guidance; likely asymmetric hybrid profile.
- **M020** — strongest current candidate for a full executable profile because affected-worker preferences, professional duty limits, and normative frameworks all contain real policy variation.

### High-value scholarly cases likely to remain non-executable for now
- **M011** — strong patient evidence, but not yet two direct public policies.
- **M013** — strong patient demand for disclosure and strong professional consent duties; institutional policy variance should not be inferred from willingness alone.
- **M014** — professional consensus is deliberately strong; affected-public policy evidence is thin.
- **M015** — professional practice is plural, but direct parent evidence on dismissal policy is thin.
- **M016** — strong harm-threshold literature but public evidence does not directly answer the state-intervention policy.
- **M017** — excellent stewardship dilemma, but public evidence is stronger on AMR concern than on withholding/restricting a personally beneficial reserve agent.

These classifications are provisional. The next step is deep case construction with matched concise/detailed representations, followed by a candidate-level audit. No executable records should be authored from this packet alone.
