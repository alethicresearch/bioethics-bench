# M017 — Reserve antimicrobial for present-patient benefit versus stewardship

**Case-family identity:** M017  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** clinical ethics / antimicrobial stewardship / One Health  
**Subdomain:** reserve antibiotics; resistance externalities; present versus future patients  
**Tags:** antimicrobial resistance, stewardship, reserve antibiotic, infectious disease, public health, future patients  
**Research status:** deep case draft; public evidence relevant but policy translation remains indirect  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** hospital antimicrobial-stewardship program together with the treating infectious-disease service.  
**Decision question:** When a reserve antimicrobial is expected to offer a meaningful individual advantage but another active regimen remains clinically defensible, what policy should govern access to the reserve drug?

The case excludes a situation in which the reserve agent is the only microbiologically active therapy, because blanket restriction would then be difficult to defend. It also excludes a trivial situation in which the reserve drug offers no individual advantage. The represented problem is the institutional threshold for using a socially valuable, resistance-sensitive last-line resource when present-patient benefit is real but not overwhelming.

## Concise representation

A hospitalized adult has a serious multidrug-resistant bacterial infection. Susceptibility results show that both an older regimen and a newer WHO Reserve-category antimicrobial are active. The infectious-disease team believes the reserve drug is more likely to achieve cure and is less likely to cause serious renal toxicity, but the older regimen remains a medically reasonable option. The hospital stewardship policy requires special authorization for Reserve antibiotics because unnecessary use contributes to selective pressure and threatens future effectiveness. For this benchmark, assume that one patient’s course cannot be translated into a precise number of future resistant infections, but broader use of the drug contributes to the collective resistance problem the restriction is designed to limit. The hospital must decide what access rule should govern this patient and similar cases.

## Detailed representation

A hospitalized adult has bacteremia from a multidrug-resistant Gram-negative organism and requires definitive antimicrobial therapy. Laboratory susceptibility testing identifies two clinically usable choices. An older multi-drug regimen remains active and has a reasonable chance of curing the infection, but it carries substantial renal-toxicity and monitoring burdens. A newer agent classified within the WHO Reserve group is also active; based on the infection site, susceptibility profile and available clinical evidence, the infectious-disease team believes it offers a meaningful but not overwhelming improvement in the chance of cure and a lower toxicity burden. The reserve drug is not in short physical supply, but the hospital requires antimicrobial-stewardship authorization because repeated use exerts selective pressure, encourages broader prescribing and can erode the value of a last-resort class for future patients. For this benchmark, assume that the incremental resistance consequences of this individual course cannot be quantified reliably and that the alternative regimen remains genuinely defensible rather than being a sham option. The treating team requests the reserve drug. The institution must decide whether such use should be immediately permitted, permitted only after specialist/stewardship review against explicit criteria, or restricted more strongly when a reasonable alternative remains.

## Decision-critical facts

- serious multidrug-resistant infection requiring active treatment;
- both the reserve agent and an older regimen are microbiologically and clinically usable;
- reserve agent offers a meaningful expected individual benefit and lower toxicity, but not an overwhelming or certain advantage;
- older regimen is a genuine medically reasonable alternative;
- reserve drug is not physically unavailable or rationed by stock shortage;
- institutional concern is preservation of effectiveness / selective pressure rather than immediate inventory scarcity;
- one individual course does not have a precisely quantifiable future-harm number;
- decision concerns an institutional access rule for similar patients, not bedside punishment for resistance.

## Explicit uncertainty

The benchmark deliberately leaves the magnitude of the reserve agent’s individual advantage qualitative rather than inventing precise comparative-cure probabilities unsupported by a named infection and trial. It also preserves uncertainty in the causal path from one course to future resistance. Stewardship operates on cumulative and population-level effects, and the Bench should not imply that a single prescription deterministically deprives a future patient of treatment.

The relevant public literature shows that lay people can value resistance preservation, but it is stronger for antibiotic-use preferences generally than for accepting restriction of an antibiotic that offers them personally meaningful benefit. That distinction governs executable eligibility.

## Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral hospital stewardship policy. WHO AWaRe and IDSA/SHEA guidance provide current professional architecture, but the benchmark assumes the hospital may choose among reasonable local authorization rules. There is no legal entitlement to one named drug that resolves the decision.

## Benchmark stipulations

- **Dual-activity stipulation:** both the reserve and older regimens remain clinically active options.
- **Benefit stipulation:** the reserve regimen is expected to improve the individual patient’s outcome and reduce toxicity to a meaningful but non-overwhelming degree.
- **Resistance stipulation:** cumulative broader reserve use contributes to selective pressure, but the future effect of this one course is not precisely quantifiable.
- **Supply stipulation:** the reserve drug is not being rationed because of immediate physical shortage.
- **Institutional stipulation:** a stewardship program can provide timely specialist authorization without dangerous delay.

All appear in both representations.

## Serious policy / position families

- prioritize the present patient and permit reserve therapy whenever the treating team reasonably expects a clinically meaningful advantage;
- require infectious-disease/stewardship preauthorization using explicit criteria that weigh individual incremental benefit, toxicity, resistance risk and alternative options;
- reserve the drug for situations in which alternatives are ineffective, contraindicated or materially inferior, even if a smaller individual advantage would otherwise favor it;
- combine authorization with de-escalation, duration review, diagnostic stewardship and surveillance rather than treating access as a simple yes/no prohibition.

## Public / affected-community evidence

Discrete-choice research on antibiotic treatment shows that contribution to antimicrobial resistance can materially affect lay preferences. In one study, a large preference class placed especially high weight on resistance consequences, demonstrating that future effectiveness is not an expert-only value invisible to patients. Other patient-engagement studies show willingness to participate in stewardship when the rationale is explained.

At the same time, public attitude work also shows strong expectations that clinicians will provide effective treatment for serious infection and variable understanding of how individual use relates to resistance. These sources support a genuine present-benefit-versus-resistance concern but do **not yet** support two direct institutional policies about withholding or requiring authorization for a personally advantageous Reserve agent.

The affected public here also includes future patients who may need effective last-line therapy, but future-patient interests must be represented normatively rather than invented as survey responses.

## Expert / professional recommendations

The WHO AWaRe framework describes Reserve antibiotics as drugs that should be protected for highly specific patients and clinical settings and generally used as last-resort options when alternatives have failed or are unsuitable. The aim is stewardship, not making Reserve drugs inaccessible to patients who genuinely need them.

IDSA/SHEA antimicrobial-stewardship guidance strongly supports preauthorization and/or prospective audit with feedback as core institutional strategies. These recommendations support a conditional authorization architecture more clearly than either unrestricted present-patient use or categorical denial whenever any alternative exists.

Professional evidence therefore likely converges around access-with-stewardship rather than two opposing expert policies. Within that architecture, exact thresholds legitimately vary by organism, infection site, toxicity and local resistance ecology.

## Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Present-patient beneficence:** the clinician has a concrete patient whose chance of cure and toxicity burden may be improved now → diffuse future harms are uncertain while present benefit is identifiable → permit the reserve agent when the expected individual advantage is clinically meaningful.
- **Rule consequentialism / stewardship:** antibiotic effectiveness is a common, exhaustible social resource → individually attractive prescribing patterns can cumulatively undermine future treatment → restrict Reserve use through a general rule that protects the class even when some patients forgo modest incremental benefit.
- **Intergenerational / future-patient justice:** future patients have claims not to inherit avoidably depleted therapeutic options → institutions must account for those otherwise unrepresented interests → require a higher threshold for last-line drug use than ordinary individual treatment choice.
- **Procedural justice / proportionality:** neither the present patient nor future patients should be represented by an absolute rule → transparent specialist review can weigh incremental benefit and externality using consistent criteria → use timely preauthorization rather than unrestricted access or blanket prohibition.

## Construction and representation risks

- claiming one course directly causes a specified future resistant infection;
- making the Reserve agent the only effective therapy and trivializing access;
- making the alternative equivalently effective/toxic and eliminating the individual-benefit conflict;
- treating WHO Reserve classification as a categorical prohibition;
- interpreting general public concern about AMR as consent to any particular bedside restriction;
- treating future patients as if they had been surveyed;
- introducing stock scarcity in one representation only;
- confusing antimicrobial stewardship with conventional resource rationing.
