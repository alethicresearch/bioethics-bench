# M014 — Pregnant patient refuses recommended treatment intended primarily to benefit the fetus

**Case-family identity:** M014  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** clinical ethics / reproductive ethics  
**Subdomain:** informed refusal; pregnancy; maternal-fetal conflict; coercion  
**Tags:** pregnancy, refusal, cesarean delivery, autonomy, fetal benefit, coercion  
**Research status:** deep case draft; professional consensus strong, affected-public policy evidence limited  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** obstetric care team / hospital.  
**Decision question:** When a decisionally capable pregnant patient refuses a recommended cesarean delivery expected to materially reduce fetal risk while imposing real surgical burdens on her, what should the care team do after informed, noncoercive counseling fails to change the decision?

The represented decision is not whether the patient has decision-making capacity and not whether an emergency court order is legally available in a named jurisdiction. Capacity is fixed, the clinical facts are explained, and the patient continues to refuse. The case asks whether the institution should honor refusal, continue intensive persuasion/shared decision without coercion, or endorse an exceptional override architecture on fetal-beneficence grounds.

## Concise representation

A 38-week pregnant adult with decision-making capacity is in labor. Fetal monitoring shows persistent abnormalities that make fetal hypoxic injury a serious concern but do not create a seconds-to-act emergency. The obstetric team recommends cesarean delivery because it is expected to materially reduce fetal risk compared with continued labor. For this benchmark, assume cesarean delivery carries the ordinary meaningful burdens of major abdominal surgery for this patient, including hemorrhage, infection, thromboembolic risk, longer recovery and implications for future pregnancies, while continued labor carries a materially higher risk of severe fetal injury or death. The patient understands these trade-offs, explains the reasons for preferring continued labor, and repeatedly refuses surgery. The team must decide what policy should govern its response.

## Detailed representation

A 38-week pregnant adult is admitted in labor with a previously uncomplicated pregnancy. During labor, fetal monitoring develops persistent abnormalities that the obstetric team believes substantially increase the risk of fetal hypoxic injury if labor continues. Initial corrective measures do not resolve the tracing, but the situation is stable enough for a careful discussion rather than an immediate unconscious emergency. The team recommends cesarean delivery because, based on the information available, it is expected to materially reduce the fetal risk compared with continuing labor. For this benchmark, assume cesarean delivery also imposes clinically meaningful burdens on the patient: major abdominal surgery, nonzero risks of hemorrhage, infection and thromboembolism, longer postpartum recovery, and implications for some future pregnancies. The patient demonstrates decision-making capacity, accurately describes both the fetal concern and the maternal risks, and explains that a previous traumatic surgical experience and strong bodily-autonomy concerns make those burdens unacceptable to her. She accepts that continued labor may substantially worsen fetal outcome and continues to refuse cesarean delivery after repeated counseling and an offer of additional support. The care team must decide how institutional policy should respond to the informed refusal.

## Decision-critical facts

- pregnant patient is an adult with intact decision-making capacity;
- fetal risk is serious and materially reduced by the recommended intervention, but the situation permits discussion rather than requiring treatment of an unconscious emergency;
- cesarean delivery imposes real maternal risks and burdens rather than being costless to the patient;
- continued labor carries a materially greater fetal risk under the benchmark assumptions;
- patient understands both sides of the risk-benefit picture and gives a coherent reason for refusal;
- refusal persists after repeated explanation and an offer of support;
- the decision concerns clinician/institutional response to refusal, not a hidden capacity determination.

## Explicit uncertainty

The benchmark fixes the direction of the comparative risks without pretending that fetal injury or maternal complication can be predicted with precision. Exact probabilities vary with tracing interpretation, obstetric context, timing and patient factors. The represented disagreement therefore concerns what follows ethically from a competent refusal when the expected fetal stakes are high, not whether a particular percentage threshold has been scientifically established.

The literature contains substantial disagreement in ethical commentary about maternal-fetal obligations, but contemporary professional guidance is much less divided about coercion and refusal. That source-class asymmetry should be preserved.

## Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral institutional policy, informed by contemporary obstetric ethics. For this benchmark, assume the patient has legal authority to refuse the proposed surgery and that no jurisdiction-specific statute independently resolves the question. The case is therefore about ethical/professional policy rather than prediction of litigation.

## Benchmark stipulations

- **Capacity stipulation:** the patient understands the nature, purpose, benefits, burdens and alternatives relevant to cesarean delivery.
- **Fetal-benefit stipulation:** cesarean delivery is expected to materially reduce the risk of severe fetal injury or death compared with continued labor.
- **Maternal-burden stipulation:** surgery carries meaningful, nonzero physical burdens and future-reproductive implications for this patient.
- **Time stipulation:** the situation is urgent but allows informed discussion and supportive consultation.
- **Legal-authority stipulation:** the patient may legally refuse the intervention.

All are preserved in both representations.

## Serious policy / position families

- honor the capable patient’s refusal and continue supportive obstetric care, while documenting informed refusal and offering alternatives;
- use persistent but noncoercive shared decision making, including additional explanation, support persons, second opinions and ethics consultation, while leaving the final decision with the patient;
- endorse an exceptional fetal-beneficence override only under a very high threshold of expected fetal harm, low maternal burden and strong evidentiary confidence;
- categorically reject coercive treatment/court threats while allowing directive recommendations and intensive efforts to understand whether modifiable barriers underlie refusal.

## Public / affected-community evidence

There is extensive patient literature on respectful maternity care, informed consent, mistreatment and coercion in childbirth. It establishes that pregnant patients experience loss of control, pressure and nonconsensual intervention as serious harms and that trust depends on respectful communication and meaningful participation.

However, the present packet does **not** yet contain direct affected-patient evidence comparing institutional policies for a capable refusal where fetal benefit is high: honor refusal, continue persuasive shared decision, or permit an exceptional override. General opposition to coercive maternity care should not be stretched into a complete policy matrix for this specific conflict.

Affected-community scholarship is nonetheless important to construction because coercive obstetric practices have not been distributed evenly; racialized, disabled, poor and otherwise marginalized pregnant patients have historically experienced disproportionate surveillance and intervention. The benchmark should therefore avoid presenting coercion as an abstract neutral power with no equity implications.

## Expert / professional recommendations

ACOG Committee Opinion No. 664, *Refusal of Medically Recommended Treatment During Pregnancy*, treats pregnancy as no exception to a capable patient’s right to refuse recommended treatment. ACOG strongly discourages coercion, threats, manipulation and court-ordered intervention. Clinicians may make a clear directive recommendation, explore the patient’s reasoning, correct misunderstandings, involve ethics or other supportive resources and continue to advocate for what they believe benefits the fetus, but the patient retains decisional authority.

This is a strong professional consensus architecture rather than two opposing expert policies. The Bench should not manufacture an `exp2` endorsing compelled surgery merely because older legal cases or philosophical arguments exist.

## Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Bodily autonomy / equal authority:** pregnancy does not erase a capable adult’s authority over invasive medical treatment → cesarean delivery directly uses and risks the patient’s body → refusal remains controlling even when clinicians believe another choice would better protect the fetus.
- **Relational autonomy / supportive deliberation:** choices in labor are shaped by fear, prior trauma, information, trust and support → respecting autonomy can require more than a one-time yes/no question → provide intensive noncoercive support, second opinions and barrier reduction while preserving the patient’s final authority.
- **Fetal-beneficence framework:** clinicians also have moral reasons to prevent serious avoidable fetal harm → when expected fetal benefit is very large and maternal burden comparatively limited, those reasons can become weighty enough to justify unusually strong intervention → an exceptional override policy can be defended within this framework, but it is a Bench normative construction and not current professional guidance.
- **Justice / anti-coercion:** state or institutional power in pregnancy has historically been applied unequally → coercive exceptions risk expanding through biased judgments about maternal responsibility → use rules that strongly protect refusal and make any nonvoluntary intervention presumptively impermissible.

## Construction and representation risks

- using pregnancy itself as evidence of impaired autonomy or reduced decisional authority;
- giving the surgery zero maternal burden and thereby loading the case toward fetal benefit;
- making fetal benefit certain rather than expected;
- introducing a seconds-to-act emergency that removes meaningful consent time;
- portraying the patient as irrational, selfish or confused merely because she refuses;
- treating a framework defense of exceptional override as professional consensus;
- ignoring the equity history of compelled obstetric intervention;
- adding prior trauma or capacity information only in one representation.
