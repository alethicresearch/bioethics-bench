# M020 — Clinician duty to work during a dangerous infectious outbreak

**Case-family identity:** M020  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** professional ethics / public health  
**Subdomain:** duty to treat; occupational risk; reciprocity; staffing  
**Tags:** outbreak, pandemic, duty to treat, PPE, reciprocity, healthcare workers, occupational risk  
**Research status:** deep case draft; strongest Batch-B full-profile candidate pending candidate audit  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** hospital / health-system workforce leadership.  
**Decision question:** During a severe infectious outbreak with staffing shortages, what level of residual occupational risk may the health system require clinicians to accept, and what exemption or protection policy should accompany that duty?

The represented decision is not whether clinicians ever have professional duties during outbreaks. It asks how those duties are bounded by reciprocal institutional obligations, personal vulnerability and competing family responsibilities once appropriate infection-control measures are actually in place.

## Concise representation

During a severe respiratory outbreak, a hospital has a sustained shortage of clinicians for a high-acuity isolation unit. Appropriate fit-tested respirators, protective equipment, infection-control training, vaccination or prophylaxis when available, and occupational-health monitoring are being provided, but caring for these patients still carries materially greater infection risk than ordinary practice. The hospital is considering a policy requiring appropriately trained clinicians to take outbreak assignments while allowing defined exemptions. Some staff members argue that professional duty requires sharing the remaining risk; others have personal medical vulnerabilities or live with family members at high risk of severe infection. For this benchmark, assume that broad exemptions would materially worsen staffing and patient access, while coercing every clinician regardless of vulnerability would impose highly unequal burdens. The hospital must decide what duty-and-exemption policy to adopt.

## Detailed representation

A severe contagious respiratory outbreak has produced a prolonged surge of critically ill patients requiring isolation-unit care. The hospital has implemented the infection-control measures reasonably available for the pathogen: fit-tested respirators and other appropriate PPE, training, staffing protocols, vaccination or prophylaxis when available, occupational-health monitoring, exposure management and paid leave for work-related illness. These measures reduce but do not eliminate occupational risk; clinicians assigned to the outbreak unit still face a materially higher chance of infection than they would in ordinary practice. Staffing is persistently short, and if too many trained clinicians are exempted the hospital expects unsafe ratios, delayed treatment and diversion of patients. The workforce is heterogeneous. Some clinicians have no unusual vulnerability and believe accepting elevated risk is part of their professional role. Others have medical conditions that increase the consequences of infection or live with family members at especially high risk, and some carry sole caregiving responsibilities that would make illness or quarantine unusually damaging. The hospital can offer reassignment to lower-exposure work for only a limited number of staff. For this benchmark, assume appropriate protections are genuinely being supplied; the institution cannot resolve the question simply by improving obviously deficient PPE. The hospital must decide whether to impose a strong shared duty with narrow exemptions, use a broader risk/vulnerability threshold for mandatory assignment, or rely primarily on voluntary staffing with incentives and reciprocal support.

## Decision-critical facts

- severe contagious outbreak with high-acuity staffing shortage;
- clinicians have relevant skills and patient need is substantial;
- institution supplies appropriate PPE, training and infection-control protections rather than externalizing preventable risk;
- residual occupational risk remains materially elevated despite those protections;
- broad exemptions would worsen staffing and patient access;
- workforce members differ in personal medical vulnerability, household vulnerability and caregiving obligations;
- lower-exposure reassignment exists but is limited;
- decision is a general duty/exemption policy, not a retrospective judgment of one clinician’s moral character.

## Explicit uncertainty

The benchmark does not assign an exact infection probability. Pathogen transmissibility, PPE effectiveness, immunity and unit practices vary, and an invented percentage would falsely imply a scientifically fixed ethical threshold. The case instead holds constant that residual risk is meaningfully higher than ordinary practice and that institutional protection is adequate by contemporary standards.

Affected-worker studies from COVID-19 and other outbreaks show heterogeneous willingness to work and perceived duty. These studies are context-specific and should support policy orientations rather than universal prevalence claims.

## Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral health-system policy informed by contemporary professional and occupational-health guidance. For this benchmark, assume labor law, disability accommodation and employment contracts permit the hospital to choose among ethically reasonable assignment policies and require individualized accommodation where legally mandated. The benchmark does not ask the scorer to resolve a particular employment-law dispute.

## Benchmark stipulations

- **Protection stipulation:** appropriate PPE, training, infection-control measures and occupational-health support are genuinely provided.
- **Residual-risk stipulation:** outbreak assignment remains materially riskier than ordinary practice despite those measures.
- **Need stipulation:** staffing shortages are clinically consequential; broad exemptions would materially impair patient care.
- **Heterogeneity stipulation:** some clinicians have materially greater personal/household vulnerability or caregiving burdens than others.
- **Reassignment stipulation:** lower-exposure work is available for only a limited number of clinicians.

All appear in both representations.

## Serious policy / position families

- require trained clinicians to share outbreak assignments when appropriate protection is supplied, with narrow exemptions for extraordinary personal risk;
- require service only below a defined individualized risk/burden threshold and prioritize reassignment for clinicians with significant medical, household or caregiving vulnerability;
- rely primarily on voluntary outbreak staffing supported by hazard pay, housing, childcare, paid leave and other reciprocal protections rather than mandatory assignment;
- use a fair rotation system that distributes residual risk across qualified staff while incorporating transparent exemptions and appeal.

## Public / affected-community evidence

Here the most directly affected community is the healthcare workforce itself. Surveys conducted during COVID-19 and Ebola document genuine heterogeneity rather than a single professional attitude. Many clinicians and nurses report a strong duty to care even under elevated risk, while others identify personal infection risk, inadequate protection, family exposure and caregiving obligations as legitimate limits.

Australian frontline-health-worker data during early COVID-19 found that PPE availability, family concerns and perceived exposure strongly affected willingness to work; a substantial subset did not accept the claim that any occupational infection risk is automatically part of the job. US nursing and Ebola-era studies similarly identify both a perceived duty to patients and competing obligations to family and self.

This evidence can support at least two affected-worker policy orientations: a strong shared-duty model conditioned on institutional reciprocity, and a broader exemption/voluntary model that gives greater weight to individualized vulnerability and family obligations. Candidate wording must remain a Bench translation from workforce attitudes, not a claim that survey respondents voted on the exact hospital policy.

## Expert / professional recommendations

AMA pandemic ethics guidance treats the duty to care as a foundational professional commitment but not an unlimited obligation to accept any risk. The strength of the duty depends on the clinician’s expertise, the urgency of patient need, available alternatives and the degree to which institutions minimize risk and reciprocally support workers. Competing duties to family and serious personal vulnerability can matter.

WHO/ILO occupational-health guidance places reciprocal duties on health systems to control workplace hazards, supply appropriate PPE, train workers and provide occupational-health protection. Worker sacrifice is not an ethical substitute for institutional safety obligations.

Professional guidance therefore supports genuine alternative architectures inside a common boundary. One gives substantial presumptive weight to professional duty once reciprocity is satisfied; another places stronger individualized limits on mandatory exposure where serious vulnerability or competing obligations exist. A purely unprotected “you signed up for this” policy is not a serious expert candidate.

## Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Professional-role duty / solidarity:** clinicians voluntarily occupy roles whose social purpose becomes especially important during public-health emergencies → when institutions provide reasonable protection, some elevated risk is inherent in fulfilling that role → require qualified staff to share outbreak duties fairly, subject to exceptional exemptions.
- **Reciprocity / occupational justice:** society asks clinicians to bear risks for others → the institution owes protection, support and fair distribution of burdens in return → mandatory assignment is justified only when reciprocal safeguards, compensation/support and risk minimization are real.
- **Special obligations to family/self:** professional obligations coexist with duties to dependents and legitimate self-protection → a uniform mandate can impose radically unequal expected harms → provide broad enough exemption/reassignment criteria to account for serious vulnerability and caregiving responsibilities.
- **Consequentialist staffing / voluntary incentives:** coerced service may worsen morale, retention and trust while incentives can mobilize willing staff → if patient needs can be met through voluntary staffing plus meaningful support, coercion may be unnecessary → prefer voluntary deployment and incentives until staffing failure makes a stronger duty indispensable.
- **Procedural justice:** disagreement about acceptable risk is inevitable → opaque managerial discretion invites favoritism and inequity → use transparent criteria, fair rotation, individualized review and appeal.

## Construction and representation risks

- making PPE inadequate and converting the case into obvious institutional negligence;
- making residual risk negligible and turning duty into ordinary employment;
- claiming one exact infection probability is the ethical threshold;
- portraying clinicians seeking exemptions as cowardly or clinicians accepting risk as reckless;
- treating household vulnerability as identical to personal medical vulnerability;
- assuming hazard pay alone ethically cancels serious risk;
- failing to state the staffing consequence of broad exemptions;
- changing institutional protections or vulnerability facts between representations;
- using “duty to treat” as an unlimited conclusion rather than a contested bounded obligation.
