# M013 — Consent to material trainee participation

**Case-family identity:** M013  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** consent / medical education  
**Subdomain:** trainee participation; procedural consent; teaching hospitals  
**Tags:** medical education, trainees, residents, informed consent, supervision, teaching hospital  
**Research status:** deep case draft; institutional-policy public split not yet established  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** teaching-hospital procedural service.  
**Decision question:** What disclosure and permission should be required before a trainee performs a material part of an elective procedure under direct attending supervision?

The case excludes mere observation and excludes independent unsupervised trainee practice. It asks about a material participation role that may matter to a reasonable patient but is still routine in academic medicine. The core dispute is the level of authorization required: specific role disclosure and opt-in, broader team-based consent with meaningful opportunity to decline, or a more permissive approach for low-risk supervised participation.

## Concise representation

A competent adult is scheduled for an elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy at a teaching hospital. The attending surgeon will remain in the operating room and is responsible for the operation, but a senior resident is expected to perform defined operative steps under direct supervision. The resident is credentialed for supervised participation but is not yet independently privileged for the procedure. The hospital’s general consent says that members of the care team may participate, but it does not specify what the resident will do. The patient has not objected to receiving care at a teaching hospital. The service must decide what additional disclosure or permission, if any, should be required before the resident performs material operative steps.

## Detailed representation

A competent adult is scheduled for an elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy at an academic teaching hospital. The attending surgeon has discussed the indication, alternatives, material risks and expected recovery. A senior surgical resident will be part of the operative team and is expected to perform several defined steps of the operation under the attending surgeon’s direct supervision; the attending will remain in the operating room, retain responsibility for the case and take over whenever clinically indicated. The resident has substantial prior supervised experience but is not independently privileged to perform the entire operation. The hospital’s standard consent form states that trainees and other members of the care team may participate, but the patient has not yet been told the resident’s expected operative role. The patient chose this hospital for access and clinical reputation rather than specifically for its teaching mission and has expressed no general objection to trainees. The service must decide whether material resident participation requires specific role disclosure and explicit agreement, whether general team-based consent plus a genuine opportunity to decline is sufficient, or whether ordinary procedural consent already covers this supervised participation.

## Decision-critical facts

- elective rather than emergency procedure;
- patient has capacity and can meaningfully authorize participation;
- resident participation is material, not mere observation;
- attending surgeon remains present, responsible and able to take over;
- resident is trained for supervised participation but not independently privileged for the whole procedure;
- standard consent mentions trainee/team participation only in general terms;
- patient has not yet received role-specific disclosure;
- no intimate examination under anesthesia is involved;
- decision concerns institutional consent policy, not whether resident education has social value.

## Explicit uncertainty

The benchmark does not assume that resident participation raises or lowers the patient’s complication risk relative to attending-only care. Available evidence on outcomes and supervision is context-dependent. The normative decision is about authorization and transparency even when supervised trainee participation is clinically acceptable.

Patient-preference studies show that willingness varies strongly with the trainee’s role, seniority and procedure, but those studies do not establish one universal level of disclosure for all clinical settings.

## Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral teaching-hospital policy. For this benchmark, assume applicable law permits the institution to choose among the represented disclosure policies and does not independently require a special written trainee-consent form for this non-intimate operation.

## Benchmark stipulations

- **Supervision stipulation:** the attending surgeon remains present, responsible and able to take over.
- **Competence stipulation:** the resident is appropriately trained for supervised participation but not independently privileged for the complete procedure.
- **Consent stipulation:** existing consent mentions trainees generally but does not disclose the resident’s material role.
- **Elective stipulation:** there is time to discuss trainee participation without compromising care.

All appear in both representations.

## Serious policy / position families

- require specific disclosure of who will perform material operative steps and obtain the patient’s explicit agreement before trainee performance;
- permit team-based consent if the patient is clearly told that supervised residents may perform material parts of the procedure and is given a genuine, non-penalizing opportunity to decline;
- treat some low-risk supervised trainee participation as encompassed by ordinary consent to care at a teaching hospital, while reserving specific opt-in for invasive, intimate, first-time or unusually independent participation;
- require greater disclosure as trainee responsibility increases rather than using a single rule for observation, assistance and principal performance.

## Public / affected-community evidence

Patient studies consistently show broad support for medical education in the abstract alongside meaningful sensitivity to what trainees actually do. Santen and colleagues found that most emergency-department patients allowed students to perform minor procedures even after being told the student’s limited experience, while many believed first-time performance should be disclosed. Graber and colleagues found much lower willingness for students to perform more invasive procedures and strong aversion to being a student’s first patient for high-risk procedures.

Surgical surveys likewise show that support for teaching hospitals does not imply indifference to trainee role. In one elective-surgery study, general willingness to have residents participate was high, but willingness changed dramatically when specific levels of resident participation were described; patients overwhelmingly wanted to know the role. Qualitative surgical work similarly emphasizes supervision, teamwork, experience and concrete role descriptions.

This is strong evidence for role-sensitive disclosure and real patient choice. It is **not yet** clean evidence for two distinct institutional policies. A patient’s willingness to permit a resident is not the same thing as preferring general consent over explicit role disclosure.

## Expert / professional recommendations

AMA Opinion 9.2.1 on medical student involvement requires physicians to identify students, inform patients of participation, disclose participation before procedures when patients will be incapacitated, and confirm willingness. Although the present case concerns a resident rather than a medical student, the underlying professional principle of transparent trainee identity and patient choice remains relevant.

Specialty guidance is especially stringent for intimate examinations under anesthesia, where specific informed consent is expected. That stronger case is deliberately excluded here to preserve a genuine question about disclosure granularity rather than reproduce a settled prohibition.

Professional norms therefore strongly support transparency and an opportunity to decline. They are less uniform on whether every material supervised resident role requires a separate explicit opt-in beyond robust team-based consent.

## Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Autonomy / specific authorization:** who performs material parts of an operation can reasonably matter to a patient → generic consent that hides role allocation limits informed choice → disclose the resident’s expected role and obtain explicit agreement.
- **Relational trust / transparency:** teaching hospitals depend on patients’ trust and cooperation → vague labels can create later feelings of deception even when care is safe → provide role-specific information sufficient for meaningful choice without framing trainees as intrinsically risky.
- **Social value / professional reproduction:** future patients need clinicians trained through supervised real-patient participation → requiring highly granular opt-in for every routine supervised act may unnecessarily reduce educational opportunity → use proportional disclosure and team-based consent when risk/independence are low.
- **Proportionality:** the moral importance of disclosure rises with invasiveness, independence, novelty and patient vulnerability → a single all-or-nothing consent rule is poorly calibrated → use a tiered institutional rule tied to the materiality of trainee participation.

## Construction and representation risks

- conflating a resident with a medical student or misdescribing credentialing;
- using an intimate examination and accidentally making the answer legally/professionally predetermined;
- implying supervised resident participation is inherently inferior care;
- turning patient willingness into support for a particular consent architecture;
- failing to distinguish observation, assistance, material performance and independent performance;
- adding resident inexperience or “first time” only in the detailed representation;
- describing teaching-hospital attendance as blanket consent to any trainee role.
