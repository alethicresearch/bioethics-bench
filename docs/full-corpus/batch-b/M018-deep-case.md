# M018 — Patient-requested antibiotics under genuine diagnostic uncertainty

**Case-family identity:** M018  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** clinical ethics / antimicrobial stewardship  
**Subdomain:** outpatient prescribing; diagnostic uncertainty; delayed prescription  
**Tags:** antibiotics, respiratory infection, delayed prescribing, shared decision making, stewardship

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** outpatient clinician / primary-care service.  
**Decision question:** When bacterial infection is possible but not likely, immediate antibiotics offer low expected benefit, the patient strongly prefers treatment, and reliable follow-up is available, what prescribing strategy should the clinician use?

The case avoids an obviously viral illness, because that would make immediate prescribing a straw option. It also avoids a high-probability bacterial infection that clearly warrants antibiotics. The bounded decision is whether to prescribe immediately, use a delayed/back-up prescription, or withhold antibiotics with safety-net follow-up under genuine but modest diagnostic uncertainty.

## Concise representation

A generally healthy adult presents with several days of an uncomplicated respiratory illness. Examination and available testing show no red flags; bacterial infection is possible but is judged unlikely enough that immediate antibiotics are expected to provide little average benefit. The patient strongly prefers antibiotics because a previous similar illness improved after treatment and is worried about worsening before returning to work. For this benchmark, assume follow-up within 24–48 hours is reliable and the patient can recognize defined warning signs. The clinician must choose between no immediate antibiotic with return precautions, a delayed prescription to use if symptoms persist or worsen under specified conditions, or an immediate antibiotic prescription after discussing benefits and harms.

## Detailed representation

A generally healthy adult presents to primary care after several days of cough, congestion, facial pressure and fatigue. Vital signs are stable, oxygenation is normal, there is no severe systemic illness, and the examination does not identify a complication requiring urgent treatment. The available clinical picture leaves a real possibility of bacterial infection, but the clinician judges it more likely that the illness will improve without antibiotics and expects only a small average benefit from immediate treatment. The patient asks strongly for an antibiotic, explaining that a prior respiratory illness seemed to improve after antibiotics and that missing additional work would be difficult. The clinician explains that improvement after a previous prescription does not establish that antibiotics caused recovery, and discusses adverse effects and antimicrobial resistance. For this benchmark, assume the patient has reliable access to follow-up within 24–48 hours, can understand written safety-net instructions, and has no major immune compromise or other feature that would lower the threshold for treatment. The clinician must decide whether to withhold antibiotics with return precautions, issue a delayed/back-up prescription with clear activation criteria, or prescribe immediately after shared decision making.

## Decision-critical facts

- uncomplicated respiratory syndrome with genuine but modest diagnostic uncertainty;
- no red flags or high-risk host features;
- bacterial disease is possible, but immediate antibiotics have low expected average benefit;
- patient has a strong preference for treatment and practical concerns about prolonged symptoms;
- prior perceived response to antibiotics does not establish causation;
- reliable short-interval follow-up and safety-netting are available;
- decision concerns prescribing strategy, not refusal to provide care.

## Explicit uncertainty

The benchmark intentionally avoids assigning a precise probability of bacterial infection. Diagnostic thresholds vary by syndrome and test availability, and the ethical interest lies in how to act under a bounded uncertainty that is neither negligible nor high enough to make immediate treatment clearly standard.

Delayed-prescribing trials cover multiple respiratory syndromes and settings. Their results support the architecture but should not be read as proving that delayed prescribing is superior for every individual presentation.

## Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral outpatient practice. Contemporary outpatient stewardship guidance and randomized evidence provide the empirical background. For this benchmark, all three strategies are assumed legally available; the question is clinical-ethical policy rather than regulatory authority.

## Benchmark stipulations

- **Risk stipulation:** no red flags or major host factors require immediate antibiotics.
- **Uncertainty stipulation:** bacterial infection remains possible but is not the most likely explanation.
- **Benefit stipulation:** immediate antibiotics have low expected average benefit in this presentation.
- **Follow-up stipulation:** reliable reassessment within 24–48 hours is available.
- **Safety-net stipulation:** the patient can understand and act on explicit warning signs / delayed-prescription activation criteria.

All appear in both representations.

## Serious policy / position families

- do not prescribe immediately; explain the low expected benefit, provide symptomatic care and explicit return precautions;
- use a delayed/back-up prescription with clearly specified criteria or timing, preserving access if the illness persists or evolves while reducing unnecessary use;
- prescribe immediately after shared decision when uncertainty is clinically meaningful and the informed patient places high value on even a small possibility of shorter illness;
- use diagnostic testing or reassessment to refine the decision rather than allowing patient demand alone to determine prescribing.

## Public / affected-community evidence

Patient-expectation studies show real heterogeneity. Some patients attending with respiratory symptoms expect or prefer antibiotics and may interpret receiving a prescription as evidence of good care. Other patients accept non-antibiotic treatment when clinicians explain why antibiotics are unlikely to help and provide a positive treatment plan rather than a bare refusal.

Recent US primary-care work suggests that underestimation of antibiotic harms contributes to expectations for antibiotics. Public-attitude studies also show concern about antimicrobial resistance alongside incomplete understanding of when antibiotics are useful.

Delayed prescribing is especially important for candidate construction because it is not merely a philosophical compromise: randomized and systematic-review evidence shows that many patients accept it, it substantially reduces actual antibiotic use relative to immediate prescribing, and satisfaction is generally similar to immediate treatment in studied uncomplicated respiratory infections. This can support a patient-centered orientation that values having a treatment option available if symptoms fail to improve.

The public layer may therefore support two actionable orientations: immediate-access preference under uncertainty and delayed/no-immediate treatment with reassurance. Candidate wording must distinguish measured patient expectations from Bench policy translation.

## Expert / professional recommendations

CDC outpatient stewardship emphasizes commitment to evidence-based prescribing, appropriate diagnosis and communication strategies that reduce unnecessary antibiotic use. When antibiotics are not indicated, patient demand alone is not a professional reason to prescribe.

Systematic reviews and randomized trials support delayed/back-up prescriptions as a legitimate strategy for selected respiratory infections when clinicians judge that immediate antibiotics are unnecessary but uncertainty, patient concern or follow-up considerations make a contingency plan useful. Delayed prescribing sharply reduces antibiotic consumption compared with immediate prescribing and generally preserves satisfaction, with similar complication outcomes in appropriately selected uncomplicated infections.

Professional evidence therefore plausibly supports at least two distinguishable clinical strategies in the represented uncertainty range: no immediate prescription with safety net, and a delayed prescription with activation criteria. It does not support immediate antibiotics merely because the patient prefers them.

## Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Nonmaleficence / stewardship:** antibiotics expose the patient to adverse effects and contribute cumulatively to resistance while expected benefit is low → clinicians should not prescribe low-value treatment solely to satisfy demand → use no immediate antibiotic with explanation and safety net.
- **Shared decision / option value:** the diagnosis remains uncertain and follow-up is reliable → a delayed prescription gives the patient a bounded contingency without committing to immediate drug use → offer a back-up prescription with clear activation conditions.
- **Respect for informed preference:** some patients reasonably place high value on small possible symptom benefit and avoiding future access barriers → where immediate treatment remains within a clinically defensible range, preference may break a close call → permit immediate prescribing only when professional uncertainty truly leaves that option reasonable.
- **Public-health externality:** antibiotic decisions affect people beyond the present encounter through resistance → private preference cannot fully internalize the social cost → use institutional prescribing standards that constrain immediate treatment when expected benefit is low.

## Construction and representation risks

- using a clearly viral illness and turning immediate antibiotics into obvious bad practice;
- assigning a precise bacterial probability unsupported by the scenario;
- allowing patient preference to redefine an option as professionally reasonable when it is not;
- treating delayed prescribing as identical to immediate prescribing because a prescription exists;
- omitting reliable follow-up in one representation;
- presenting antimicrobial resistance as a deterministic harm from one course;
- changing immune status or red flags between concise and detailed forms;
- assuming prior improvement proves antibiotics worked.
