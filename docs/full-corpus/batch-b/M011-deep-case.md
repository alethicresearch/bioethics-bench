# M011 — Expanded access to an investigational intervention

**Case-family identity:** M011  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** clinical ethics / research translation  
**Subdomain:** expanded access; investigational drugs; compassionate use  
**Tags:** expanded access, compassionate use, investigational drug, evidence generation, access justice  
**Research status:** deep case draft; direct patient evidence strong but executable public-policy structure unresolved  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** treating oncology service together with the institution’s expanded-access process and the manufacturer supplying the investigational drug.  
**Decision question:** When a seriously ill patient cannot enroll in a relevant trial but meets the clinical conditions for possible expanded access, what policy should govern whether the institution and manufacturer pursue investigational treatment outside the trial?

The represented decision is not whether expanded access is legally possible in general and not whether all dying patients have a right to any experimental drug. It is the bounded institutional decision about a plausible individual-patient request where no satisfactory approved therapy remains, clinical-trial participation is genuinely unavailable, and the product has enough early evidence to make use medically arguable but not enough evidence to establish efficacy.

## Concise representation

A 46-year-old adult with metastatic cancer has progressed after all standard therapies. A phase 1/early phase 2 investigational drug targeting the patient’s tumor alteration has shown acceptable short-term safety and a preliminary response signal, but its efficacy remains uncertain and it is not approved. The patient cannot enter the ongoing trial because no recruiting site is accessible and the sponsor confirms that trial enrollment is not otherwise available. The oncologist believes there is a plausible chance of benefit and no comparable satisfactory alternative. The manufacturer can supply an individual course without reducing drug already allocated to enrolled trial participants, but doing so requires additional staff, monitoring, regulatory and manufacturing work. The patient understands the uncertainty and requests treatment. The institution and manufacturer must decide what policy should govern whether expanded access is pursued.

## Detailed representation

A 46-year-old adult with metastatic solid-tumor cancer has received and progressed through all standard therapies appropriate to the disease. Molecular testing identifies an alteration targeted by an investigational drug now in an early efficacy trial. Completed phase 1 data provide a clinically usable safety picture and early trial data show a response signal in a small number of patients with similar tumors, but the evidence is too limited to establish effectiveness or predict this patient’s outcome. The patient is medically fit enough to receive the drug and has no comparable satisfactory approved therapy. The nearest recruiting trial site is not accessible to the patient and the sponsor confirms that no alternative enrollment pathway is available. The treating oncologist judges that potential benefit could justify the known and unknown risks, and the patient demonstrates understanding that treatment may not help and may cause serious unexpected toxicity. The manufacturer has enough product to supply this patient without taking drug already allocated to enrolled trial participants, but each non-trial use requires additional manufacturing release, monitoring, regulatory documentation and staff effort. The patient asks the oncologist and manufacturer to pursue individual-patient expanded access. The institution and manufacturer must decide what policy should govern such requests.

## Decision-critical facts

- serious progressive disease with no comparable satisfactory approved therapy;
- relevant clinical trial exists but this patient cannot enroll through an available route;
- early safety evidence is adequate to make treatment medically discussable;
- preliminary efficacy signal exists but effectiveness remains genuinely uncertain;
- patient has decision-making capacity and understands uncertainty and risk;
- treating oncologist believes expected benefit may justify risk;
- requested supply will not remove product already allocated to current trial participants;
- access still creates real manufacturing, monitoring, regulatory and staff burden;
- the question concerns institutional/manufacturer policy, not a legal entitlement.

## Explicit uncertainty

The benchmark preserves uncertainty about individual benefit, uncommon toxicity, durability of any response and the generalizability of early trial data. It does not preserve uncertainty about whether the patient has exhausted satisfactory approved options or whether ordinary trial enrollment is available; those are fixed so the normative dispute is actually about non-trial access.

The literature also contains uncertainty about how expanded access affects drug-development efficiency and equity across institutions. Those empirical uncertainties should be represented in metadata and policy reasoning, not converted into invented numerical effects.

## Jurisdiction and time strategy

The case uses the contemporary US expanded-access architecture as its factual background because FDA criteria are unusually explicit and reconstructable. For this benchmark, assume the relevant regulatory pathway is legally available if the institution, physician and manufacturer choose to pursue it and the required review is obtained. The benchmark decision is therefore not resolved by law.

## Benchmark stipulations

- **No-alternative stipulation:** no comparable satisfactory approved therapy remains.
- **Trial-access stipulation:** the patient cannot enroll in the relevant trial through an available route.
- **Evidence stipulation:** completed early studies provide an acceptable safety basis and a plausible efficacy signal, but efficacy is unproven.
- **Supply stipulation:** providing one course will not take drug already allocated to currently enrolled trial participants.
- **Capacity stipulation:** the patient understands that benefit is uncertain and serious unknown harms remain possible.

All stipulations appear in both representations.

## Serious policy / position families

- pursue expanded access when the patient satisfies defined seriousness, no-alternative, risk-benefit and trial-unavailability criteria and the treating clinician recommends it;
- prefer trial-based access and decline non-trial treatment unless additional institutional criteria are met, such as stronger evidence, standardized review, available follow-up infrastructure or equitable access rules;
- permit expanded access through a transparent independent review process that incorporates medical plausibility, patient circumstances, product-development effects and fairness across requests;
- discuss expanded access as a real option but do not frame access as an entitlement against a manufacturer or as equivalent to approved treatment.

## Public / affected-community evidence

Bunnik and colleagues interviewed/focus-grouped 39 patients with chronic or life-threatening diseases about investigational options and expanded access. Patients had heterogeneous knowledge but commonly described careful attention to safety, efficacy, quality of life and equality of access. They wanted clinicians to discuss relevant non-standard options rather than assuming that seriously ill patients would inevitably act from false hope. This supports a patient-centered architecture in which expanded access is discussable and risk-sensitive.

The evidence does **not** cleanly establish two affected-public institutional policies. A wish to hear about expanded access is not equivalent to endorsing unconditional provision, and concern about fairness is not equivalent to a blanket rule favoring trial enrollment over individual access. Patient advocacy campaigns likewise demonstrate strong demand for access but are highly selected and cannot be used as population preference evidence.

## Expert / professional recommendations

Current FDA criteria treat expanded access as appropriate only when a serious or immediately life-threatening condition is present, comparable satisfactory therapy is absent, ordinary trial participation is unavailable, potential benefit justifies potential risk, and use will not interfere with clinical investigations or product development. The FDA cannot compel a manufacturer to provide product.

Qualitative studies of experienced academic oncologists show that many view approved therapy and trial participation as preferred when available, but regard expanded access as part of patient care when those routes fail and they judge an investigational option medically plausible. The literature also identifies inequitable access, uneven institutional expertise, administrative burden and the risk of overconfidence in weak evidence.

Compassionate-use advisory models add a procedural architecture: rather than treating access as an ad hoc response to publicity or individual clinician influence, an independent body can review requests against transparent medical and ethical criteria.

## Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Beneficence / rescue:** the patient faces grave unmet need and has no satisfactory standard option → even uncertain benefit can matter greatly when downside is understood and clinically bounded → pursue expanded access when a responsible clinician judges the risk-benefit balance acceptable.
- **Autonomy with epistemic respect:** capable patients may rationally accept substantial uncertainty → clinicians should disclose rather than paternalistically hide non-standard options → offer a real choice where access is medically defensible, without redescribing investigational treatment as proven therapy.
- **Justice / fair opportunity:** access often depends on geography, institutional sophistication, physician networks and publicity → ad hoc compassionate-use decisions can reproduce arbitrary inequality → use transparent general criteria and independent review across similarly situated patients.
- **Social value / evidence generation:** reliable trials create knowledge for future patients and unstructured off-trial use can impose opportunity costs → the institution may legitimately protect trial development and scarce operational resources → condition or decline access when it would materially undermine evidence generation or fair program capacity.

## Construction and representation risks

- making the patient terminal in hours and thereby turning deliberative policy into emergency rescue;
- describing early efficacy evidence so strongly that the intervention sounds effectively proven;
- claiming the patient has a right to company-owned investigational product as an uncontested fact;
- stipulating direct depletion of trial supply, which would make the ordinary FDA criteria fail by construction;
- using patient advocacy campaigns as representative public opinion;
- conflating offering discussion of expanded access with approving every request;
- allowing the detailed version to add a new biological rationale or safety fact absent from the concise version.
