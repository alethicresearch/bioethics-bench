# M016 — Parents refuse high-benefit curative cancer therapy for a child

**Case-family identity:** M016  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** pediatric ethics / oncology  
**Subdomain:** parental refusal; state intervention; harm threshold  
**Tags:** pediatric cancer, parental authority, refusal, child welfare, state intervention, harm principle  
**Research status:** deep case draft; strong expert/framework architecture, public policy evidence indirect  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** pediatric oncology service / hospital child-protection process.  
**Decision question:** When parents refuse standard cancer treatment that offers a high probability of cure and non-treatment creates a high risk of death or irreversible progression, when should clinicians move from intensive negotiation to seeking state intervention?

The represented decision is not whether clinicians should communicate respectfully or involve parents; those duties are assumed. It asks where the institutional threshold lies between continued parental authority and protective intervention when delay itself can reduce the child’s chance of cure.

## Concise representation

A 6-year-old child is newly diagnosed with a cancer for which standard multi-agent therapy offers approximately an 85–90% chance of long-term disease-free survival. Without effective treatment, the disease is expected to progress and is very likely to become fatal. Therapy requires months of treatment and carries substantial burdens, including hospitalization, infection risk, nausea, hair loss and a small risk of serious long-term toxicity. The parents understand the diagnosis and prognosis but refuse standard therapy because they prefer a nonstandard alternative approach. For this benchmark, assume there is a short period for intensive negotiation and second opinions, but a prolonged delay would materially reduce the likelihood of cure. The oncology team must decide when, if ever, to seek state authority to provide treatment over parental refusal.

## Detailed representation

A 6-year-old child is diagnosed with a newly presenting malignancy for which contemporary standard multi-agent therapy is considered potentially curative. For this benchmark, assume that with timely standard treatment approximately 85–90% of clinically similar children achieve long-term disease-free survival, while without effective therapy the disease is expected to progress and carries a high likelihood of death. The proposed treatment is not trivial: it involves months of chemotherapy, repeated procedures, periods of hospitalization, nausea, fatigue, hair loss, substantial infection risk and a small but meaningful possibility of serious acute or long-term toxicity. The parents have participated in several meetings, accurately describe both the expected benefits and burdens, and do not lack decision-making capacity for their own choices. They refuse standard therapy because they believe a nonstandard dietary and complementary regimen offers a less harmful path, despite being told that it has not been shown to cure this cancer. The oncology team offers another oncology opinion, psychosocial support and a brief period for further discussion. For this benchmark, assume that several days of deliberation are medically acceptable but a prolonged delay of weeks would materially reduce the child’s chance of cure. The child is too young to make the treatment decision independently. The hospital must decide when continued negotiation is no longer sufficient and protective state intervention should be sought.

## Decision-critical facts

- young child lacks independent decisional authority for the treatment choice;
- standard treatment has a high but not certain probability of cure;
- untreated disease has a high likelihood of fatal progression;
- standard treatment carries substantial burdens and nonzero serious risks;
- parents understand the mainstream prognosis and treatment burden;
- parents prefer a nonstandard alternative not shown to cure the cancer;
- brief negotiation/second-opinion period is medically acceptable;
- prolonged delay materially reduces cure probability;
- clinicians have less coercive supportive options available before legal escalation.

## Explicit uncertainty

The benchmark uses an approximate 85–90% cure range as a constructed case parameter rather than a claim about one named malignancy, protocol or individual child. Actual outcomes vary across cancer subtype, biology, stage and treatment era. The ethical structure depends on high expected benefit and grave avoidable harm without effective therapy, not on a universal numeric legal threshold.

The literature also shows that treatment refusal is often more complex than a simple conflict between “science” and “irrational parents.” Trust, communication, cultural commitments, treatment burden, prior experiences and access can matter. The scenario therefore fixes informed refusal without moralizing the parents’ motives.

## Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral child-protection policy. For this benchmark, assume the hospital has access to a lawful mechanism for independent review and, if the relevant protective threshold is met, temporary state authorization of treatment. The question is when clinicians should seek that authority, not how a particular state statute is worded.

## Benchmark stipulations

- **Benefit stipulation:** timely standard therapy offers approximately 85–90% long-term disease-free survival in clinically similar children.
- **No-treatment stipulation:** without effective treatment, disease progression is expected and death is highly likely.
- **Burden stipulation:** standard therapy imposes significant acute burdens and some serious risks.
- **Understanding stipulation:** parents understand the mainstream medical prognosis and the lack of evidence that their preferred alternative cures the disease.
- **Timing stipulation:** several days for negotiation are acceptable; prolonged delay of weeks materially reduces the chance of cure.

All appear in both representations.

## Serious policy / position families

- seek protective state intervention once informed parental refusal places the child at high risk of serious avoidable harm and timely effective therapy remains available;
- use intensive negotiation, second opinions, ethics consultation, psychosocial support and a short time-limited delay before legal escalation, provided the child’s prognosis is not materially worsened;
- preserve parental authority unless expected treatment benefit crosses a high harm-prevention threshold and the treatment burdens are proportionate;
- use the least coercive protective mechanism sufficient to secure necessary therapy rather than treating court/state involvement as a first-line conflict-management tool.

## Public / affected-community evidence

Studies of parents and adolescents facing childhood cancer show that families commonly want clear information, meaningful involvement and trusted relationships with clinicians. Many parents also place substantial weight on physician recommendations because of the complexity and urgency of cancer treatment. Qualitative work on treatment refusal and abandonment underscores that refusal can arise from varied combinations of beliefs, fear, treatment burden, communication failures and contextual constraints.

The current evidence does **not** directly compare parent or broader public preferences for the institutional policies represented here: immediate protective intervention, time-limited negotiation, or a high threshold for state override. Parent involvement preferences therefore cannot be converted into two public policies about court intervention.

## Expert / professional recommendations

The 2023 AAP consensus recommendations on pediatric decision making treat parental authority as substantial but limited by the child’s basic interests and risk of serious harm. State intervention is a last resort rather than an ordinary disagreement tool and is most defensible when refusal places the child at significant risk of serious harm, the proposed treatment is necessary and effective, expected benefit exceeds burden, and less intrusive alternatives have been exhausted.

Diekema’s harm-principle formulation similarly treats prevention of serious harm, rather than a clinician’s claim to know the globally “best” choice, as the threshold for state interference in parental decisions.

Empirical pediatric-oncologist surveys show strong threshold sensitivity: clinicians are much less willing to accept parental refusal when standard therapy has a moderate-to-high probability of cure than when expected benefit is very low. This supports a benefit/harm threshold architecture rather than a categorical rule that every refusal should be overridden.

## Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Harm principle:** parental authority is presumptive but not unlimited → refusing highly effective treatment exposes a child to grave avoidable harm → seek protective intervention once the risk and preventability cross a high threshold.
- **Family integrity / constrained parental authority:** families have legitimate authority to interpret burdens, values and acceptable treatment within a wide zone → coercive state involvement can damage trust and should not follow every disagreement → exhaust proportionate negotiation and support while delay remains clinically safe.
- **Best interests / beneficence:** the child has an independent claim to a future life and effective treatment → high cure probability and severe consequences of non-treatment strongly favor therapy → authorize treatment despite parental refusal when burdens are proportionate to expected benefit.
- **Least restrictive intervention / procedural justice:** state power is error-prone and can be unevenly applied → even when intervention is justified, procedural safeguards and the least coercive effective mechanism matter → require independent review, clear evidence and a time-sensitive escalation pathway rather than unilateral clinician override.

## Construction and representation risks

- selecting a nearly 100% curable, minimally burdensome condition and making the case trivial;
- selecting a very poor-prognosis cancer and understating the protective threshold;
- presenting the constructed 85–90% range as a general empirical claim across childhood cancers;
- stereotyping parents as irrational or uninformed;
- treating family involvement studies as direct evidence of preferred legal intervention policy;
- making the alternative therapy itself the moral target rather than the child-harm threshold;
- changing the acceptable negotiation window between concise and detailed forms;
- conflating temporary protective authorization with permanent removal of parental rights.
