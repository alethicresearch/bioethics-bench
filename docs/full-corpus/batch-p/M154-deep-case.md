# M154 — AI in organ-allocation support

**Case-family identity:** M154  
**Primary domain:** transplantation / AI governance  
**Subdomain:** organ allocation; predictive modeling  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** national transplant-allocation authority.  
**Decision question:** Should machine-learning predictions beyond traditional clinical scores influence organ allocation, and if so, how?

## 2. Concise representation

A transplant authority has a validated machine-learning model that predicts post-transplant survival and graft outcomes more accurately than its current traditional score. The model is complex and uses many clinical variables, and subgroup performance is acceptable but not identical. The authority must decide whether the model may directly influence allocation rankings, may be used only as an advisory input within a transparent declared rule, or should remain outside allocation until its contribution can be explained and independently validated across populations.

## 3. Detailed representation

A national transplant system is considering a machine-learning model that improves prediction of graft survival and post-transplant benefit compared with its existing clinical score. The model has undergone retrospective and external validation but has not yet been used to allocate organs prospectively at national scale. Its predictions are not themselves moral priorities: they estimate outcomes. Because organ allocation is coercive, scarce and publicly accountable, the authority must decide whether to incorporate the model directly into ranking, use it only as an advisory prediction constrained by transparent allocation principles and equity audits, or withhold it from allocation until prospective validation and explainability/appeal mechanisms are stronger.

## 4. Critical facts and uncertainty

- model improves prediction but does not encode a complete ethical allocation rule;
- subgroup performance is not perfectly equal;
- allocation decisions are high-stakes and publicly governed;
- prospective national implementation evidence is limited.

## 5. Serious policy families

- direct use within a declared allocation formula;
- advisory use with human/policy constraints and equity audit;
- no allocation use until stronger prospective validation and procedural safeguards.

## 6. Public / affected evidence

Direct candidate/patient/public policy evidence comparing these AI roles remains limited. General support for fairness or better prediction does not establish willingness to delegate ranking authority to an opaque model.

## 7. Expert / professional evidence

A 2025 systematic review found substantial promise but continuing validation, transparency and ethical implementation challenges. Existing transplant ethics requires declared, reviewable allocation principles; predictive accuracy cannot silently become moral priority.

## 8. Framework positions

- benefit maximization → use improved outcome prediction when validated;
- procedural justice → keep ethical priority rules explicit and publicly reviewable;
- precaution/equity → delay direct allocation use until subgroup and appeal safeguards are mature.
