# Bioethics Bench — Batch O source and construction packet (M141–M150)

**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27  
**Scope:** commercial health-data partnerships and clinical/administrative AI governance.

## M141 — Commercial partnership using health-system data
2024 systematic reviews show public support for secondary health-data sharing is conditional and commercial involvement commonly reduces trust unless privacy, transparency, control and public benefit are clear. Strong candidate-audit target if permissive public-benefit partnership and consent-restrictive orientations can be kept source-grounded.

## M142 — AI care-management allocation and structural bias
WHO AI guidance requires safety, autonomy, transparency, inclusiveness and equity. Classic evidence shows apparently accurate health-management algorithms can systematically under-identify need in disadvantaged groups when cost proxies encode structural inequality. Professional guidance converges on correction/mitigation rather than knowingly accepting avoidable disparity. Strong deep case; executable only if stakeholder evidence supports distinct rules.

## M143 — Clinician override of validated AI recommendation
WHO and AMA emphasize meaningful human control/qualified human intervention. AMA principles state that clinical decisions influenced by AI should contain specified qualified human intervention points and generally preserve capacity to override model output. This likely produces one professional human-oversight architecture, not a clean expert split.

## M144 — Disclosure of AI involvement to patients
AMA 2025–2026 policy emphasizes transparency to physicians and patients, while disclosure content should be meaningful rather than boilerplate. Direct patient studies generally favor awareness and transparency, with concern increasing as AI has more autonomous/high-impact roles. Candidate audit should test always-disclose versus material/high-impact disclosure rules.

## M145 — Diagnostic AI with unequal subgroup performance
FDA lifecycle/GMLP guidance emphasizes intended-population performance, representative data, monitoring and risk mitigation; WHO emphasizes inclusiveness/equity. Deliberately accepting a known major subgroup deficit is poorly supported as professional policy. Likely research-complete/non-executable unless public/patient evidence supports deployment tradeoffs.

## M146 — Ambient AI recording for clinical documentation
2025 JAMA Network Open consent study of 103 patients and 18 clinicians supports explicit, flexible informed consent; patients strongly valued information about audio use/storage/access, and many wanted opt-out. Stanford 2025–2026 patient experience data show high acceptability among exposed users. This is a strong candidate-audit target, especially encounter-level opt-in versus well-disclosed default/opt-out.

## M147 — Autonomous diagnostic AI and human review
AMA principles favor human-in-the-loop medical decision-making with earlier human intervention as harm risk rises. Autonomous FDA-authorized tools exist for defined tasks, creating a real risk-tiered governance question, but broad professional support for eliminating review in high-stakes care is limited.

## M148 — AI-driven prior authorization
AMA 2026 policy explicitly opposes autonomous or semiautonomous AI substituting for physician review in coverage determinations and calls for physician-led review and transparency. Automated approvals may be low-risk; adverse decisions raise due-process and conflict-of-interest concerns. Strong policy case but professional convergence may limit executability.

## M149 — Updating a deployed clinical AI model
FDA's 2025 final PCCP guidance provides a mature alternative to full reauthorization after every update: predetermined boundaries, validation methods, impact assessment, transparency and lifecycle monitoring. This creates a strong governance case contrasting material-update revalidation, bounded PCCP updates and frozen periodic versions. Candidate audit should test whether public/user evidence supports more than one rule.

## M150 — Synthetic data: privacy versus representativeness
2025 scoping review finds synthetic health data promising but no consensus on standardized privacy/utility evaluation; many studies inadequately assess privacy. Synthetic data can distort minority patterns and therefore affect representativeness/equity. Strong frontier case; public policy evidence is currently limited, likely non-executable.
