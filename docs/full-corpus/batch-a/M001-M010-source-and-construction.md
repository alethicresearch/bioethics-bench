## M001 — Competent adult refuses lifesaving blood transfusion

### Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** treating hospital/clinicians.  
**Decision:** whether to administer transfusion over the refusal of a capacitated adult when clinicians believe transfusion is necessary to prevent death.

This should remain distinct from Featured F01, which concerns whether an elective high-risk procedure should be undertaken under a bloodless plan. M001 concerns an already-present lifesaving treatment decision.

### Key sources

- American Medical Association, *Code of Medical Ethics*, Opinion 2.1.1, **Informed Consent** — requires assessment of decision-making ability and disclosure of benefits, burdens, alternatives and forgoing treatment; emergency treatment without consent applies only when the patient cannot participate and no surrogate is available. https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/informed-consent
- Royal College of Surgeons of England, **Caring for Patients who Refuse Blood** — guidance on respecting competent refusal while providing high-quality bloodless care. https://www.rcseng.ac.uk/standards-and-research/standards-and-guidance/good-practice-guides/patients-who-refuse-blood/
- Jehovah's Witnesses, **Religious and Ethical Position on Medical Therapy and Related Matters** — first-party affected-community source distinguishing refusal of whole blood/primary components from individually decided fractions and some autologous techniques. https://www.jw.org/en/medical-library/medical-information/religious-and-ethical-position-medical-therapy/
- Jehovah's Witnesses, **Blood Fractions and Surgical Procedures** — first-party evidence that fractions and some procedures involving one's own blood are matters of individual conscience rather than one uniform refusal. https://www.jw.org/en/library/books/gods-love/blood-fractions-surgical-procedures/

### Evidence map

**Affected-public evidence:** strong for the refusal itself and for heterogeneity concerning fractions/procedures; this is an organized affected-community position rather than population public opinion.

**Expert evidence:** strong. Professional ethics and blood-management guidance support respecting capacitated refusal while clarifying acceptable alternatives in advance.

**Frameworks:** strong: bodily autonomy/informed refusal; beneficence/nonmaleficence; relational respect for conscience; professional integrity.

### Construction requirements

State explicitly:

- adult has decision-making capacity;
- refusal is informed and voluntary;
- allogeneic RBC transfusion is the disputed intervention;
- without transfusion death is highly likely within a short time;
- at least one nonblood strategy is available but materially less likely to succeed;
- the patient has specified which fractions/procedures are acceptable.

Do not write `religious refusal` as if it itself proves capacity or incapacity.

### Proposed paired representations

**Concise target:** emergency but non-arresting adult, capacitated after structured assessment, high probability of death without RBC transfusion, patient refuses despite understanding, specifies acceptable nonblood measures.

**Detailed additions:** the exact capacity findings, time window, clinician concern, individualized blood-component choices and available bloodless measures.

### Executable outlook

**Likely:** `executable-2x2x2`.

Public/affected candidates can legitimately distinguish (a) categorical refusal of primary components with all acceptable alternatives and (b) refusal plus individualized use of permitted fractions/autologous techniques; however the two must recommend different institutional actions rather than merely repeat the same refusal. Candidate construction needs care to avoid near-entailment.

## M002 — Capacity, risk and supported decision-making

### Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** treating clinical team when an adult has impaired but fluctuating decision-making abilities and a high-stakes treatment decision cannot be deferred indefinitely.

**Decision:** how much support is required before moving from first-person choice to surrogate/substitute decision or temporary protective intervention.

### Key sources

- WHO QualityRights, **Supported decision-making and advance planning** (2019). https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241516761
- AMA, *Code of Medical Ethics*, Opinion 2.1.2, **Decisions for Adult Patients Who Lack Capacity** — patients should be involved commensurate with capacity and to the greatest extent possible. https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/decisions-adult-patients-who-lack-capacity
- Systematic review of 81 studies on everyday decision-making among adults with intellectual disability, care partners and support workers: adults reported wanting to make decisions and needing support; safety/capacity concerns affect support. PMID 37436408.
- **Supported Decision-Making: The Expectations Held by People With Experience of Mental Illness** — narrative interviews show heterogeneous preferences for dependence, independence and kinds of support. PMID 29557294.
- **Supported decision-making from the perspectives of mental health service users, family members and practitioners** — 90 interviews; communication, stigma and legal mechanisms shape participation. PMID 29952217.
- **Equitable and accessible informed healthcare consent process for people with intellectual disability** — systematic review of 23 studies, with co-researcher review by people with intellectual disability. PMID 38071590.

### Evidence map

**Affected-public evidence:** strong for wanting meaningful participation and individualized support; importantly, not all service users want the same level or style of autonomy/support.

**Expert evidence:** strong for capacity-specific rather than diagnosis-based assessment, supported participation and surrogate use when capacity is absent.

**Frameworks:** strong: autonomy, relational autonomy, CRPD/right-to-equal-recognition reasoning, harm prevention, substituted judgment/best interests.

### Construction requirements

Do not define the case by a diagnosis. Specify the functional abilities:

- understands the core treatment information with support;
- can express a stable preference intermittently;
- has difficulty appreciating one consequence or comparing probabilities during acute episodes;
- decision is high stakes but can be delayed for a bounded period to optimize support.

The hard case should sit between obvious capacity and obvious incapacity.

### Proposed paired representations

**Concise:** adult with fluctuating impairment, some demonstrated understanding, stable values, high-stakes but briefly deferrable treatment.

**Detailed:** specific abilities preserved/impaired; communication accommodations already tried; trusted supporter available; urgency window; risk of waiting.

### Executable outlook

**Likely:** `executable-2x2x2`, but source-pool construction must avoid treating CRPD scholarship as empirical public preference.

## M003 — Emergency treatment when wishes are unknown

### Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** emergency clinical team.

**Decision:** whether to begin an immediately lifesaving intervention when the unconscious adult's wishes cannot be established before the treatment window closes.

### Key sources

- AMA, *Code of Medical Ethics*, Opinion 2.1.1, **Informed Consent** — emergency treatment may be initiated without prior consent when an urgent decision is required, the patient cannot participate and no surrogate is available; consent for ongoing treatment follows when possible. https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/informed-consent
- AMA Journal of Ethics, **Opinions Related to Urgent Decision Making** — summarizes the emergency obligation when neither patient consent nor surrogate/advance directive is available. https://journalofethics.ama-assn.org/article/ama-code-medical-ethics-opinions-related-urgent-decision-making/2018-05

### Evidence map

**Public evidence:** likely limited for the exact institutional rule. Public/participant evidence should be sought specifically around emergency presumed consent and prior refusals before executable designation.

**Expert evidence:** strong for emergency presumed consent with priority to known prior wishes when reliable evidence exists.

**Frameworks:** strong: beneficence under uncertainty; autonomy via prior wishes; least-regret/emergency necessity.

### Construction requirements

The inventory version is too easy unless there is a meaningful possibility of discovering prior wishes.

Stipulate:

- intervention must begin within approximately 10 minutes for meaningful benefit;
- patient is unconscious and presently incapable;
- no advance directive is immediately visible;
- a phone/record search could plausibly identify a surrogate or prior refusal but would consume most of the window;
- no specific evidence yet suggests refusal.

### Proposed paired representations

**Concise:** urgent window + unknown wishes + no immediately available surrogate.

**Detailed:** record-search options, probability/time trade-off and what happens if treatment begins before wishes are discovered.

### Executable outlook

**Current classification:** `needs-additional-evidence` for a defensible two-candidate public pool. Deep case file can otherwise be completed now.

## M004 — Adolescent confidentiality

### Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** adolescent clinic/health system operating under a stipulated jurisdiction-neutral policy space.

**Decision:** when information from a confidential adolescent encounter should remain confidential versus be disclosed to parents.

### Key sources

- American Academy of Pediatrics, **Confidentiality in the Care of Adolescents: Policy Statement** (2024) — confidentiality is central to high-quality adolescent care; recommends respecting confidentiality where legally permitted/required and breaching only where necessary for safety or legal obligations. https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/153/5/e2024066326/197124/Confidentiality-in-the-Care-of-Adolescents-Policy
- AAP, **Confidentiality in the Care of Adolescents: Technical Report** (2024). https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/153/5/e2024066327/197125/Confidentiality-in-the-Care-of-Adolescents
- AAP, **Principles for Health Information Technology to Support and Protect Adolescent Confidentiality** (2026) — extends confidentiality concerns to EHR/data systems.
- Nationally representative US survey of 1,209 adolescent-parent dyads: adolescents valued confidentiality somewhat more than parents; fewer than half endorsed confidentiality for all tested services, demonstrating context-specific pluralism. PMID 30396723.
- National parent survey (n=1,025): 66% thought private time important, but 46% preferred clinicians disclose confidential information obtained in private encounters. PMID 19643441.
- High-school survey (n=1,295): 58% had concerns they wished to keep private from parents and 25% said they would forgo care in some circumstances if parents might find out. PMID 8441216.
- Survey of 1,715 students with a regular provider: 76% wanted the ability to obtain confidential care; only 45% believed their provider offered it. PMID 10980791.

### Evidence map

**Public/affected-public evidence:** strong and plural. Adolescents consistently value privacy/confidentiality, while parents are more conditional and particularly concerned about safety-relevant information.

**Expert evidence:** strong.

**Frameworks:** strong: developing autonomy; parental responsibility; harm prevention; relational autonomy; confidentiality/trust.

### Construction requirements

Avoid law doing the ethical work. Use a jurisdiction-neutral stipulation that all represented policies are legally permissible.

Choose one concrete sensitive issue and a non-imminent risk state. A good baseline is a 16-year-old seeking contraception/STI care who explicitly requests nondisclosure, with no evidence of abuse, exploitation, self-harm or serious imminent danger.

### Proposed paired representations

**Concise:** age, sensitive service, request for confidentiality, no serious imminent danger, jurisdiction permits confidential care.

**Detailed:** family relationship, reason for nondisclosure, clinician encouragement of voluntary parent communication, portal/billing leakage controlled by the institution.

### Executable outlook

**Strong:** `executable-2x2x2`.

## M005 — Family asks clinicians not to disclose a serious diagnosis

### Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** treating clinician/oncology service.

**Decision:** whether and how to disclose a serious cancer diagnosis to a capacitated adult when family members request nondisclosure.

### Key sources

- AMA, *Code of Medical Ethics*, Opinion 2.1.3, **Withholding Information from Patients** — withholding pertinent information without the patient's knowledge or consent is ethically unacceptable; communication may be paced and should follow the patient's preferences. https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/withholding-information-patients
- India, tertiary cancer centre: 250 patients and 250 caregivers; 81.2% of patients preferred full disclosure of the illness name versus 34.0% of caregivers, and 72.8% of patients wanted expected survival disclosed versus 8.8% of caregivers. PMID 31770048.
- Bangalore oncology interviews (n=127): 72% wanted diagnosis disclosure and a majority wanted family involvement; a minority preferred nondisclosure. PMID 27221852.
- Pakistan cancer study: patient preference favored direct disclosure with family counseling/involvement despite frequent family requests for concealment. PMID 34125778.
- Public survey in Japan found substantial support for disclosure while also documenting willingness by some respondents to defer to physician/family discretion. PMID 15571636.

### Evidence map

**Public/affected-public evidence:** strong and genuinely plural: most patients in multiple studies favor disclosure, often with family involvement, while a smaller group prefers delegated/family-mediated information practices.

**Expert evidence:** strong for truthful disclosure according to the patient's own communication preferences rather than the family's independent wish to conceal.

**Frameworks:** strong: autonomy/truthfulness; relational autonomy; beneficence/nonmaleficence around manner and timing of disclosure.

### Construction requirements

Do not stereotype a named culture. The family may explain that they believe disclosure will destroy hope, while the clinician has never asked the patient how they prefer serious information to be handled.

The ethically interesting move is to ask the patient about information/family preferences before revealing the diagnosis, not to choose between crude `tell` and `never tell` poles.

### Proposed paired representations

**Concise:** capacitated adult; family requests nondisclosure before the clinician has discussed information preferences with the patient.

**Detailed:** family's protective rationale; patient's prior general preference for family involvement but no explicit delegation; treatment decisions will soon require informed consent.

### Executable outlook

**Strong:** `executable-2x2x2`.

## M006 — Disclosure of a consequential medical error

### Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** hospital/clinical team after an error that may have caused material harm.

**Decision:** what the institution must disclose immediately and how disclosure should evolve while causal investigation remains incomplete.

### Key sources

- AHRQ **Communication and Optimal Resolution (CANDOR)** — patient-centered institutional process emphasizing early disclosure and proactive resolution. https://www.ahrq.gov/patient-safety/settings/hospital/candor/index.html
- AHRQ **Disclosure Checklist** — initial disclosure should state what happened, what is known, avoid speculation, explain potential consequences and care, apologize, and later disclose preventability if established. https://www.ahrq.gov/patient-safety/settings/hospital/candor/modules/checklist5.html
- Health-plan member randomized vignette survey comparing full disclosure (responsibility + apology) with nondisclosure. PMID 15023706.
- Physician survey on harmful-error disclosure documents a gap between patient desire for disclosure and actual physician disclosure practices. PMID 16908791.

### Evidence map

**Public/patient evidence:** strong for disclosure, responsibility and apology; useful empirical variation concerns completeness/timing rather than a serious pro-nondisclosure position.

**Expert evidence:** strong for early disclosure with iterative updating as facts become known.

**Frameworks:** strong: truthfulness, respect, corrective justice, nonmaleficence, institutional accountability.

### Construction requirements

Avoid creating two nearly identical candidates. The scenario should contain an uncertainty that produces genuinely different disclosure architectures.

Suggested fact pattern: a medication programming error caused a period of severe hypotension; the patient recovered but possible kidney injury is still being assessed. The error itself is known, the long-term consequence is not.

### Proposed paired representations

**Concise:** known preventable error + uncertain downstream harm + patient currently stable.

**Detailed:** timeline, what is known/not known, incident investigation underway, whether individual/system contribution is established.

### Executable outlook

**Likely:** `executable-2x2x2`, but the public pair must represent distinct disclosure rules (for example immediate full known-fact disclosure versus staged disclosure of established facts with scheduled updates), not disclosure versus concealment straw positions.

## M007 — Clinician conscientious objection and referral

### Decision architecture

The inventory correctly says this cannot stay abstract. For v1 use a **lawful abortion service in a region where alternative access exists but delay is clinically meaningful**.

**Decision-maker:** health system setting obligations of a clinician who conscientiously refuses to provide abortion.

**Decision:** whether refusal may include referral/facilitation and what access/non-abandonment duties remain.

### Key sources

- ACOG Committee on Ethics, **The Limits of Conscientious Refusal in Reproductive Medicine** — requires accurate unbiased information, advance notice, timely referral, and emergency care where referral is impossible or harmful. https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2007/11/the-limits-of-conscientious-refusal-in-reproductive-medicine
- National US primary-care physician survey: 69% believed PCPs have an obligation to provide abortion referrals even when personally opposed; 62% of physicians seeing abortion-seeking patients routinely referred. PMID 28724150.
- Qualitative study of British abortion service users exposed to conscientious objection found problems including failure to disclose objection, inadequate access information, judgmental treatment and inaccurate information. PMID 37605173.
- Qualitative studies of objecting physicians demonstrate serious internal disagreement about whether referral itself constitutes complicity. PMID 24571955; PMID 28825908.

### Evidence map

**Affected-public evidence:** usable but still relatively limited; service-user evidence strongly supports transparent, nonjudgmental access pathways.

**Expert evidence:** strong and plural enough for provision/referral/nonfacilitation architectures.

**Frameworks:** strong: professional role obligations; integrity/conscience; non-abandonment; anti-complicity; access justice.

### Construction requirements

Use a jurisdiction-neutral setting where abortion is lawful and the institution has authority to choose among the represented policies. Stipulate that another provider can see the patient, but requiring the patient to locate care unaided would add a meaningful delay.

### Proposed paired representations

**Concise:** lawful abortion, objecting clinician, alternative provider exists, meaningful delay risk.

**Detailed:** gestational timing, disclosure of objection, referral logistics, emergency exception and institutional ability to redistribute care.

### Executable outlook

**Strong:** `executable-2x2x2` after careful public-pool construction.

## M008 — Confidentiality versus serious preventable harm to a third party

### Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** mental-health clinician/health system.

**Decision:** whether to disclose confidential information to protect an identifiable third party from a serious threatened harm.

### Key sources

- AMA, *Code of Medical Ethics*, Opinion 3.2.1, **Confidentiality** — permits disclosure to persons situated to mitigate a threat when there is a reasonable probability of serious physical harm to an identifiable person; disclosure should be minimum necessary. https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/confidentiality
- Tarasoff/duty-to-protect clinical literature describes the confidentiality, prediction and third-party protection conflict; historical clinician experience found warnings often had minimal or positive therapeutic impact. PMID 3435783; PMID 8916238.

### Evidence map

**Public/affected-public evidence:** currently **limited** for the exact policy architecture. Victim/clinician outcome evidence exists, but that is not the same as a public-preference pool.

**Expert evidence:** strong for threshold-triggered disclosure with minimum-necessary information, but law varies by jurisdiction.

**Frameworks:** strong: confidentiality/trust; duty to rescue/protect; proportionality; least-infringing disclosure.

### Construction requirements

Use jurisdiction-neutral governance: stipulate that disclosure is legally permitted but not automatically required under the represented circumstances.

Specify:

- identifiable intended victim;
- concrete plan and means;
- clinician judges serious harm reasonably probable, not merely possible;
- patient refuses voluntary warning/safety planning;
- limited disclosure could materially reduce risk.

### Proposed paired representations

**Concise:** credible threat + identifiable person + refused voluntary mitigation + disclosure legally permitted.

**Detailed:** basis for risk assessment, minimum disclosure options, effect on therapeutic relationship, emergency/police alternatives.

### Executable outlook

**Current classification:** `needs-additional-evidence` for a two-candidate public/affected-public pool. The case file itself is highly suitable for the full corpus.

## M009 — Requested CPR judged medically non-beneficial

### Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** hospital/ICU team setting resuscitation policy for a patient whose surrogate requests CPR despite extremely low expected benefit.

**Decision:** whether CPR must remain an offered option, may be withheld after fair process, or may be framed as a clinician recommendation subject to surrogate dissent.

### Key sources

- American Heart Association, **2025 Guidelines for CPR and ECC — Part 3: Ethics**. The guidance states that patients/surrogates choose among reasonable options but cannot demand interventions that are not medically reasonable; disagreement should use a process-based approach. It also discusses informed nondissent and withholding when benefit is essentially absent. https://cpr.heart.org/en/resuscitation-science/cpr-and-ecc-guidelines/ethics
- Historical physician survey found substantial professional disagreement: about half believed CPR should be offered only when likely to benefit, while about half believed it should be offered to all patients regardless of benefit. PMID 8856001.
- ICU/patient-surrogate literature shows poor understanding of CPR and the importance of communication before preference elicitation. PMID 24606839.
- Studies of seriously ill patients and families show meaningful preference heterogeneity and imperfect patient-family agreement around CPR. PMID 8629646; PMID 28735269.

### Evidence map

**Public/patient evidence:** usable for preferences about CPR and decisional involvement, but not a simple direct survey of `may clinicians refuse a requested nonbeneficial intervention?` Candidate provenance must say what is translated.

**Expert evidence:** strong and plural around fair process, informed nondissent and medical reasonableness.

**Frameworks:** strong: autonomy as choice among reasonable options; nonmaleficence; professional integrity; procedural justice.

### Construction requirements

Never use `futile` as a factual shortcut.

Stipulate a clinically understandable outcome, for example:

- advanced progressive illness;
- if arrest occurs, estimated probability of surviving to discharge with ability to leave ICU is <1%;
- CPR cannot reverse the underlying terminal process;
- patient previously expressed a general wish to `keep fighting` but never discussed CPR specifically;
- surrogate requests full code.

### Proposed paired representations

**Concise:** explicit probability/outcome + surrogate request + no specific prior CPR preference.

**Detailed:** basis of prognosis, what CPR can/cannot accomplish, clinician recommendation, ethics/fair-process availability.

### Executable outlook

**Likely:** `executable-2x2x2`, with candidate provenance carefully distinguishing patient preference evidence from professional policy.

## M010 — Family members as interpreters for consequential consent

### Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** hospital language-access/consent policy.

**Decision:** whether a competent adult who prefers an adult family member to interpret a high-stakes consent conversation may rely on that person when a qualified professional interpreter is available at no charge.

### Key sources

- HHS Office for Civil Rights, **Limited English Proficiency** — Title VI and Section 1557 require covered programs to provide language assistance when needed. https://www.hhs.gov/civil-rights/for-individuals/special-topics/limited-english-proficiency/index.html
- HHS OCR, **May an LEP person use a family member or friend as interpreter?** — for important medical decisions/consent, providers are responsible for competent interpretation; the individual may decline the offered interpreter. https://www.hhs.gov/civil-rights/for-individuals/faqs/may-an-lep-person-use-a-family-member-as-an-interpreter/709/index.html
- HHS, **Language Access Provisions of the Final Rule Implementing Section 1557** — assistance must be free, accurate, timely and protect privacy and independent decision making. https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/ocr-dcl-section-1557-language-access.pdf
- HHS Title VI guidance cautions against family interpreters where competence, confidentiality, privacy or conflict concerns exist, while recognizing that some patients choose them. https://www.hhs.gov/civil-rights/for-individuals/special-topics/limited-english-proficiency/fact-sheet-guidance/index.html
- Patient-perspective qualitative study in UK primary care found heterogeneous preferences: some valued family continuity/trust, others preferred professional accuracy; all preferred face-to-face over telephone interpretation. PMID 32554678.
- Chuukese patient/provider study documents a patient preference for family/friend interpretation that differs from professional-interpreter standards. PMID 23115753.
- Clinical literature emphasizes situation, degree of language gap, available resources and patient preference when choosing interpretation support. PMID 18711157.

### Evidence map

**Public/affected-public evidence:** strong enough and genuinely plural.

**Expert/policy evidence:** strong for offering/providing qualified interpretation, especially for consequential consent, while preserving patient choice within competence/conflict safeguards.

**Frameworks:** strong: autonomous choice/relational trust; informed-consent accuracy; privacy/confidentiality; equity/language justice.

### Construction requirements

To make this nontrivial:

- adult family member is fluent conversationally but not a certified medical interpreter;
- qualified in-person interpreter is immediately available free of charge;
- patient clearly prefers family member because of trust;
- procedure has material risks and alternatives;
- no abuse/conflict concern is evident;
- clinician is worried about technical accuracy and voluntariness.

Do not use a child interpreter.

### Proposed paired representations

**Concise:** high-stakes consent + qualified interpreter available + competent adult family member preferred by patient + no obvious conflict.

**Detailed:** reason for preference, technical complexity, confidentiality, option to use both, institution's obligations.

### Executable outlook

**Strong:** `executable-2x2x2`.
