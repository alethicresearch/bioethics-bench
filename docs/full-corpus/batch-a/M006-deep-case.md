# M006 — Disclosure of a consequential medical error

**Case-family identity:** M006  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** professional duties  
**Subdomain:** error disclosure; patient safety; institutional accountability  
**Tags:** medical error, disclosure, apology, uncertainty, patient safety  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** hospital/clinical team.  
**Decision question:** After a known preventable medication error with uncertain downstream harm, what must the hospital disclose now and what information may wait for the investigation?

The decision is not whether to conceal a known error. The difficult issue is how a disclosure policy should distinguish established facts from causal or long-term conclusions that are still being investigated.

## Concise representation

A hospitalized patient received an excessive medication dose because of a known infusion-pump programming error and developed severe hypotension before the error was recognized and corrected. The patient is now stable, but clinicians do not yet know whether a new kidney abnormality will resolve or whether the error will have lasting effects. The programming error itself is established; the event investigation has not yet determined all contributing causes or preventability at the system level. The hospital must decide what to disclose immediately, including responsibility and apology, and what should be identified as uncertain and updated after investigation.

## Detailed representation

A hospitalized patient received an excessive medication dose after a clinician entered the wrong infusion-pump setting. The patient developed severe hypotension for approximately 30 minutes before the discrepancy was recognized, the infusion was stopped and supportive treatment restored stable blood pressure. A new kidney abnormality is being monitored, but clinicians do not yet know whether it is transient, whether the hypotension caused it, or whether there will be lasting effects. The incorrect programming and resulting overdose are established facts. A formal investigation has begun to determine why normal checking processes did not prevent the error and whether additional system factors contributed. The hospital must decide what to disclose immediately, including the known error, possible consequences, responsibility and apology, and what should be explicitly labeled uncertain pending follow-up investigation.

## Decision-critical facts

- a preventable medication programming error is known to have occurred;
- the error produced a clinically significant adverse event;
- the patient is currently stable;
- a possible downstream kidney injury remains uncertain;
- the immediate error is established while complete causal/system analysis is not;
- follow-up communication is feasible.

## Explicit uncertainty

Long-term harm and the complete causal chain are unresolved. The scenario must preserve the difference between uncertainty about consequences/system causes and uncertainty about whether an error occurred.

## Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral institutional disclosure policy. Malpractice law and apology statutes are not used to answer the ethical question.

## Benchmark stipulations

None beyond the constructed clinical facts above. If numerical kidney-risk or compensation assumptions are later introduced, they must be marked explicitly and matched across representations.

## Serious policy / position families

- disclose the known error promptly, explain known and possible consequences, apologize, identify uncertainty and schedule updates;
- provide an immediate adverse-event disclosure of established facts while reserving statements about preventability, causal attribution and corrective responsibility until investigation supports them;
- pair disclosure with an institutional response-and-resolution process including mitigation and later corrective-action reporting.

Concealment is not included merely to create contrast.

## Public / affected-community evidence

Mazor et al. randomized 1,500 health-plan members to error/disclosure vignettes; 66% responded. Almost all respondents (98.8%) wanted errors disclosed. Full disclosure that included responsibility and apology increased reported satisfaction, trust and positive emotional response compared with less forthright disclosure.

This is strong patient/public evidence for disclosure and forthrightness. It is **not** strong evidence for two distinct public policy architectures about which unresolved causal details should be stated immediately. The public source pool therefore remains narrower than the expert/normative debate.

## Expert / professional recommendations

AHRQ CANDOR recommends early disclosure of adverse events, disclosure of what is known, avoidance of speculation, explanation of possible consequences and current care, apology, and planned follow-up. Its Disclosure Checklist treats the initial conversation as one in a series and distinguishes known preventability from matters still under investigation.

## Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Veracity / respect:** patients are entitled to material information about what happened in their care → established error facts should not be withheld → disclose the known event promptly.
- **Epistemic responsibility:** truthfulness also requires not presenting conjecture as fact → incomplete causal analysis should be labeled uncertain → use staged updates without using uncertainty as a reason to delay known-fact disclosure.
- **Corrective justice / accountability:** preventable harm creates duties beyond information transfer → institutions should acknowledge responsibility, mitigate consequences and learn from the event → connect disclosure to remediation and prevention.
- **Nonmaleficence / trust:** concealment can compound injury and damage trust → timely, empathetic communication reduces secondary harm → train disclosure and maintain a clear follow-up pathway.

## Construction and representation risks

- making one candidate conceal a known error simply to generate divergence;
- conflating uncertainty about downstream harm with uncertainty that the error occurred;
- giving the detailed scenario causal findings absent from concise;
- turning a public preference for disclosure into unsupported public positions about investigation timing;
- combining disclosure, compensation and quality improvement into candidates at mismatched granularity.
