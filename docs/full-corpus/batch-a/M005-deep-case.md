## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** treating oncology service.  
**Decision question:** When family members ask that a serious cancer diagnosis be withheld, what disclosure process should clinicians use before treatment decisions require the capacitated patient's informed consent?

The bounded decision is the disclosure process when the patient's own preference for receiving or delegating information is not yet known. The case avoids a crude conflict between `Western autonomy` and a stereotyped culture. Family involvement may itself be something the patient values; the decisive question is whose preference governs the information relationship.

## Concise representation

A capacitated adult has a newly confirmed serious cancer diagnosis. Before the clinician discusses the result, close family members ask that the diagnosis and prognosis not be told directly because they fear disclosure will destroy hope. The patient has previously welcomed family involvement in routine care but has never said that the family should receive serious information first or decide what the patient is told. Treatment choices requiring informed consent must be made soon. The clinician has not yet asked how the patient wants serious medical information handled. The oncology service must decide what disclosure process to use.

## Detailed representation

A capacitated adult has a newly confirmed serious cancer diagnosis and will soon need to choose among treatment options with materially different burdens and expected benefits. Before the clinician discusses the result, close family members ask that the diagnosis and prognosis not be disclosed directly, explaining that they believe the information would cause despair and reduce the patient's willingness to pursue treatment. The patient routinely invites family members into visits and values their help, but has never said that they should receive serious information first, withhold it, or make disclosure decisions on the patient's behalf. The clinician has not yet asked whether the patient prefers full direct information, gradual disclosure, family-mediated communication or delegation of some information to relatives. The oncology service must decide what disclosure process to use before informed treatment decisions are required.

## Decision-critical facts

- adult patient has decision-making capacity;
- serious cancer diagnosis is confirmed;
- family requests nondisclosure for a protective reason;
- patient values family involvement but has not delegated control over serious information;
- patient's own disclosure preference is unknown because it has not yet been elicited;
- treatment decisions requiring informed consent are approaching.

## Explicit uncertainty

The patient's actual information preference is unknown. That is the ethical reason to ask rather than infer. The case should not imply that family involvement predicts either direct-disclosure or nondisclosure preference.

## Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral clinical ethics. The scenario assumes the patient is a capacitated adult and no legal rule independently requires clinicians to adopt the family's requested nondisclosure.

## Serious policy / position families

- ask the patient privately how they want serious information and family involvement handled, then disclose according to that preference;
- disclose the diagnosis directly and truthfully while allowing pacing, emotional support and family participation chosen by the patient;
- use family-mediated or delegated disclosure when the patient affirmatively prefers that arrangement;
- temporary staged disclosure of established information when responsive to the patient's readiness/preferences, with a definite plan rather than indefinite concealment.

A policy that lets family members independently veto disclosure to a capacitated patient lacks support in the professional guidance and is not included merely to create symmetry.

## Public / affected-community evidence

Ghoshal et al. studied 250 adult cancer patients and 250 family caregivers at a tertiary cancer center in India. Patients and caregivers differed sharply: 81.2% of patients preferred full disclosure of the illness name compared with 34.0% of caregivers, and 72.8% of patients wanted expected survival disclosed compared with 8.8% of caregivers. Patients cited preparation and planning; caregivers commonly feared loss of hope and psychological harm.

Other oncology studies in India, Pakistan and Japan document substantial support for disclosure alongside a minority preference for family-mediated, delegated or less direct communication. This supports two genuine patient-preference policy translations while undermining any assumption that a family's preference can stand in for the patient's own.

## Expert / professional recommendations

AMA Opinion 2.1.3 and the 2026 ASCO communication guideline converge on one patient-centered architecture: determine the patient's desire for information and support-network involvement; provide truthful diagnostic/prognostic information tailored to that preference and readiness; honor genuine requests not to receive information or to delegate it; do not let family request alone authorize concealment. Direct and paced disclosure are therefore not split into artificial expert alternatives.

## Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Autonomy / veracity:** informed self-government requires access to material information → family concern cannot substitute for the patient's authorization → ask the patient about information preferences and provide truthful disclosure consistent with them.
- **Relational autonomy:** patients may exercise autonomy through trusted family relationships → family mediation can be autonomy-supporting when affirmatively chosen → offer direct, shared or delegated communication according to the patient's preference.
- **Beneficence / nonmaleficence in communication:** serious information can impose psychological burden and timing/manner matter → welfare concerns justify skilled pacing and support, not indefinite deception → stage communication when responsive to patient preference and use a definite plan.
- **Respect against cultural stereotyping:** cultural practices shape but do not determine individual preferences → do not infer an information rule from ethnicity, nationality or a family request → elicit the individual patient's desired communication structure.
