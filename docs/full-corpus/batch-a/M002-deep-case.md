# M002 — Capacity, risk and supported decision-making

**Case-family identity:** M002  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** consent/capacity/refusal  
**Subdomain:** fluctuating decision-making ability; supported decision-making  
**Tags:** capacity, supported decision-making, surrogate, relational autonomy, mental health  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** hospital clinical team.  
**Decision question:** Before relying on a surrogate or temporary protective intervention, what support and reassessment should the clinical team require for this adult's high-stakes treatment decision?

The represented object is the clinical decision process at the margin of capacity, not a diagnosis-based judgment about whether a class of patients is competent. The case preserves a genuine middle state: the patient can understand and communicate much of the decision, but one decision-relevant ability is unreliable during an acute episode and time for support is limited.

## Concise representation

An adult with an acute mental-health episode must decide within about six hours whether to undergo urgent surgery for a serious infection. With simplified explanations, the patient can describe the operation, alternatives and major risks and repeatedly says they want to avoid surgery, but during periods of greater symptoms has difficulty applying the risk of life-threatening deterioration to their own situation. The preference remains consistent, a trusted supporter is available, and further communication support and reassessment can occur without using up the treatment window. For this benchmark, assume the hospital may legally use supported decision-making, a surrogate if capacity is absent, or a temporary protective intervention. The team must decide what process to require before moving away from the patient's own decision.

## Detailed representation

An adult experiencing an acute mental-health episode has a serious infection for which the clinical team recommends urgent surgery. The decision can be delayed for about six hours to improve communication and reassess, but a longer delay would materially increase the risk of life-threatening deterioration. Using short explanations and teach-back, the patient accurately describes what the operation involves, the main alternative and the possibility of major complications. The patient consistently says they want to avoid surgery. During periods when symptoms intensify, however, the patient has difficulty applying the possibility of severe deterioration to their own situation and treats a favorable outcome from antibiotics as effectively guaranteed. A trusted supporter chosen by the patient is available and the team can repeat the discussion when symptoms are quieter. For this benchmark, assume the hospital may legally use supported decision-making, a surrogate if capacity is absent, or a temporary protective intervention. The team must decide what support and reassessment must occur before moving away from the patient's own decision.

## Decision-critical facts

- high-stakes treatment decision with approximately six hours available for support and reassessment;
- patient can understand and communicate core information with support;
- stated preference is stable;
- appreciation/application of one serious consequence becomes unreliable during acute symptoms;
- a patient-chosen supporter is available;
- communication accommodations are feasible before the deadline;
- diagnosis alone is not evidence of incapacity;
- surrogate/protective pathways are legally available if capacity is absent.

## Explicit uncertainty

The central uncertainty is whether the demonstrated difficulty with appreciation is sufficient, after reasonable support, to make this particular decision non-capacitated. The case should not imply that capacity can be read directly from diagnostic status or a single interview.

The literature also contains normative disagreement over how conventional functional capacity standards should relate to strong rights-based accounts of legal capacity and supported decision-making. That disagreement belongs in the case file and candidate provenance, not in loaded scenario wording.

## Jurisdiction and time strategy

## Serious policy / position families

- maximize individualized communication/support and reassess at a time of best functioning before substituting another decision-maker;
- use a decision-specific functional capacity threshold and move to the designated surrogate if the relevant ability remains impaired after reasonable support;
- permit a temporary protective intervention only when the treatment window closes and unresolved impairment creates a grave, otherwise preventable risk;
- rights-maximizing approaches that resist substitute decision-making more strongly and continue support/interpretation of will and preferences despite substantial impairment.

The final executable slice must distinguish these as operational rules, not abstract slogans.

## Public / affected-community evidence

Knight et al. interviewed 29 people with experience of mental illness and found heterogeneous expectations for support, dependence, independence and practitioner involvement. The evidence supports individualized support rather than one assumed preference for either maximal independence or surrogate control.

The source packet also includes qualitative and systematic-review evidence from people with intellectual disability, mental-health service users, families and support workers showing recurring preferences for meaningful participation, accessible communication and individualized assistance. These sources can support affected-public-derived policy families, but diagnostic groups must not be collapsed into one homogeneous constituency.

## Expert / professional recommendations

AMA Opinion 2.1.2 recommends involving patients whose capacity is impaired to the greatest extent possible and using an appropriate surrogate when capacity is absent. WHO QualityRights materials emphasize supported decision-making and advance planning. Together they support attempting support before substitution while still requiring an explicit policy for cases in which decision-specific capacity remains absent and urgent harm cannot be deferred.

## Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Relational autonomy / supported agency:** decision-making ability can depend on communication, relationships and support → untreated interactional barriers should not be mistaken for incapacity → require reasonable support and reassessment before substitution.
- **Decision-specific autonomy plus protective beneficence:** autonomy protects choices that meet a functional threshold for the particular decision → persistent inability to appreciate a grave consequence can undermine valid authorization → use surrogate/substituted judgment when that impairment remains after support.
- **Rights-based equal recognition:** disability or mental-health diagnosis should not itself reduce moral agency → maximize interpretation of the person's will and preferences and minimize substitution → use the least restrictive process compatible with urgent clinical facts.
- **Nonmaleficence under time constraint:** delay can create irreversible harm → support cannot be open-ended when the treatment window is closing → allow a narrowly bounded protective step only after feasible support has failed and risk is grave.

## Construction and representation risks

- treating diagnosis as incapacity;
- making the functional deficit so severe that the case collapses into obvious surrogate use;
- making impairment so slight that substitution becomes a straw position;
- changing support already attempted or the six-hour window between representations;
- treating rights-based normative scholarship as affected-public preference;
- using one diagnostic community's qualitative evidence as universal patient preference;
- writing `temporary protective intervention` without keeping it narrow and time-bound.
