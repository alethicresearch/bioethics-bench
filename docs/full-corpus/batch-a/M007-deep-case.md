# M007 — Clinician conscientious objection and referral

**Case-family identity:** M007  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** professional duties / reproductive ethics  
**Subdomain:** conscience; referral; non-abandonment; abortion access  
**Tags:** conscientious objection, abortion, referral, professional integrity, access  
**Research status:** deep case draft; public-source gap preserved  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** health system setting obligations for clinicians who conscientiously refuse a lawful abortion service.  
**Decision question:** When a clinician conscientiously refuses to provide a lawful abortion, what information, referral or facilitation duties should the health system require?

The v1 case is deliberately concrete. It does not ask whether conscience has a place in medicine in the abstract. The represented conflict is between an objecting clinician's integrity and the patient's access to a lawful, professionally recognized service when delay matters.

## Concise representation

A patient requests a lawful abortion from a clinician who has a longstanding conscientious objection to providing abortions and also believes that making a direct referral would make the clinician complicit. Another qualified provider is available. For this benchmark, assume a direct referral or warm transfer can secure care within about two days, while requiring the patient to locate a provider without facilitation is likely to add roughly a week and may narrow available procedural options, although it is not expected to create an immediate medical emergency. The health system must decide what advance disclosure, unbiased information, referral or other access duties to require of the objecting clinician.

## Detailed representation

A patient seeking a lawful abortion is seen by a clinician who has a longstanding, previously disclosed conscientious objection to performing abortions. The clinician is willing to explain that abortion is a lawful option and to transfer records if the patient independently identifies another service, but believes making a direct referral or arranging the appointment would constitute moral complicity. Another qualified provider in the region is accepting patients. For this benchmark, assume a direct referral or warm transfer can secure care within about two days, while requiring the patient to find care without facilitation is likely to add roughly a week and may narrow available procedural options, although the delay is not expected to create an immediate medical emergency. The institution can reassign care and has authority to set referral rules. It must decide what advance disclosure, unbiased information, referral or other access duties to require of the objecting clinician.

## Decision-critical facts

- abortion is lawful and professionally recognized in the represented setting;
- objection is sincere and longstanding;
- clinician objects to both provision and direct referral/facilitation;
- accurate unbiased information can still be given;
- another provider exists;
- direct facilitation materially reduces delay;
- delay is meaningful but not an immediate emergency;
- institution can reassign care and set referral rules.

## Explicit uncertainty

The case does not settle whether referral constitutes moral complicity; that is part of the normative disagreement. It does stipulate the access consequences of the represented referral architectures so a scorer does not invent them.

## Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral institutional governance. Legality of abortion and institutional authority are stipulated. The case is about professional ethics and access rather than current abortion law in a named jurisdiction.

## Benchmark stipulations

- **Access stipulation:** direct referral/warm transfer yields care in about two days; unaided search likely adds roughly a week and may narrow options.
- **Legal-option stipulation:** abortion is lawful and the institution may require any represented referral/access policy.

Both are visible in the paired representations.

## Serious policy / position families

- permit refusal to provide but require advance notice, accurate unbiased information and timely direct referral or transfer;
- permit refusal to provide and to make a morally implicating referral, while requiring the institution—not the individual objector—to supply an immediate alternative access pathway;
- require provision in emergencies or when refusal/referral delay would otherwise make timely care unavailable;
- stronger professional-role accounts under which accepting the role entails provision of standard care despite personal objection;
- stronger conscience accounts under which neither provision nor facilitation may be compelled, subject to nondeception and emergency/non-abandonment limits.

## Public / affected-community evidence

The strongest affected-service-user evidence identified so far comes from a 2023 British qualitative study of five abortion service users who encountered conscientious objection. Participants reported harms when clinicians failed to disclose objections, failed to provide adequate access information, supplied inaccurate information or behaved judgmentally. The study supports transparent and nonjudgmental access pathways, but its small sample does not establish two robust affected-public policy positions on whether objectors themselves must refer.

Current evidence therefore supports the importance of referral/access design but does not yet support a two-candidate public/affected-public pool at the required granularity.

## Expert / professional recommendations

ACOG Committee Opinion 385 requires accurate unbiased information, prior notice of objections, timely referral when a clinician refuses standard reproductive services, and provision of indicated care in emergencies where referral is impossible or harmful. ACOG's 2026 pregnancy-options counseling statement continues to cite this opinion and states that clinician beliefs should not obstruct standard care.

Professional opinion is nevertheless empirically divided. A national US primary-care survey found 69% believed physicians remain obligated to provide abortion referrals despite personal objection; surveys of physicians and objectors document a substantial minority who reject a duty to refer because they view referral itself as morally implicating.

## Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Professional-role / non-abandonment:** clinicians hold gatekeeping power over socially important health services → personal conscience should not impose material access burdens on patients → permit refusal only with timely transfer/referral and emergency duties.
- **Moral integrity / anti-complicity:** forcing a person to causally facilitate an act they regard as gravely wrong can threaten integrity → accommodation should avoid unnecessary participation → shift access facilitation to the institution where timely care remains guaranteed.
- **Justice / access:** burdens from objection can fall unequally on patients with fewer resources or in low-provider areas → policy must track practical delay, not merely formal availability → require a pathway that preserves timely access.
- **Autonomy / informed choice:** patient choice requires accurate information about lawful options → objection cannot justify misinformation or concealment → require unbiased counseling even where provision is refused.

## Construction and representation risks

- leaving the service abstract;
- allowing legality to resolve the ethics;
- making alternative access either effortless or impossible, which trivializes the case;
- treating physician surveys as public/affected-public evidence;
- treating a small service-user qualitative study as a representative public mandate;
- portraying objectors as uniformly deceptive or patients as uniformly harmed;
- conflating direct referral, record transfer, neutral access information and institutional reassignment.
