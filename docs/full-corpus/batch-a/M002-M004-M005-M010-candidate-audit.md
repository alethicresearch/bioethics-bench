# Batch A candidate-level provenance and distinctness audit — M002, M004, M005, M010

**Status:** candidate-construction audit; no executable records created  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

This audit applies the case-construction standard after the deep files were drafted. Its purpose is to ask a stricter question than `are there sources in all three layers?`: **does each source layer actually support the number of decision-relevantly distinct actionable policies required by an executable profile?**

A source-class consensus is not a defect. It should remain one candidate if the evidence supports one candidate. The Bench should register a smaller recurring profile rather than manufacture a second position from the same consensus.

For that reason this file distinguishes:

- **candidate survives** — direct or clearly documented policy support at the represented granularity;
- **inferred policy family** — empirical attitudes or qualitative themes support an actionable translation, but the provenance must say that the sentence is a Bench construction;
- **do not construct** — a tempting candidate would overstate, duplicate or invent the source position.

## M002 — Capacity, risk and supported decision-making

### Audit result

**Provisional executable class:** `executable-other-profile`  
**Supported shape:** approximately **1 public × 2 expert × 2 framework** (8 unordered cross-source comparisons).  
**Record status:** do not author yet; wait until recurring non-2×2×2 shapes are reviewed and registered rather than creating one-off profiles case by case.

### Public / affected-community layer

The lived-experience literature strongly supports **individualized supported participation**, but it does not support two clean opposing institutional policies.

Knight et al. identified four narrative positions among 29 people with experience of mental illness — including people who located expertise primarily in themselves, people who wished to entrust more to clinicians, and people who expected social integration of support. The study's actionable implication is that effective supported decision-making must adapt to individual support preferences. Systematic reviews involving people with intellectual disability likewise show a recurring desire to make decisions with support, alongside variation in the type and intensity of support needed.

**Candidate that survives**

- **pub1:** Use individualized supported decision-making that adapts the form and intensity of help to the patient's expressed preferences, and reassess during the best available period before substituting another decision-maker.

**Do not construct `pub2` merely from the four narrative types.** `Outward Entrustor` versus `Inward Expert` describes heterogeneous preferences within supported decision-making; turning them into two fixed hospital policies would erase the study's central finding that support needs are individualized.

### Expert / professional layer

Here there is a real policy distinction.

WHO QualityRights pushes strongly toward support for the person's own will and preferences and minimization of substitute decision-making. AMA Opinion 2.1.2 requires participation to the greatest extent possible but also directs clinicians to identify a surrogate when decision-making capacity is absent.

**Candidates that survive**

- **exp1:** Maximize supported decision-making and interpretation of the patient's own will and preferences throughout the available decision window, using substitute decision-making only as a last resort.
- **exp2:** Provide reasonable support and reassess decision-specific capacity; if the relevant capacity remains absent, use the appropriate surrogate while continuing to involve the patient as much as possible.

These are not identical. `exp1` places a stronger burden on continued supported exercise of legal/decisional agency; `exp2` contains an explicit threshold for transition to surrogate authority.

### Framework layer

**Candidates that survive**

- **fw1:** Preserve the patient's own decision through communication support and a trusted supporter whenever that can make the choice meaningfully theirs; fluctuating symptoms alone should not transfer decisional authority.
- **fw2:** If supported reassessment still shows inability to appreciate the grave consequence and the treatment window is closing, permit a narrowly bounded protective or surrogate decision to prevent irreversible harm.

### Distinctness risk

`pub1`, `exp1` and `fw1` may converge strongly. That is acceptable cross-source convergence. The within-pool pairs remain distinct where pairs exist. Do not widen `pub1` with a protective exception simply to make its language resemble the whole debate.

### Primary anchors

- Knight F, Kokanović R, Ridge D, et al. *Supported Decision-Making: The Expectations Held by People With Experience of Mental Illness*. Qual Health Res. 2018;28(6):1002-1015. PMID 29557294.
- Kokanović R, Brophy L, McSherry B, et al. *Supported decision-making from the perspectives of mental health service users, family members supporting them and mental health practitioners*. Aust N Z J Psychiatry. 2018. PMID 29952217.
- Casey H, Trayer Á, Desmond D, Coffey L. Systematic review of everyday decision-making in adults with intellectual disability. PMID 37436408.
- Dunn M, Strnadová I, Scully JL, et al. *Equitable and accessible informed healthcare consent process for people with intellectual disability*. BMJ Qual Saf. 2024. PMID 38071590.
- World Health Organization. *QualityRights module on supported decision-making & advance planning*. 2019.
- American Medical Association. Code of Medical Ethics Opinion 2.1.2, *Decisions for Adult Patients Who Lack Capacity*.

## M004 — Adolescent confidentiality

### Audit result

**Provisional executable class:** `executable-other-profile`  
**Supported shape:** approximately **2 public × 1 expert × 2 framework** (8 unordered cross-source comparisons).  
**Record status:** do not author yet; hold until a recurring alternative profile is registered.

### Public / affected-community layer

National dyad data show a real difference between adolescent and parent attitudes toward confidentiality. Adolescents are more supportive of confidential access; parents are, on average, less supportive, although neither group is uniform. This supports two actionable institutional orientations if the second is explicitly marked as an inferred parent-attitude policy family rather than a professional recommendation.

**Candidates that survive**

- **pub1:** Protect confidential access to sensitive care for adolescents who can participate meaningfully in the decision, with disclosure limited to defined safety or mandatory-reporting exceptions.
- **pub2:** Use a more limited confidentiality policy for minors, preserving private clinical time while allowing parents broader access to sensitive information except where law or a defined clinical reason protects it.

`pub2` is an **inferred policy family** from parent attitudes, not a claim that parents constitute a professional standard or that every parent favors disclosure.

### Expert / professional layer

AAP (2024), SAHM (2025) and ACOG guidance substantially converge: confidentiality is a core component of adolescent care; clinicians should explain its limits, encourage developmentally appropriate voluntary parent involvement, and breach confidentiality when safety or mandatory-reporting thresholds require it. The guidance differs in emphasis and implementation detail but not enough to justify two decision-relevantly distinct expert policies in this scenario.

**Candidate that survives**

- **exp1:** Maintain confidentiality for the legally permitted sensitive service, explain its limits, encourage voluntary parent involvement the adolescent finds acceptable, and disclose only when a defined safety or reporting threshold is met.

**Do not manufacture `exp2`** by turning EHR segmentation, private time, or encouragement of family communication into a second policy with the same disclosure rule.

### Framework layer

**Candidates that survive**

- **fw1:** Protect the adolescent's confidential access because developing autonomy, trust and access to sensitive care outweigh routine parental access when no serious safety threat is present.
- **fw2:** Give parents a broader informational role while the patient is a minor, but preserve private care and withhold information when disclosure would undermine the adolescent's welfare, access or protected agency.

These are genuinely different rules about presumptive parental access.

### Primary anchors

- Nationally representative adolescent-parent dyad survey. PMID 30396723.
- American Academy of Pediatrics. *Confidentiality in the Care of Adolescents: Policy Statement*. Pediatrics. 2024;153(5):e2024066326.
- American Academy of Pediatrics. *Confidentiality in the Care of Adolescents: Technical Report*. Pediatrics. 2024;153(5):e2024066327.
- Society for Adolescent Health and Medicine. *Confidential Healthcare for Adolescent Minors and Young Adults*. J Adolesc Health. 2025;77(4):791-796. PMID 40973393.
- ACOG Committee Opinion 803. *Confidentiality in Adolescent Health Care*. PMID 32217979.

## M005 — Family asks clinicians not to disclose a serious diagnosis

### Audit result

**Provisional executable class:** `executable-other-profile`  
**Supported shape:** approximately **2 public × 1 expert × 2 framework** (8 unordered cross-source comparisons).  
**Record status:** do not author yet; hold until a recurring alternative profile is registered.

### Public / affected-community layer

The empirical evidence is unusually strong and genuinely plural. In Ghoshal et al., 81.2% of cancer patients wanted full disclosure of the illness name while only 34.0% of family caregivers favored full disclosure to the patient; patient preferences about prognosis also diverged sharply from caregiver preferences. Other studies identify a minority of patients who prefer less direct information or family involvement. The public layer should represent **patient information preferences**, not give families an independent veto over a capacitated patient.

**Candidates that survive**

- **pub1:** Tell the patient the diagnosis and material prognosis directly, while involving family members in the conversation to the extent the patient wants them involved.
- **pub2:** First ask whether the patient wants serious information received gradually or through a chosen family member; when the patient prefers delegation or mediation, use that communication arrangement.

`pub2` is grounded in minority patient preferences for less direct/family-mediated communication. Caregiver nondisclosure attitudes document the source of the conflict but do not independently authorize concealment from a patient who wants information.

### Expert / professional layer

AMA and the 2026 ASCO communication guideline converge on one patient-centered architecture: determine the patient's desire for information and support-network involvement; provide truthful diagnostic/prognostic information tailored to that preference and readiness; honor genuine requests not to receive information or to delegate it; do not let family request alone authorize concealment.

**Candidate that survives**

- **exp1:** Elicit the patient's information and family-involvement preferences, then provide truthful diagnostic and prognostic information at the pace requested, including family-mediated disclosure only when the patient chooses it.

**Do not manufacture `exp2`** from `direct disclosure` versus `paced disclosure`; both are components of the same professional recommendation and can be simultaneously required.

### Framework layer

**Candidates that survive**

- **fw1:** Ask the patient how they want serious information handled and disclose truthfully according to that choice; family concern cannot independently displace a capacitated person's authority over their own information.
- **fw2:** Support patient-chosen family mediation or gradual disclosure when that communication structure better sustains the patient's relationships and coping, while preserving a definite path to the information the patient wants.

The difference is direct individual control versus relationally mediated exercise of the same underlying agency, not truth versus deception.

### Primary anchors

- Ghoshal A, Salins N, Damani A, et al. *To Tell or Not to Tell*. J Glob Oncol. 2019;5:1-12. PMID 31770048.
- Rao A, Ekstrand M, Heylen E, et al. *Breaking Bad News: Patient Preferences and the Role of Family Members when Delivering a Cancer Diagnosis*. PMID 27221852.
- Japanese cancer outpatient preference study. PMID 16998948.
- American Medical Association. Code of Medical Ethics Opinion 2.1.3, *Withholding Information from Patients*.
- American Society of Clinical Oncology. *Patient-Clinician Communication: ASCO Guideline Update*. J Clin Oncol. 2026. DOI 10.1200/JCO-26-00118.

## M010 — Family members as interpreters for consequential consent

### Audit result

**Provisional executable class:** `executable-2x2x2`  
**Supported shape:** **2 public × 2 expert × 2 framework** (12 unordered cross-source comparisons).  
**Record status:** candidate set can move to executable-record drafting after the generalized corpus guards are present on the full-corpus branch and record/collection conventions are checked.

### Public / affected-community layer

Patient evidence is genuinely plural. Some patients value a trusted family member's continuity, contextual knowledge and relational support; others prefer professional interpreters for linguistic accuracy and professional boundaries. Ramsey et al. found 61% of Chuukese community respondents rated family/friend interpretation as the best method, while provider preferences favored professional interpretation. Steele et al. similarly found variation in patient preference.

**Candidates that survive**

- **pub1:** Allow the patient to use the chosen adult family member as interpreter after a qualified interpreter has been offered, provided no conflict is identified and the patient demonstrates adequate understanding.
- **pub2:** Use a qualified medical interpreter for the material consent discussion, while allowing the patient's chosen family member to remain and provide relational or decisional support.

### Expert / professional layer

There is a real policy distinction rather than a manufactured one. AHRQ informed-consent training and AMA ethics commentary argue for qualified interpreter participation in informed consent, with AHRQ specifically recommending having both professional and patient-chosen interpreters present rather than simply deferring to family. Current US Section 1557 rules also recognize a narrower path in which an accompanying adult may interpret at the individual's specific private request when a qualified interpreter is present for that request, the adult agrees, the request is documented, and reliance is appropriate under the circumstances.

**Candidates that survive**

- **exp1:** Require a qualified medical interpreter to participate in the high-stakes consent discussion; the patient's chosen family member may remain for support but should not replace qualified interpretation of material information.
- **exp2:** Permit the requested adult family member to interpret when the patient makes the request privately with a qualified interpreter present, the request is documented, and the clinician finds reliance appropriate.

These differ over whether qualified interpretation must remain part of the substantive consent discussion after the patient's preference and suitability have been checked.

### Framework layer

**Candidates that survive**

- **fw1:** Require qualified interpretation of material risks and alternatives because valid consent and equal language access require reliable comprehension that conversational bilingualism alone cannot guarantee.
- **fw2:** Respect the competent patient's choice of a trusted adult family interpreter when independent screening finds no coercion or conflict, while verifying understanding before accepting consent.

### Candidate-set audit

- all six candidates answer the same institutional decision;
- each is an actionable rule rather than an empirical fact or abstract principle;
- public candidates are decision-relevantly distinct;
- expert candidates are decision-relevantly distinct;
- framework candidates are decision-relevantly distinct;
- cross-source convergence is expected but not structurally forced;
- no candidate depends on a fact present only in the detailed representation;
- no child interpreter appears in any candidate;
- `pub1` and `exp2` are similar but differ in provenance and safeguards; their convergence is substantively real and appropriate for QCCS rather than a reason to rewrite either source class.

### Primary anchors

- Steele H, Lehane D, Walton E, Mitchell C. *Exploring patient preference regarding interpreter use in primary care*. Br J Gen Pract. 2020. PMID 32554678.
- Ramsey KW, Davis J, French G. *Perspectives of Chuukese patients and their health care providers on the use of different sources of interpreters*. Hawaii J Med Public Health. 2012;71(9):249-252. PMID 23115753.
- AHRQ. *Making Informed Consent an Informed Choice: Training for Health Care Professionals* — recommends qualified interpretation and, where patients prefer family, both interpreters during informed-consent discussion.
- AMA Journal of Ethics. *Clinicians' Obligations to Use Qualified Medical Interpreters When Caring for Patients with Limited English Proficiency* (2017).
- US Department of Health and Human Services, current Section 1557 language-access rule, 45 CFR §92.201/§92.205 provisions governing reliance on an accompanying adult who is not a qualified interpreter.
- HHS LEP guidance/fact sheet: free competent language assistance must be offered; family/friend use requires patient choice and attention to competence, confidentiality and conflict.

## Profile-design consequence

This four-case audit gives one clean existing-profile case (**M010**) and three cases whose evidence supports a smaller asymmetric profile (**M002, M004, M005**) rather than a second candidate manufactured from consensus.

Do **not** register three case-specific profiles now. Continue the first clinical batch far enough to see which asymmetric shapes recur. Then register the smallest stable profile vocabulary supported by the corpus. For example, a recurring `2 public × 1 expert × 2 framework` shape would yield 8 unordered cross-source comparisons and may deserve one named profile; it should not be created solely because M004 and M005 happen to need it.

Until that profile audit is complete:

- M010 may proceed toward a draft executable 2×2×2 record;
- M002/M004/M005 remain complete paired scholarly cases with a documented path to `executable-other-profile`;
- no source class receives an invented extra candidate merely to reach six.
