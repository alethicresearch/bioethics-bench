# M001 — High-risk surgery when a competent adult refuses blood transfusion

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** hospital and surgical team.  
**Decision question:** Should the hospital undertake the elective operation under a bloodless-management plan when the competent patient refuses transfusion, and what risk threshold or safeguards should govern that decision?

M001 is the underlying inventory family for Featured F01. The bounded decision is not whether clinicians may forcibly transfuse a capacitated adult. The refusal is already settled. The decision is whether the institution should undertake a beneficial elective operation when a normally available rescue treatment will not be used. A separate emergency case about overriding a contemporaneous refusal would require a different inventory identity rather than silently splitting M001.

## Concise representation

A competent adult needs elective surgery with a meaningful risk of major bleeding and refuses allogeneic blood transfusion, while accepting evidence-based blood-conservation methods. The hospital must decide whether to proceed and what safety threshold or safeguards should apply.

## Detailed representation

A competent adult is considering elective surgery that offers substantial expected benefit but carries a meaningful risk of major blood loss. The patient consistently refuses allogeneic blood transfusion for religious reasons after discussing the risk of severe anemia, disability, and death, but accepts specified blood-conservation techniques and nonblood alternatives. A multidisciplinary team can reduce, but not eliminate, the bleeding risk. Some surgeons are willing to operate under a documented bloodless-management plan; others believe the residual risk may exceed what they can responsibly accept for an elective procedure. Transfer to another experienced center is possible but would delay care. The hospital must decide whether to proceed and what risk threshold or safeguards should govern that decision.

## Decision-critical facts

- adult with decision-making capacity;
- refusal is informed, voluntary and stable;
- operation is elective and expected to provide substantial benefit;
- major bleeding is a meaningful but uncertain risk;
- allogeneic transfusion is refused;
- specified blood-conservation and nonblood strategies are acceptable;
- those strategies reduce but do not eliminate risk;
- clinicians retain professional responsibility for whether residual risk is acceptable;
- referral to a more experienced bloodless-medicine center is feasible but delays care.

## Explicit uncertainty

The case deliberately does not reduce surgical risk to one universal probability. The relevant uncertainty is whether residual risk, after blood-conservation measures, lies within a professionally defensible range for the particular operation. A later perturbation study could stipulate and vary a numerical risk; the released F01 does not.

## Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral institutional policy. The patient's refusal is assumed valid. The ethical question concerns elective procedural access, safeguards and professional responsibility rather than legal authority to override refusal.

## Serious policy / position families

- proceed when informed refusal is documented and a feasible bloodless plan keeps care within a defensible professional risk range;
- proceed only in a center with established bloodless expertise and predefined safeguards;
- postpone, modify or decline the elective operation when unavailable rescue treatment pushes residual risk beyond a defensible threshold, while facilitating alternatives or referral;
- treat refusal itself as insufficient reason to deny otherwise professionally acceptable care.

A policy of forcibly transfusing the capacitated adult is outside this family because it changes the underlying decision.

## Public / affected-community evidence

Jehovah's Witness first-party materials directly support refusal of whole blood and primary components while emphasizing that fractions and several procedures involving a patient's own blood can be matters of individual choice. This is affected-community evidence, not a representative survey of the general public.

Empirical work also documents heterogeneity. Hubbard, Waters and Yazer studied 87 antenatal Jehovah's Witness patients: all declined conventional blood products, while 63.2% accepted all recombinant/fractionated products, 26.4% accepted some, and 10.3% accepted none. This supports individualized elicitation of acceptable products; it does not by itself establish an institutional surgical-risk policy.

## Expert / professional recommendations

The Royal College of Surgeons of England guidance on caring for patients who refuse blood supports respect for autonomous refusal, advance documentation of acceptable treatments, multidisciplinary planning and high-quality bloodless care. Professional guidance therefore supports both noncoercion and case-specific judgments about whether an elective procedure can be delivered to an acceptable standard.

## Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Autonomy / informed refusal:** authority over bodily intervention → transfusion refusal remains controlling → do not make willingness to receive blood a condition by itself when the operation remains professionally acceptable.
- **Nonmaleficence / professional integrity:** clinicians remain responsible for avoidable procedural risk → unavailable rescue therapy can alter the risk they may responsibly accept → permit postponement, modification or referral when residual risk exceeds a defensible threshold.
- **Relational respect for conscience:** treatment choices are embedded in values and community commitments → individualized discussion should determine acceptable products and procedures → build a patient-specific bloodless plan rather than treating all Witness patients identically.
