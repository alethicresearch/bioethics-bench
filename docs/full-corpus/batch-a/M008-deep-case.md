# M008 — Confidentiality versus serious preventable harm to a third party

**Case-family identity:** M008  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** professional duties / mental-health ethics

**Tags:** confidentiality, duty to warn, duty to protect, threat, mental health  
**Research status:** deep case draft; public-source gap preserved  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** mental-health clinician/health system.  
**Decision question:** When a patient presents a serious, credible threat to an identifiable person, what confidentiality rule should govern targeted disclosure intended to reduce that risk?

The case is not a generic `confidentiality versus safety` vignette. It fixes identifiability, seriousness, probability, available mitigation and legal permission so the represented disagreement concerns the disclosure threshold and the least-intrusive response.

## Concise representation

During outpatient treatment, a patient tells a mental-health clinician about a concrete plan to seriously injure an identifiable former partner, has access to the means described, and refuses voluntary warning or safety planning. After assessment, the clinician judges serious physical harm to that person reasonably probable rather than merely possible. A limited disclosure to people able to reduce the risk could materially improve safety. For this benchmark, assume disclosure is legally permitted but not automatically required in these circumstances and that the clinician may choose among the represented policies. The service must decide whether confidentiality should be maintained, whether targeted disclosure should occur, and what threshold and limits should govern it.

## Detailed representation

During outpatient treatment, a patient describes a specific plan to seriously injure an identifiable former partner, identifies when the confrontation is intended to occur, and has access to the means described. The clinician conducts a structured risk assessment and, based on the specificity, means and recent escalation, judges serious physical harm to the former partner reasonably probable rather than merely possible. The patient refuses to contact the person, involve a trusted supporter or participate in another voluntary safety plan. A limited disclosure to the intended person and/or an appropriate protective service could materially reduce risk, but broader disclosure could damage privacy and the therapeutic relationship. For this benchmark, assume disclosure is legally permitted but not automatically required and that the clinician may choose among the represented policies. The service must decide what confidentiality rule and disclosure threshold should apply.

## Decision-critical facts

- intended third party is identifiable;
- threat concerns serious physical harm;
- plan is concrete and means are available;
- clinician judges harm reasonably probable, not merely conceivable;
- voluntary patient-mediated mitigation has been offered and refused;
- limited disclosure can materially reduce risk;
- disclosure is legally permitted but not automatically required for the benchmark.

## Explicit uncertainty

Risk prediction is inherently imperfect. The scenario therefore stipulates a professional judgment of reasonable probability without pretending certainty. The ethical problem remains how much predictive uncertainty justifies overriding confidentiality and how narrow the disclosure should be.

## Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral institutional ethics. Duty-to-warn/protect law varies substantially, so the case stipulates legal permission without a mandatory rule.

## Benchmark stipulations

- **Legal-option stipulation:** disclosure is legally permitted but not automatically required.
- **Risk stipulation:** serious physical harm to an identifiable person is judged reasonably probable after assessment.
- voluntary mitigation has been attempted and refused; limited disclosure could materially reduce risk.

All are present in both representations.

## Serious policy / position families

- maintain confidentiality unless a still higher/imminent threshold is met, while intensifying treatment and voluntary safety planning;
- make minimum-necessary targeted disclosure once serious harm to an identifiable person is reasonably probable and voluntary mitigation is insufficient;
- use a stepped duty-to-protect policy that first seeks patient-mediated or clinical containment and escalates to targeted disclosure when those measures cannot adequately reduce risk;
- broader protective-action approaches involving law enforcement or emergency intervention when the specified threshold is met.

## Public / affected-community evidence

Rubanowitz's 1987 public survey found strong general expectations against unauthorized psychotherapy disclosure while also finding support for breaching confidentiality in circumstances including planned murder and suicide threats. Miller and Thelen's 1986 work similarly documented strong beliefs in psychotherapy confidentiality and a desire to be informed of its limits.

These studies establish genuine public tension rather than a one-sided intuition. They are, however, old, context-specific and do not provide a contemporary affected-public comparison of the precise threshold represented here. No sufficiently strong recent direct public/service-user evidence was identified in this audit.

## Expert / professional recommendations

AMA Opinion 3.2.1 permits disclosure to people situated to mitigate a threat when there is a reasonable probability of serious physical harm to an identifiable individual and recommends limiting disclosure to the minimum necessary. Clinical and legal scholarship after *Tarasoff* documents substantial jurisdictional variation and continuing concern about prediction, trust and overly broad warning practices.

## Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Confidentiality / therapeutic trust:** effective mental-health care depends on reliable privacy → unnecessary warnings can deter candor and care → retain a demanding threshold and disclose no more than necessary.
- **Duty to rescue/protect:** confidentiality is not absolute when a specific person faces serious preventable harm → an identifiable, reasonably probable threat creates a protective duty → permit targeted disclosure to people able to reduce the risk.
- **Proportionality / least infringement:** protective benefit should be achieved with the smallest privacy intrusion → voluntary mitigation and clinical containment should be attempted where feasible → escalate to minimum-necessary disclosure only when lesser measures are inadequate.
- **Epistemic humility:** violence prediction is uncertain and false positives have costs → policy should not equate any disturbing statement with dangerousness → require documented risk features and reviewable thresholds.

## Construction and representation risks

- using vague `dangerousness` language;
- omitting identifiability, probability or means;
- treating legal doctrine as ethically uniform;
- assuming risk assessment can produce certainty;
- converting historical public acceptance of warnings for planned murder into a contemporary policy mandate at this exact threshold;
- making the confidentiality candidate absurd by denying disclosure even under any level of danger;
- changing the availability of voluntary mitigation between representations.
