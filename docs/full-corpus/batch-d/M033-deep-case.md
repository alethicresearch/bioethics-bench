# M033 — Advance request for assisted dying after loss of capacity in dementia

**Case-family identity:** M033  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** dementia ethics / assisted dying  
**Subdomain:** advance euthanasia directive; capacity loss; precedent autonomy  
**Tags:** dementia, advance directive, euthanasia, precedent autonomy, capacity, Netherlands  
**Research status:** deep case draft; jurisdiction-specific  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** physician/assisted-dying service in the Netherlands.  
**Decision question:** When a person made a competent, specific advance euthanasia directive for advanced dementia but can no longer communicate a current request, what policy should govern whether euthanasia may proceed?

This differs from M027. M027 concerns refusal of life-prolonging treatment; M033 concerns an affirmative clinician act intended to end life after the person has lost contemporaneous capacity.

## 2. Concise representation

A person with advanced Alzheimer disease can no longer make or communicate complex decisions. Years earlier, while fully capable, the person completed and repeatedly reaffirmed a specific advance euthanasia directive requesting euthanasia if dementia progressed to permanent inability to recognize close family and make independent decisions. Those conditions are now met. For this benchmark, assume the person is not persistently resisting care and sometimes appears calm, but clinicians cannot obtain a current verbal request or reliably establish how the person now experiences the situation. Dutch law permits an advance directive to replace an oral request only if all other due-care criteria are met. The physician must decide what policy should govern whether the advance request can be acted upon.

## 3. Detailed representation

A person with advanced Alzheimer disease has lost capacity to understand or decide about euthanasia and can no longer communicate a coherent current treatment preference. Six years earlier, while fully decisionally capable and after detailed discussion with a physician, the person completed an advance euthanasia directive stating that euthanasia should be performed if dementia progressed to a permanent inability to recognize close family and make independent decisions. The directive was reaffirmed on several later visits while capacity remained intact. Those specified conditions are now present. The person sometimes appears calm and receptive to familiar touch and music, but also has periods of fear and agitation; clinicians cannot establish a stable current judgment about whether the person experiences life as unbearable. For this benchmark, assume there is no consistent contemporaneous verbal request and no persistent physical resistance to ordinary care. Under current Dutch law, a competent advance directive can replace an oral euthanasia request after capacity is lost, but the physician must still establish all other due-care criteria. The physician must decide whether a specific prior request may control, whether current suffering/behavior must independently support implementation, or whether euthanasia should require contemporaneous communicative confirmation.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- specific advance euthanasia directive made and reaffirmed with capacity;
- advanced dementia now satisfies the directive's triggering description;
- patient lacks current decisional capacity and coherent oral request;
- current experiential state is mixed/uncertain, not stipulated as continuous suffering;
- no persistent resistance to ordinary care;
- Dutch law permits advance directive substitution in principle but still requires all due-care criteria;
- affirmative euthanasia, not treatment refusal, is at issue.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

The decisive uncertainty concerns whether and how unbearable suffering can be established after communicative capacity is lost, and how much moral/legal authority a prior directive should carry when current experiential interests are not clearly negative. The benchmark does not assume that a directive alone satisfies all due-care criteria.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Explicitly Netherlands, 2026. Current RTE guidance states that an advance directive may replace an oral request when the patient can no longer express wishes, provided the directive was made with decisional competence and the other due-care criteria are met.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- **Directive stipulation:** directive was specific, informed and repeatedly reaffirmed while competent.
- **Trigger stipulation:** the dementia state described in the directive is now present.
- **Capacity stipulation:** no current capacity to request or revoke euthanasia coherently.
- **Current-state stipulation:** behavior/affect do not establish continuous unbearable suffering or a stable wish to die.
- **Legal stipulation:** current Dutch law permits advance-directive substitution in principle but does not waive other due-care criteria.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- allow a clear advance euthanasia directive to provide the request component and assess all remaining due-care criteria from current clinical evidence and prior values;
- permit implementation only when the advance directive is specific **and** current observable suffering/behavior strongly confirms the anticipated condition;
- require contemporaneous communicative confirmation of an assisted-dying request and therefore do not perform euthanasia after the person can no longer express it;
- require exceptional multi-clinician review for advance-request dementia cases even where ordinary legal criteria are formally available.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Dutch public opinion is substantially more permissive than physician opinion. A national survey found 60% of the general public agreed that people with advanced dementia should be eligible for euthanasia based on a prior request (PMID 32652560). Earlier Dutch stakeholder research found 77% of the general public, compared with one third of physicians, accepted euthanasia in severe dementia based on an advance directive (PMID 22695742). Qualitative work shows public respondents often focus on prior autonomy and feared degradation, while physicians emphasize difficulty verifying voluntariness and unbearable suffering once communication is lost (PMID 25630339).

These sources are directly relevant to the represented policy, though candidate wording must avoid equating public support for eligibility with support for acting on any directive regardless of current facts.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

Current RTE guidance permits a competent advance directive to replace an oral request when communication is no longer possible, but all due-care criteria remain mandatory. Dutch GPs remain markedly cautious: in a vignette survey, 42.2% disagreed that an advance directive can replace an oral request when communication is impossible; many also rejected family initiation of the euthanasia trajectory (PMID 34789166). Review of Dutch dementia EAS cases found greater scrutiny and more due-care problems in advance-request cases than concurrent-request cases.

Thus professional practice contains a genuine divide between legally available advance-request implementation under strict review and a contemporaneous-communication threshold favored by many clinicians.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Precedent autonomy:** a capable person's settled values about the future shape what should happen after capacity is lost → loss of capacity should not nullify a specific advance request → allow implementation when stated conditions and other safeguards are met.
- **Current welfare / present-person interests:** the current person remains a subject of experiences even without decisional capacity → an earlier self should not authorize death when present suffering and wishes cannot be established → require strong current evidence or refuse implementation.
- **Irreversibility / precaution:** euthanasia cannot be corrected and the person cannot contemporaneously confirm or revoke → evidentiary uncertainty should be resolved toward nonperformance → require current communicative confirmation.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- treating the advance directive as automatically sufficient under Dutch law;
- treating calm affect as proof of wellbeing or agitation as proof of unbearable suffering;
- conflating M033 with M027 treatment refusal;
- making family preference a substitute request;
- implying Dutch law represents international consensus;
- omitting current-state uncertainty and thereby making precedent autonomy trivially decisive.
