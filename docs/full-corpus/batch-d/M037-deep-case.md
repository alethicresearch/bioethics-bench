# M037 — Unilateral limitation of a newly requested nonbeneficial escalation

**Case-family identity:** M037  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** critical-care ethics / professional duties  
**Subdomain:** treatment non-offer; escalation; surrogate conflict; fair process  
**Tags:** ECMO, nonbeneficial treatment, treatment escalation, surrogate, fair process, professional integrity  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** ICU/hospital service.  
**Decision question:** When a surrogate requests a **new escalation** of intensive treatment that clinicians judge cannot reasonably achieve the patient's agreed goal, what fair process should govern whether the intervention is offered?

M037 is distinguished from M009 (CPR-specific), M026 (withdrawal of already ongoing support) and M036 (withholding/withdrawal equivalence). Here current support continues; the disputed act is adding a new, burdensome intervention such as ECMO.

## 2. Concise representation

An adult in the ICU lacks decision-making capacity and is receiving mechanical ventilation and vasopressors for advanced irreversible disease. The surrogate asks clinicians to initiate ECMO if cardiopulmonary failure worsens. For this benchmark, assume two experienced critical-care specialists agree that ECMO cannot reasonably restore the patient's agreed goal of surviving to leave intensive care and would add major invasive burdens; existing ventilation, symptom treatment and other current care will continue while conflict is reviewed. The surrogate understands the prognosis but requests “everything possible.” The hospital must decide what process should govern non-offer of the requested escalation.

## 3. Detailed representation

An adult with advanced irreversible disease has developed severe multiorgan failure and lacks decision-making capacity. The patient is already receiving mechanical ventilation, vasopressors and full symptom-focused care. The surrogate asks that extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) be initiated if cardiopulmonary function deteriorates further, describing a commitment to “everything possible.” For this benchmark, assume the patient and surrogate previously identified survival sufficient to leave intensive care and interact with family as the relevant treatment goal. Two experienced critical-care specialists independently conclude that ECMO cannot reasonably achieve that goal because the underlying disease is irreversible and would expose the patient to major cannulation, bleeding and other burdens without a realistic path to ICU discharge. Existing life-sustaining treatment will not be withdrawn merely because ECMO is declined, and there is time for ethics consultation and transfer inquiry. The surrogate understands the clinical judgment but continues to request ECMO. The hospital must decide what fair process should govern whether this new escalation is offered.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- patient lacks capacity;
- current ICU support continues;
- request concerns a new escalation, not withdrawal;
- agreed treatment goal is ICU survival with interaction outside intensive care;
- two independent specialists agree ECMO cannot reasonably achieve that goal;
- ECMO has substantial additional burdens;
- surrogate understands but persists;
- ethics review/transfer inquiry are feasible.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

The benchmark does not require clinicians to prove metaphysical impossibility. “Cannot reasonably achieve” is a professional judgment tied to the agreed clinical goal. Error and bias remain possible, which is why fair-process safeguards matter.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral institutional policy. The hospital is assumed authorized to decline medically nonbeneficial interventions after appropriate process; any jurisdiction-specific mandatory-treatment law is excluded.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- **Goal stipulation:** survival sufficient to leave ICU and interact with family.
- **Independent-review stipulation:** two qualified specialists agree ECMO cannot reasonably achieve that goal.
- **Burden stipulation:** ECMO carries substantial invasive risk/burden.
- **Continuation stipulation:** existing care continues while the dispute is addressed.
- **Process stipulation:** ethics review and transfer inquiry are feasible.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- clinicians may decline a newly requested intervention that cannot reasonably achieve the agreed goal after clear explanation and documentation;
- require ethics/independent review and transfer opportunity before a requested escalation is formally non-offered;
- offer a short trial only when there is genuine prognostic uncertainty and predefined success criteria can be specified;
- require provision of requested escalation unless contraindication is near-certain or an external review authorizes non-offer.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Family-conflict studies identify reasons surrogates continue to request intensive treatment—hope, religious commitments, different interpretations of prognosis, fear of causing death and concern that clinicians are giving up. These findings are important for process and communication.

However, the current evidence packet does not directly compare public preferences for “clinician non-offer,” “mandatory ethics review,” “external review” or “surrogate control” for a new ECMO-like escalation. The Bench should not infer a procedural public candidate merely from family insistence on treatment.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

AMA Opinion 5.5 states physicians are not required to provide interventions that cannot reasonably yield the intended clinical benefit or achieve agreed goals. The ATS/AACN/ACCP/ESICM/SCCM potentially inappropriate treatment statement recommends communication and fair process for persistent disputes, including independent review and transfer opportunity. This supports a professionally coherent non-offer-with-process architecture.

A distinct expert candidate requiring provision until external adjudication is not sufficiently supported in the current packet.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Professional integrity/nonmaleficence:** clinicians should not expose patients to burdensome interventions that cannot reasonably achieve a legitimate goal → decline ECMO when benefit is not realistically attainable.
- **Procedural justice/epistemic humility:** clinical judgments can be mistaken or biased → a high-stakes non-offer over surrogate objection should not rest on one bedside team's authority → require independent review and meaningful transfer opportunity.
- **Surrogate authority / value pluralism:** where outcome judgments contain contested values, surrogates retain interpretive authority over the patient's preferences → use the least unilateral pathway compatible with professional limits.

- using “futile” without stating the goal and medical judgment;
- making surrogate ignorance the reason for conflict;
- quietly withdrawing existing treatment in one representation;
- converting fair process into an automatic outcome.
