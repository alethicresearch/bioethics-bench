# M031 — Medical aid in dying for terminal illness

**Case-family identity:** M031  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** end-of-life ethics / assisted dying  
**Subdomain:** medical aid in dying; terminal illness; clinician participation  
**Tags:** MAID, physician-assisted death, terminal illness, autonomy, conscience, safeguards  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** health system setting policy in a jurisdiction where medical aid in dying for qualifying terminally ill adults is legally permitted.  
**Decision question:** Should the health system permit clinicians to participate in medical aid in dying for capable terminally ill adults under defined safeguards, and what participation policy should it adopt?

The decision is institutional participation under legal permission. It does not ask whether one specific patient should receive a prescription and does not treat legality as an ethical conclusion.

## 2. Concise representation

A health system operates in a jurisdiction where capable adults with a terminal illness expected to cause death within six months may legally request self-administered medical aid in dying. For this benchmark, assume patients requesting it receive information about prognosis, hospice and palliative options, are screened for decision-making capacity and coercion, and may withdraw the request at any time. The system includes clinicians who support participation, clinicians who object on conscience grounds and patients who want access. The system must decide whether and how clinicians may participate while preserving palliative care, informed choice and conscientious nonparticipation.

## 3. Detailed representation

A large health system operates in a jurisdiction with a mature legal medical-aid-in-dying framework for capable adults whose terminal illness is expected to cause death within six months. The law permits a qualifying patient to obtain medication for voluntary self-administration after required assessments and procedural safeguards. For this benchmark, assume every requesting patient is informed about prognosis, hospice and specialist palliative care, is assessed for treatment-specific decision-making capacity and coercion, and may withdraw the request at any point. The system's physicians are divided: some view participation as compatible with respect for autonomy and relief of suffering, while others believe prescribing medication intended to enable death is incompatible with medicine or their conscience. Patients receiving care within the system reasonably expect accurate information and continuity even when an individual clinician objects. The system must decide whether to permit clinician participation under safeguards, prohibit institutional participation while maintaining ordinary end-of-life care, or permit participation while protecting individual conscience and ensuring access through another pathway.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- jurisdiction legally permits MAID for qualifying terminal illness;
- patient eligibility includes decision-making capacity and limited prognosis;
- self-administration, not clinician-administered euthanasia, is the represented practice;
- palliative/hospice care and prognosis information are available;
- individual clinicians hold sincere opposing views;
- patient requests are voluntary and revocable;
- institution has authority to decide its participation policy.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

The benchmark does not assume that MAID reliably improves or harms trust, palliative care or vulnerable populations. Those are contested empirical concerns. It also does not assume that a patient requesting MAID will ultimately use the prescription.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Named only as a legally permissive US-style jurisdiction, dated 2026. Oregon supplies the mature statutory reference architecture, but the case is not a test of Oregon law. If later executed, the scenario must explicitly stipulate legal permission so legal prohibition does not answer the ethical question.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- **Legality stipulation:** MAID is legally available under a terminal-illness safeguard regime.
- **Capacity/coercion stipulation:** qualifying requests receive capacity and voluntariness assessment.
- **Palliative-care stipulation:** hospice and specialist palliative options are meaningfully available and explained.
- **Conscience stipulation:** clinicians may have sincere participation objections.
- **Self-administration stipulation:** the patient, not the clinician, performs the final life-ending act.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- permit clinician participation under statutory and institutional safeguards;
- permit participation but protect individual clinician nonparticipation and guarantee an alternative access pathway;
- institutional nonparticipation: clinicians provide palliative/end-of-life care and accurate information but the system does not prescribe MAID;
- studied-neutrality model in which the institution supports lawful patient choice and clinician conscience without defining MAID as routine palliative practice.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

US public opinion is genuinely plural. In a 2024 national survey of 3,227 adults, 44% said they would definitely or probably consider MAID if terminally ill within six months (PMID 39992651). A Pew survey fielded in May 2025 found 34% of adults considered physician-assisted death morally acceptable, 29% did not view it as a moral issue and 35% viewed it as morally wrong. These data support both an access-oriented public position and an opposition/nonparticipation position without inventing polarization.

The studies concern personal/moral attitudes rather than exact institutional referral rules, so candidate translations must remain modest.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

AMA Opinion 5.7 opposes physician-assisted suicide as incompatible with the physician's role while explicitly acknowledging conscientious disagreement and that lawful physicians acting under conscience are not thereby in violation of the Code's broader conscience framework. ACP also opposes legalization. AAHPM takes studied neutrality on whether physician-assisted dying should be legally permitted but urges strong safeguards if it is legal and emphasizes high-quality palliative care and conscientious practice.

These are genuinely distinct professional stances: opposition to physician participation versus institutional/professional neutrality with safeguards. Oregon's statutory framework supplies a real access architecture but should be labeled policy/regulatory rather than professional consensus.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Autonomy / relief of suffering:** capable adults have strong authority over the manner and timing of dying when death is approaching → medicine should not force continued suffering or loss of control where safeguards are met → permit MAID access.
- **Professional role / nonmaleficence:** medicine's distinctive role is healing, palliation and nonabandonment rather than intentionally enabling death → normalization may create risks to trust and vulnerable patients → prohibit institutional clinician participation while intensifying palliative care.
- **Conscience and pluralism:** reasonable moral disagreement exists among clinicians and patients → coercing participation or blocking lawful access can each violate integrity → permit access through a structured pathway while protecting individual nonparticipation.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- treating legal permission as ethical endorsement;
- conflating self-administered MAID with euthanasia;
- presenting palliative care as absent or ineffective by assumption;
- presenting opposition as necessarily religious or access support as necessarily partisan;
- confusing personal willingness to use MAID with endorsement of a specific institutional workflow;
- collapsing institutional participation and individual conscience into one decision.
