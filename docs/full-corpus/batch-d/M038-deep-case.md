# M038 — Somatic support after brain death during pregnancy

**Case-family identity:** M038  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** death determination / reproductive ethics  
**Subdomain:** BD/DNC in pregnancy; somatic support; fetal interests; prior wishes  
**Tags:** brain death, pregnancy, somatic support, fetal viability, surrogate, maternal autonomy  
**Research status:** deep case draft; rare jurisdiction-sensitive case  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** hospital multidisciplinary team with the deceased pregnant patient's surrogate.  
**Decision question:** After a pregnant patient has been validly declared dead by neurologic criteria, what policy should govern continuation of somatic support to permit fetal development?

The represented decision occurs after valid BD/DNC determination. It concerns postmortem somatic support, not treatment of a living pregnant patient and not the validity of neurologic death criteria.

## 2. Concise representation

A pregnant adult at 18 weeks' gestation has been validly declared dead by neurologic criteria after catastrophic brain injury. The fetus currently has cardiac activity but is far from viability. For this benchmark, assume somatic support could potentially continue for weeks or months but success is uncertain and would require prolonged ICU-level interventions; the patient's prior wishes did not specifically address pregnancy after death. The surrogate believes the patient strongly valued continuing a wanted pregnancy but is uncertain whether she would have wanted prolonged postmortem bodily support. The hospital must decide what policy should govern whether support continues for fetal development.

## 3. Detailed representation

A pregnant adult at 18 weeks' gestation suffers catastrophic brain injury and is validly declared dead by neurologic criteria under accepted medical standards. The pregnancy was wanted and had been progressing normally. Fetal cardiac activity is present, but delivery at the current gestational age would not result in survival. For this benchmark, assume prolonged somatic support might permit fetal growth toward viability but requires mechanical ventilation, endocrine replacement, infection management, nutrition and other ICU interventions for an uncertain period, with meaningful risks of fetal loss, preterm birth and major neonatal morbidity. The patient had previously expressed enthusiasm about the pregnancy and general preferences against prolonged dependence on machines, but left no directive specifically addressing somatic support after death for fetal benefit. The appointed surrogate is uncertain how to reconcile those values. No organ-donation plan independently requires support. The hospital must decide whether to continue support based on fetal prospects and presumed maternal values, defer primarily to the surrogate's interpretation of the patient's wishes, or limit support when prognosis and burdens are disproportionate.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- valid BD/DNC; patient is legally/medically dead in represented jurisdiction;
- pregnancy at 18 weeks, previable at decision time;
- wanted pregnancy and fetus currently alive;
- prolonged somatic support may permit later viability but outcome is uncertain;
- support is medically/resource intensive;
- no explicit prior directive for this unusual circumstance;
- prior values point in more than one direction;
- surrogate must interpret those values;
- organ donation is excluded.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

The probability of maintaining pregnancy to a gestational age compatible with neonatal survival is uncertain and strongly dependent on gestation and maternal physiologic complications. The benchmark therefore does not promise fetal survival. Prior maternal wishes are also underdetermined, which is itself part of the ethical problem.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-sensitive, dated 2026. The benchmark assumes BD/DNC is legally recognized as death and that no pregnancy-specific statute mandates one outcome. Jurisdictions with compulsory pregnancy exemptions from advance-directive law require separate cases.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- **Death stipulation:** BD/DNC determination is valid and complete.
- **Gestation stipulation:** 18 weeks and not currently viable ex utero.
- **Pregnancy stipulation:** pregnancy was wanted and fetus currently has cardiac activity.
- **Outcome stipulation:** prolonged support might reach viability but success and neonatal outcome are uncertain.
- **Prior-wishes stipulation:** no explicit directive resolves postmortem pregnancy support.
- **Donation stipulation:** organ donation is not driving the decision.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- continue somatic support when fetal survival is plausibly achievable and this is consistent with the deceased person's known reproductive values;
- defer primarily to the surrogate's reconstruction of the deceased patient's wishes, including a decision to discontinue;
- use gestational/prognostic thresholds and periodic reassessment to determine whether continued support remains proportionate;
- do not initiate prolonged somatic support without affirmative prior or surrogate authorization because the patient is dead and bodily integrity remains morally relevant.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

The event is rare and direct public or bereaved-family policy evidence is sparse. Case reports and ethical analyses document intense family, religious and cultural considerations but cannot substitute for systematic affected-public preference evidence. The Bench should not manufacture a public candidate pool from general abortion, maternal-fetal or brain-death attitudes.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

The 2025 AAN position statement explicitly addresses ethical considerations in BD/DNC determination in a pregnant person and is intended to complement the 2023 consensus guideline (PMID 41187308). Reviews and guidance emphasize clarifying valid death determination, gestational age, fetal prognosis, maternal prior wishes, surrogate authority and proportionality. Ethical literature remains plural: some analyses argue continuation may be justified or even strongly favored when fetal survival is plausible, while others emphasize that pregnancy should not erase the deceased patient's autonomy/dignity and that prolonged support is not obligatory (PMID 31127869; 33419499; 42147397).

These are not cleanly separable contemporary professional policy candidates.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Presumed reproductive intent / beneficence toward fetus:** a wanted pregnancy creates morally relevant interests and prior maternal commitments → if support can plausibly produce a surviving child, continuing may honor rather than violate the deceased person's values → continue while proportionate.
- **Precedent autonomy / bodily integrity:** death does not erase the moral authority of prior bodily and reproductive values → uncertain fetal benefit cannot automatically override a deceased person's likely refusal of prolonged machine support → give surrogate interpretation of prior wishes priority.
- **Proportionality / justice:** potential fetal benefit must be weighed against duration, invasiveness, neonatal prognosis and intensive resource use → use explicit gestational/prognostic thresholds and reassessment rather than an absolute rule.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- referring to the deceased patient as merely an incubator or fetal environment;
- treating fetal cardiac activity as equivalent to likely neonatal survival;
- importing abortion politics rather than the actual postmortem support decision;
- overlooking the deceased person's prior wishes;
- implying the surrogate owns the decision independent of patient values;
- treating rare successful case reports as probability estimates;
- making legal pregnancy restrictions the hidden answer.
