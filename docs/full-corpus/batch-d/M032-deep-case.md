# M032 — Assisted dying for severe, enduring psychiatric illness

**Case-family identity:** M032  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** psychiatry / assisted dying  
**Subdomain:** psychiatric euthanasia; irremediability; capacity; nonterminal suffering  
**Tags:** psychiatric illness, euthanasia, assisted suicide, capacity, irremediability, treatment refusal  
**Research status:** deep case draft; dated jurisdiction-specific case  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** specialist assisted-dying assessment service in the Netherlands.  
**Decision question:** Under what policy, if any, should euthanasia or assisted suicide be available when a capable adult's unbearable suffering arises solely from severe, enduring psychiatric illness rather than terminal somatic disease?

The case is not “is depression treatable?” and does not assume that psychiatric diagnosis makes a request incompetent. It isolates the difficult policy threshold around voluntariness, decision-making capacity, irremediability and reasonable alternatives.

## 2. Concise representation

A 52-year-old adult in the Netherlands has severe recurrent psychiatric illness with decades of specialist treatment, repeated hospitalizations and persistent suffering that the patient describes as unbearable. The patient requests euthanasia and demonstrates understanding of the request and its consequences. For this benchmark, assume multiple evidence-based treatments have failed or produced intolerable burdens, but some clinicians believe further therapeutic possibilities cannot be ruled out with the confidence normally possible in terminal somatic disease. There is no terminal physical illness and no acute intoxication or psychotic episode driving the request. The assessment service must decide what policy should govern eligibility for assisted dying in psychiatric illness alone.

## 3. Detailed representation

A 52-year-old adult in the Netherlands has lived for decades with severe recurrent depression, trauma-related symptoms and personality-related difficulties. The patient has undergone prolonged psychotherapy, multiple medication classes, augmentation strategies, structured rehabilitation and several psychiatric hospitalizations. For this benchmark, assume the request for euthanasia has persisted over time outside acute crises; the patient can understand relevant information, compare alternatives and explain the consequences of death; and no intoxication, delirium or acute psychotic episode is determining the request. Several experienced clinicians agree that suffering is profound and chronic, but they differ about whether “no reasonable alternative” and lack of prospect of improvement can be established with sufficient confidence because psychiatric outcomes remain uncertain and additional treatment approaches exist, though burdensome and of uncertain benefit. The patient has no terminal somatic illness. The service must decide whether psychiatric illness alone can qualify, whether an exceptional heightened-review pathway should apply, or whether such requests should be excluded.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- psychiatric illness is the sole underlying basis for the assisted-dying request;
- request is persistent rather than confined to an acute crisis;
- patient is stipulated capable for the decision at the represented time;
- extensive treatment has occurred;
- some possible interventions remain, but benefit is uncertain and burdens may be substantial;
- clinicians disagree about whether irremediability/no reasonable alternative can be established;
- no terminal somatic illness;
- Dutch law/policy context permits psychiatric cases in principle under due-care criteria.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

The central uncertainty is prognostic and conceptual: psychiatric “irremediability” is harder to establish than imminent terminal decline. The benchmark does not stipulate that all conceivable treatments have been exhausted or that any remaining intervention is reasonable for this particular patient. It also does not equate suicidal ideation with incompetence.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Explicitly Netherlands, 2026. The RTE's latest EuthanasiaCode was published January 1, 2026. The case should not be generalized to jurisdictions that prohibit nonterminal or psychiatric assisted dying.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- **Capacity stipulation:** the patient demonstrates treatment-specific decisional competence outside acute crisis.
- **Persistence stipulation:** the request is sustained over time.
- **Treatment-history stipulation:** extensive evidence-based psychiatric care has been attempted.
- **Irremediability uncertainty:** reasonable experts disagree about whether further treatment possibilities defeat the “no reasonable alternative” criterion.
- **No-terminal-illness stipulation:** no terminal somatic disease independently establishes end-of-life eligibility.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- psychiatric illness may qualify under the same statutory due-care criteria, with additional specialist psychiatric review;
- allow only through an exceptional pathway requiring prolonged assessment, multiple independent psychiatric opinions and stringent demonstration of irremediability;
- categorically exclude psychiatric-illness-only requests because reliable irremediability and voluntariness cannot be established with adequate confidence;
- defer when plausible treatment or social-resource deficits remain, without assuming every theoretical option must be attempted.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Public support for assisted death is consistently lower for nonterminal and mental-illness scenarios than for terminal physical illness. US survey evidence found only minority support for nonterminal PAD and particularly low support for mental-illness cases when unmet social or health resources were part of the scenario. Dutch stakeholder surveys likewise found substantially less acceptance for chronic depression/mental suffering than for the general euthanasia framework.

This is relevant pluralism, but the public evidence is highly sensitive to scenario wording and country. It should not be used to create a generic international public pool.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

Dutch law and the RTE due-care framework permit psychiatric EAS in principle but demand careful assessment of voluntariness, capacity, unbearable suffering, absence of prospect of improvement and reasonable alternatives. Case reviews from 2017–2022 show patients often have multiple disorders and long treatment histories and that physician judgment remains substantial (PMID 39529899). Earlier reviews found consultant disagreement and occasional lack of independent psychiatric input (PMID 26864709).

The professional object is therefore not a settled yes/no recommendation. It is a highly guarded exceptional practice embedded in one legal system.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Equal respect / non-discrimination:** unbearable suffering can arise from psychiatric as well as somatic disease → categorical exclusion may devalue psychiatric suffering and capable agency → permit eligibility under equally rigorous criteria.
- **Epistemic precaution:** psychiatric prognosis, treatment responsiveness and the relationship between illness and death wishes can remain deeply uncertain → irreversible death demands a high confidence threshold → exclude or impose exceptional heightened review.
- **Justice / anti-forced-irremediability:** poverty, isolation or lack of treatment access can make suffering appear irremediable when social remedies are missing → assisted dying should not substitute for reasonable care/resources → defer eligibility until remediable deprivation has been addressed.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- implying psychiatric diagnosis means incapacity;
- treating “treatment resistant” as a binary empirical fact;
- building a scenario whose poverty or lack of access quietly creates irremediability;
- treating legal eligibility as professional consensus;
- using terminal-MAID public attitudes as psychiatric-MAID attitudes;
- stigmatizing suicidality or psychiatric disability;
- freezing rapidly evolving practice as a universal policy object.
