# M036 — Withholding versus withdrawing the same life-sustaining treatment

**Case-family identity:** M036  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** end-of-life ethics / treatment limitation  
**Subdomain:** withholding; withdrawing; time-limited trials; status-quo bias  
**Tags:** withholding, withdrawing, life support, ventilator, ethical equivalence, treatment trial  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** hospital/ICU service setting a treatment-limitation policy.  
**Decision question:** Should the hospital treat stopping an already-started life-sustaining treatment as ethically equivalent to not starting the same treatment when prognosis, patient goals and expected burdens/benefits are otherwise identical?

This case isolates the withholding/withdrawing distinction. It is not a general dispute about whether a treatment is beneficial: the underlying clinical assessment is held constant.

## 2. Concise representation

Two otherwise equivalent ICU decisions concern the same invasive life-sustaining treatment and the same patient-centered goal. In one, treatment has not yet been started; in the other, it was started as a short trial and has produced no improvement. For this benchmark, assume the patient's prognosis, values and expected future burdens/benefits are now identical in the two situations, and the surrogate agrees that treatment should not continue if it cannot achieve the stated goal but feels that actively stopping an existing treatment is morally harder than not starting it. The hospital must decide whether policy should treat withholding and withdrawing as ethically equivalent or require a stronger process before withdrawal.

## 3. Detailed representation

An ICU uses time-limited trials of invasive organ support when prognosis is initially uncertain. The hospital is reviewing whether its policy should distinguish between two cases that, at the moment of decision, have the same prognosis, patient values, clinical goal and expected future treatment burden. In Case A, the relevant organ-support intervention has not yet been initiated and clinicians recommend not starting it because it will not achieve the patient's agreed goal. In Case B, the intervention was begun for a short trial while prognosis was uncertain; the trial has now failed, and clinicians have the same evidence that further treatment will not achieve the same goal. For this benchmark, assume stopping the intervention in Case B does not create a new physiological harm beyond allowing the underlying illness to cause death, and adequate symptom control is available. The surrogate agrees with the treatment goal and prognosis but says it feels morally acceptable not to start treatment and morally wrong to “actively stop” something already keeping the patient alive. The hospital must decide whether its policy should treat withholding and withdrawal as equivalent or require additional safeguards for withdrawal.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- same intervention and same patient-centered goal;
- prognosis and expected future benefits/burdens identical at decision time;
- withdrawal does not introduce a distinct harmful mechanism beyond stopping ineffective support;
- symptoms can be treated;
- treatment in withdrawal case was started under uncertainty as a trial;
- surrogate experiences a moral distinction even while accepting prognosis;
- decision concerns policy/process rather than a single bedside persuasion strategy.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

The benchmark intentionally removes clinical differences so the ethical distinction can be tested. It does not assume that every real-world withholding/withdrawal pair is equivalent; treatment history, dependence, promises or new harms can create differences in other cases.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Jurisdiction-neutral clinical ethics. Both withholding and withdrawing are legally permitted in the represented setting when appropriately authorized. The policy question is whether withdrawal should carry a higher procedural threshold.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- **Clinical-equivalence stipulation:** prognosis, goals and future burden/benefit are identical at the decision point.
- **Trial stipulation:** treatment was started because of earlier uncertainty, not because a commitment to indefinite treatment was made.
- **No-new-harm stipulation:** withdrawal does not create a separate injury beyond cessation of support.
- **Palliation stipulation:** symptoms after withdrawal can be appropriately managed.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- treat withholding and withdrawing as ethically equivalent when the relevant clinical and patient-value facts are identical;
- recognize equivalence in ultimate permissibility but require additional communication/review before withdrawal because stopping treatment has distinctive psychological and relational consequences;
- treat withdrawal as morally more demanding and require a stronger justification than non-initiation;
- design explicit time-limited-trial consent so withdrawal criteria are agreed before treatment begins, reducing status-quo bias later.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Public intuitions often distinguish withholding from withdrawal. A preregistered study of 1,067 participants found greater support for rationing framed as withholding than withdrawing and different attitudes depending on bedside versus policy context (PMID 38912645). Swedish public/physician survey work found substantial differences in reasoning about treatment limitation (PMID 18279501). A 2024 Japanese study likewise examined a persistent physician-citizen gap around the distinction (PMID 38588396).

These are directly relevant to whether an institution should recognize a stronger process for withdrawal. Candidate audit should distinguish public discomfort with withdrawal from a considered endorsement of a categorical ethical difference.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

AMA ethics states that there is no ethical difference between withholding and withdrawing a medical intervention when a patient or surrogate may appropriately decline it, and withdrawal is ethically appropriate when treatment no longer achieves patient goals. This equivalence view is common in professional end-of-life guidance and supports time-limited trials precisely because ineffective treatment can later be stopped.

Normative scholarship is not unanimous: some contemporary analysis argues withholding and withdrawing are not always ethically equivalent and cautions against simply educating away contrary intuitions (PMID 30896350). That dissent belongs primarily in the framework/normative pool unless an independent professional policy adopting it is found.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Consequential/causal equivalence:** if patient goals, prognosis and consequences are the same, moral evaluation should not depend on whether treatment happens to have been started → treat withholding and withdrawal equivalently.
- **Doing/allowing and reliance:** stopping established support can alter agency, expectations and relational responsibility even when outcomes match → withdrawal may warrant a stronger justificatory/process threshold.
- **Procedural commitment / time-limited trials:** the moral problem is partly created by status-quo bias after initiation → explicitly agree at initiation what outcomes trigger stopping → make trial withdrawal an anticipated component of the original plan.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- allowing the withdrawal case to have worse prognosis than the withholding case;
- treating emotional difficulty as proof of ethical nonequivalence;
- treating professional equivalence as universal philosophical consensus;
- making withdrawal causally different through ventilator-related distress or another unstated harm;
- conflating bedside permissibility with reimbursement/rationing policy evidence;
- failing to state that treatment was initially a trial under uncertainty.
