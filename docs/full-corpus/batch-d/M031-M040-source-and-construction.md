Batch D contains several legally and politically sensitive end-of-life decisions. The construction rule is therefore stricter than usual: named jurisdiction and date are required whenever law or current professional policy materially defines the decision space. Legal permission is never treated as ethical endorsement, and professional opposition is never presented as a statement of empirical fact. Cases whose policy object is actively moving remain research objects rather than frozen executable records.

## M031 — medical aid in dying for terminal illness

**Decision:** should a health system permit clinician participation in medical aid in dying (MAID) for a capable adult with terminal illness under safeguards?

**Public evidence.** A 2025 national US survey of 3,227 adults found 44% would definitely/probably consider MAID if diagnosed with a terminal illness expected to cause death within six months (PMID 39992651). Pew's spring-2025 survey of 8,937 US adults found 34% called physician-assisted death morally acceptable, 29% said it was not a moral issue and 35% said it was morally wrong, providing genuine public pluralism. Oregon's 2025 report documents an established safeguard-based statutory practice rather than public preference.

**Professional/policy evidence.** AMA Opinion 5.7 opposes physician-assisted suicide while explicitly acknowledging morally conscientious disagreement and lawful conscientious participation; ACP likewise opposes legalization. AAHPM takes studied neutrality on legal permission while insisting that any legal regime include safeguards such as decisional capacity and limited life expectancy. Oregon supplies a concrete safeguard-based access architecture.

**Initial judgment:** candidate audit warranted. Public evidence is genuinely plural and professional/policy architectures are distinct, but wording must separate “permit under safeguards,” “professional neutrality/conscience” and “prohibit physician participation.”

## M032 — assisted dying for treatment-resistant psychiatric illness

**Decision:** should eligibility for euthanasia/assisted suicide extend to a capable adult whose suffering arises solely from severe, enduring psychiatric illness and who is not terminally ill?

**Current jurisdictional anchor.** The Netherlands permits euthanasia/assisted suicide without a terminal-illness requirement when statutory due-care criteria are met. The latest RTE EuthanasiaCode was published 1 January 2026. Psychiatric cases require exceptional caution around voluntariness, decisional competence, irremediability/no reasonable alternative and independent assessment. Review studies of Dutch psychiatric EAS show complex multimorbidity, extensive physician judgment and occasional consultant disagreement (PMID 39529899; PMID 26864709).

**Public evidence.** Public support falls substantially when assisted death concerns nonterminal conditions or mental illness. A US survey found 36.9% baseline support for physician-assisted death for nonterminal conditions, with substantially lower support for mental-illness scenarios under resource limitation. Older Dutch stakeholder work likewise found mental-suffering scenarios much less accepted than the general euthanasia framework.

**Initial judgment:** `research-complete-not-executable` at present. This is a moving, jurisdiction-specific clinical/legal practice with difficult construct dependence on “irremediability.” Do not freeze a generic international candidate set.

## M033 — advance request for assisted dying after loss of capacity in dementia

**Decision:** when a person made a competent advance euthanasia directive for advanced dementia but can no longer communicate a current request, what policy should govern whether assisted death may proceed?

**Dutch policy/legal anchor.** Current RTE guidance states that an advance directive may replace an oral request when a patient can no longer express their wishes, provided the directive was made competently and all other due-care criteria are met. Dutch case law and review practice nevertheless show exceptional difficulty assessing applicability, current suffering, voluntariness and implementation.

**Public/professional evidence.** In a Dutch national survey, 60% of the general public agreed that advanced-dementia euthanasia should be possible on a prior request, while physicians were markedly more reluctant (PMID 32652560). A qualitative study found public respondents emphasized degradation/loss whereas physicians emphasized the need for current communication and difficulty verifying suffering (PMID 25630339). In a GP vignette survey, 42.2% disagreed that an advance directive can replace an oral request when communication is impossible (PMID 34789166).

**Initial judgment:** candidate audit warranted but jurisdiction must remain explicitly Dutch/2026. This is a rare case where public and professional policy disagreement is directly documented.

## M034 — clinically assisted nutrition/hydration in prolonged disorder of consciousness

**Decision:** when a patient with a prolonged disorder of consciousness has no reliable prior treatment instruction, what policy should govern continuation or withdrawal of clinically assisted nutrition/hydration (CANH)?

**Affected evidence.** Families of people in vegetative/minimally conscious states report diverse views about what counts as proper medical treatment and place major weight on the patient's understood wishes (PMID 26539603). Interview work shows even families who believe their relative would prefer death can experience withdrawal of nutrition/hydration as morally distinct because of fear of “starvation/dehydration” (PMID 24425753).

**Professional evidence.** The AAN/ACRM/NIDILRR Disorders of Consciousness guideline, reaffirmed 2024, emphasizes diagnostic accuracy, serial standardized assessment and acknowledging that prognosis is not universally poor. ASPEN treats artificial nutrition/hydration as a clinical intervention requiring autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, justice and shared interdisciplinary deliberation (PMID 33616284). This supports careful individual process more strongly than two professional endpoints.

**Initial judgment:** potential asymmetric case; affected-family evidence is genuinely plural, professional guidance broadly convergent. Candidate audit required.

## M035 — voluntary stopping of eating and drinking

**Decision:** how should clinicians respond when a capable adult with serious illness voluntarily stops oral food and fluids with the purpose of hastening death?

**Affected evidence.** VSED patient and caregiver studies show diverse motives and high caregiving burden. A Dutch qualitative study of 29 patients identified distinct groups ranging from older people seeking control to care-dependent people and younger psychiatric patients with fluctuating wishes (PMID 38012037). US caregivers described support from hospice as legitimizing and stabilizing the process but also reported heavy responsibility and uncertainty (PMID 32644873). A 2025 mixed-methods review found most caregivers/professionals accepted the patient's decision but emphasized distress and support needs (PMID 40508879).

**Professional evidence.** Published US clinical guidelines recommend assessment of capacity, coercion, untreated psychiatric/symptom burden, anticipatory guidance, symptom treatment and emotional/caregiver support (PMID 37352942). The clinical literature generally distinguishes respecting VSED from administering a lethal intervention.

**Initial judgment:** strong research case but likely `needs-additional-evidence` for multiple institutional policies; affected evidence supports accompaniment more directly than a pro/anti-accompaniment policy split.

## M036 — withholding versus withdrawing the same treatment

**Decision:** should hospital policy treat stopping an already-started life-sustaining intervention as ethically different from not starting the same intervention when prognosis, patient goals and burdens are otherwise identical?

**Public evidence.** A preregistered study of 1,067 participants found greater support for rationing when framed as withholding rather than withdrawing and different judgments at bedside versus policy level (PMID 38912645). Comparative survey work has likewise found public and physician reasoning differs, although both groups may accept withdrawal in severe cases (PMID 18279501; PMID 38588396).

**Professional/normative evidence.** AMA ethics states there is no ethical difference between withholding and withdrawing treatment and that either may be appropriate when an intervention no longer achieves the patient's goals. However, contemporary normative scholarship disputes universal equivalence and argues that morally relevant differences can exist (PMID 30896350). This makes M036 an unusually good case where public intuition and canonical professional ethics may diverge.

**Initial judgment:** candidate audit warranted, likely asymmetric rather than symmetric.

## M037 — unilateral limitation of a newly requested nonbeneficial escalation

**Decision:** when a surrogate requests a new escalation of intensive treatment that clinicians judge cannot reasonably achieve the agreed patient goal, what fair process should govern non-offer of that escalation?

**Construction distinction.** M037 is not M009 (CPR-specific) or M026 (withdrawal of already ongoing life support). It concerns **withholding a new escalation**, such as ECMO, while existing ICU support continues. This preserves a distinct procedural question without creating another generic futility case.

**Professional evidence.** AMA Opinion 5.5 states clinicians need not provide interventions that cannot reasonably achieve intended clinical benefit or agreed goals. The ATS multi-society potentially inappropriate treatment framework recommends fair-process safeguards for persistent disagreement.

**Affected evidence.** Current family-conflict literature explains reasons for objection and mistrust but remains weak on direct public endorsement of competing non-offer procedures.

**Initial judgment:** `needs-additional-evidence`; retain the differentiated withholding/escalation case without forcing execution.

## M038 — somatic support after BD/DNC during pregnancy

**Decision:** after a pregnant patient has been validly declared dead by neurologic criteria, what policy should govern continuation of somatic support for fetal development?

**Professional/ethical evidence.** A 2025 AAN position statement specifically addresses pregnancy after BD/DNC alongside objections and public trust (PMID 41187308). Reviews and case analyses stress maternal prior wishes, gestational age, fetal prognosis, treatment burden, resource use and surrogate role; ethical scholarship ranges from support as permissible/conditionally justified to strong objections when it conflicts with maternal autonomy (PMID 31127869; PMID 33419499; PMID 42147397; PMID 41982188).

**Initial judgment:** `research-complete-not-executable`. It is rare, technically fact-sensitive, legally variable and insufficiently supported by direct affected-public policy evidence.

## M039 — organ donation after medical assistance in dying

**Decision:** when a competent person has independently chosen and qualified for MAID and also wishes to donate organs after death, what policy should govern whether and how donation is offered?

**Professional/policy evidence.** Canadian Blood Services' 2019 and updated 2023 guidance supports offering donation to medically suitable competent patients who have independently chosen MAID, with safeguards separating the MAID decision from donation, first-person consent, withdrawal rights, dead-donor-rule compliance, minimization of donor burden, professional conscience provisions and reporting. The 2023 update was developed with 63 multidisciplinary experts plus patient/family participants (PMID 37364914). A 2026 Transplantation Society Ethics Committee analysis across six jurisdictions likewise emphasizes voluntariness, information timing, death determination, premortem interventions, professional roles and trust (PMID 42418265).

**Empirical professional evidence.** Québec organ-donation professionals were broadly favorable to donation after MAID based on donor autonomy but divided on directed donation, living donation before MAID and “death by donation” (PMID 33663501).

**Public evidence.** Direct MAID-donation-specific public policy data remain thinner than the mature professional layer; scoping review literature identifies public perceptions but is dominated by ethics/policy publications (PMID 37477350).

**Initial judgment:** research-complete and possibly executable only if direct public/affected-policy evidence is strengthened. Do not turn general support for organ donation or MAID into support for their combination.

## M040 — family objection to brain-death testing

**Decision:** when prerequisites for BD/DNC evaluation are met but family members object to examination/apnea testing, what process should govern whether clinicians proceed?

**Professional evidence.** The 2023 BD/DNC consensus guideline and 2025 AAN position statement distinguish communication/accommodation from abandonment of accepted death-determination standards. Ethical literature specifically debates parental refusal of brain-death testing: one position argues clinicians have moral authority/professional responsibility to determine death; another emphasizes risks of apnea testing and the moral relevance of family objection (PMID 32220905).

**Affected evidence.** Direct family/public evidence about consent to the testing procedure itself remains sparse. Public misunderstanding of BD/DNC and religious objections are relevant background but cannot be converted into a testing-policy pool without stronger evidence.

**Initial judgment:** `needs-additional-evidence`; preserve as distinct from M028, which begins after BD/DNC has already been determined.
