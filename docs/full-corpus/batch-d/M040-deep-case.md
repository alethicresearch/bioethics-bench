# M040 — Family objection to brain-death testing

**Case-family identity:** M040  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** death determination / clinical ethics  
**Subdomain:** BD/DNC testing; apnea test; parental/surrogate objection  
**Tags:** brain death, apnea test, consent, family objection, death determination, accommodation  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** ICU/hospital service before formal BD/DNC determination.  
**Decision question:** When prerequisites for brain-death/death-by-neurologic-criteria evaluation are met but the family objects to examination or apnea testing, what process should govern whether clinicians proceed?

This is distinct from M028. Here death by neurologic criteria has **not yet been formally determined** because the family objects to testing. The question is whether testing requires family permission, warrants temporary accommodation or should proceed as a professional duty to determine death.

## 2. Concise representation

A child has catastrophic irreversible brain injury, no observed brain-mediated responses and no plausible confounder, and clinicians believe formal testing for death by neurologic criteria is indicated. The parents object to the neurologic examination and apnea test because they believe testing itself may hasten death and their religious tradition recognizes death only when the heart permanently stops. For this benchmark, assume the team has explained the purpose and risks of testing, no organ-donation decision is pending, and a short delay for consultation is medically feasible. The hospital must decide what process should govern whether formal BD/DNC testing proceeds over parental objection.

## 3. Detailed representation

A child in the pediatric ICU has catastrophic irreversible brain injury after a prolonged period without cerebral perfusion. The known cause of injury has been established, temperature and blood pressure are adequate, sedating/paralyzing drugs and major metabolic confounders have been excluded, and repeated bedside examinations show no brain-mediated responses. The treating team believes the child is likely to meet criteria for death by neurologic criteria and proposes the formal neurologic examination and apnea testing required by current standards. The parents object. They fear the apnea test could itself cause cardiopulmonary deterioration and explain that their religious understanding recognizes death only after irreversible circulatory arrest. For this benchmark, assume clinicians have explained that the purpose of testing is to determine whether the child is already dead by neurologic criteria rather than to decide whether life support should be withdrawn; no organ-donation decision is pending; and a short period for ethics/spiritual consultation is feasible without immediate harm to another patient. The hospital must decide whether parental consent is required, whether testing should be delayed for accommodation or alternative testing where medically appropriate, or whether clinicians should proceed despite objection as part of their duty to determine death.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- pediatric patient, parents are surrogates while child is presumed alive until determination;
- clinical prerequisites for BD/DNC evaluation are met;
- family objects to the testing itself;
- objection includes concern about apnea-test risk and a different concept of death;
- clinicians explain testing is death determination, not treatment withdrawal;
- organ donation is excluded;
- short consultation delay is feasible;
- no local law is stipulated to require parental consent or prohibit testing.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Formal BD/DNC status remains unknown until accepted evaluation is completed. The benchmark does not stipulate that the patient “is brain dead” before testing. Risks of apnea testing are not represented as zero, which preserves the ethically relevant objection while not assuming the procedure is unacceptably dangerous.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

US-style medical-standard context dated 2026. The benchmark assumes local law does not independently settle whether surrogate consent is required for BD/DNC testing. Current professional guidance informs the case, but a jurisdiction with a specific consent statute would need a separate version.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- **Prerequisite stipulation:** accepted prerequisites for BD/DNC testing have been satisfied.
- **No-prior-determination stipulation:** death has not yet been formally determined.
- **Risk stipulation:** apnea testing has recognized clinical risks but is considered medically appropriate by the team.
- **No-donation stipulation:** organ procurement does not motivate testing.
- **Accommodation stipulation:** a short delay for consultation is feasible.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- proceed with medically indicated BD/DNC testing after explanation because determining whether death has occurred is a professional responsibility rather than an elective treatment requiring surrogate authorization;
- provide a short, defined accommodation period with ethics/spiritual support and use accepted ancillary testing when clinically appropriate before proceeding over objection;
- require surrogate consent for apnea testing because it carries physiological risk, while permitting noninvasive/ancillary alternatives where accepted;
- seek external/legal review when objection persists rather than proceeding unilaterally.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Direct empirical evidence from families specifically about consent to BD/DNC testing is limited. Broader studies show misunderstanding of brain death and recurrent religious/moral objection, but those findings do not establish preferences about whether clinicians may perform an apnea test without consent. The Bench should not infer a public procedural rule from general rejection of neurologic criteria.

Case-based ethics literature documents the seriousness of parental objections and concern about testing risk but is normative rather than public-preference evidence.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

The 2023 pediatric/adult BD/DNC consensus guideline establishes standardized medical testing requirements; the 2025 AAN position statement addresses communication and surrogate objections in order to promote consistent practice and public trust (PMID 41187308). Ethical debate remains over consent to apnea testing. A pediatric ethics analysis explicitly presents one view that physicians have both moral authority and professional responsibility to determine death, alongside the opposing view that apnea-testing risk can make family refusal morally relevant (PMID 32220905).

The professional standard strongly favors the legitimacy of death determination but does not eliminate the need for careful communication and reasonable accommodation.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Professional/public-standard duty:** whether a patient is dead is a factual/legal status clinicians have responsibility to determine → surrogates do not ordinarily have authority to veto a medically valid death determination → proceed after adequate explanation and reasonable accommodation.
- **Bodily integrity / surrogate protection:** before death is established, the patient remains presumptively living and apnea testing imposes physiological risk → parents retain authority to protect the child from a contested procedure → require consent or an accepted lower-burden alternative.
- **Procedural pluralism/public trust:** forceful testing in the face of sincere religious objection can damage trust even where clinicians ultimately may proceed → use a defined accommodation, ethics/spiritual consultation and transparent escalation process.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- calling the child brain dead before testing is completed;
- implying testing is simply another treatment option;
- treating parents' religious view as ignorance;
- hiding real apnea-test risks;
- adding organ donation and contaminating motivation;
- collapsing this case into M028 post-declaration accommodation;
- making local law the unstated answer.
