# M039 — Organ donation after medical assistance in dying

**Case-family identity:** M039  
**Featured crosswalk:** none  
**Primary domain:** transplantation / end-of-life ethics  
**Subdomain:** organ donation after MAID; consent; decision separation; dead donor rule  
**Tags:** MAID, organ donation, transplantation, consent, dead donor rule, autonomy  
**Research status:** deep case draft  
**Evidence date:** 2026-08-27

## 1. Decision architecture

**Decision-maker:** organ-donation organization and health system in a jurisdiction where both MAID and postmortem organ donation after MAID are legally permitted.  
**Decision question:** When a competent patient has independently chosen and qualified for MAID and also wishes to donate organs after death, what policy should govern whether and how donation is offered?

The case begins **after** the MAID eligibility decision is independently established. It does not ask whether organ donation should influence MAID eligibility or whether organs may be removed before death.

## 2. Concise representation

A competent adult with grievous illness has independently requested and been found eligible for legal medical assistance in dying. After that decision, the patient asks whether organs can be donated after death and strongly wants to donate if medically suitable. For this benchmark, assume donation requires hospital-based MAID and pre-donation testing that may modestly burden the patient's final days, the patient may withdraw either decision at any time, and organs will be recovered only after death is determined under accepted criteria. The health system must decide what policy should govern offering and coordinating donation after MAID without allowing donation interests to influence the assisted-dying decision.

## 3. Detailed representation

A competent adult with grievous and irremediable illness has undergone the legally required medical-assistance-in-dying assessments and has been found eligible. The MAID decision was made before any organ-donation discussion and is documented as voluntary and independent of transplantation needs. The patient subsequently asks whether they can donate organs and describes donation as an important final act of altruism. For this benchmark, assume the patient is medically suitable for potential donation; successful donation would require additional testing, coordination and hospital-based MAID rather than the patient's preferred home setting; and these logistical changes create modest but real burdens. The patient may withdraw consent for MAID or donation at any time. Organ recovery will occur only after death has been determined under accepted criteria, and transplant clinicians will not participate in evaluating MAID eligibility. The system must decide whether eligible MAID patients should routinely be offered the opportunity to donate, whether donation should be discussed only when the patient raises it, and what safeguards are required to keep the two decisions independent.

## 4. Decision-critical facts

- MAID eligibility and choice precede donation discussion;
- patient is competent and provides first-person consent;
- medically suitable for potential organ donation;
- donation changes location/logistics and adds some burden;
- either consent remains revocable;
- donor decision cannot affect MAID eligibility;
- organ recovery occurs only after death determination;
- transplant/MAID roles are separated.

## 5. Explicit uncertainty

Donation may not ultimately proceed because of organ suitability or peri-death circumstances. The benchmark does not assume that offering donation pressures all patients or that not offering it protects autonomy. The ethical question concerns policy safeguards around opportunity, information and separation.

## 6. Jurisdiction and time strategy

Canada-style policy context dated 2026, where MAID and organ donation after MAID are established practices. The legal permissibility of both is stipulated so the ethical object is donation policy, not MAID legalization.

## 7. Benchmark stipulations

- **Independent-MAID stipulation:** MAID decision/eligibility was established before donation discussion.
- **Capacity/consent stipulation:** patient provides first-person consent and may withdraw either decision.
- **Dead-donor-rule stipulation:** organs are recovered only after accepted death determination.
- **Separation stipulation:** transplant teams do not decide MAID eligibility.
- **Burden stipulation:** donation requires additional testing/location changes with modest but real burden.

## 8. Serious policy / position families

- proactively offer organ-donation information to medically suitable patients after MAID eligibility is established, with strict separation safeguards;
- discuss donation only when the patient independently raises interest, to minimize the risk of perceived pressure;
- permit donation but require an independent consent process, clear opt-out, minimized testing burden and separation of clinical teams;
- prohibit or severely restrict donation after MAID because combining the practices creates unacceptable risks to voluntariness or public trust.

## 9. Public / affected-community evidence

Direct population-level evidence specifically comparing **how donation after MAID should be offered** remains limited. Canadian guidance development has included patients who requested MAID and family members of MAID donors, providing important affected-participant input but not a representative public preference distribution. Broader donation surveys cannot substitute for views about the ethically distinctive combination of MAID and donation.

A 2023 scoping review identifies public perceptions as a recurring theme in the literature but also shows the evidence base is dominated by ethical/legal/policy discussion (PMID 37477350). This is not yet enough to fabricate two public candidates.

## 10. Expert / professional recommendations

Canadian Blood Services guidance is mature and explicit. The 2019 guidance recommends that medically suitable, conscious and competent patients who have independently chosen MAID should not be prohibited from donation and should have the opportunity to donate; the MAID decision must precede organ-donation discussion; first-person consent and withdrawal rights are central; donor burden should be minimized; death must be determined before recovery; and conscientious clinicians should still support the patient's donation wishes through appropriate pathways. The 2023 update adds guidance on referral, consent after loss of capacity in some Track 1 cases, directed/conditional donation, procedural issues, clinician support and reporting (PMID 37364914).

A 2026 Transplantation Society Ethics Committee review across six jurisdictions similarly identifies voluntariness, information timing, dead-donor-rule compliance, premortem interventions, professional roles and public trust as central safeguards (PMID 42418265). Québec organ-donation professionals were broadly favorable to donation after MAID but divided on some extensions such as directed donation and death by donation (PMID 33663501).

The core expert architecture strongly converges on **permit with strict separation and consent safeguards**.

## 11. Normative / framework positions and reasoning bridges

- **Autonomy / altruism:** a capable person who independently chooses MAID may also have a strong interest in donating → withholding the option can frustrate a meaningful final value → offer donation with robust consent safeguards.
- **Nonmaleficence / anti-instrumentalization:** transplantation needs could subtly alter the MAID experience or create perceived pressure → the system must prevent the patient from becoming a means to organ supply → separate decisions/teams and minimize burden; a cautious policy may wait for patient initiation.
- **Public trust / rule integrity:** organ transplantation depends on confidence that death is not caused to obtain organs → strict dead-donor-rule compliance and transparent governance are non-negotiable → permit only under independently auditable safeguards.

## 13. Construction and representation risks

- letting donation need influence MAID eligibility or timing;
- implying organ recovery causes death;
- conflating general organ-donation support with support for donation after MAID;
- understating the burden of hospital transfer/testing;
- assuming proactive offering is neutral rather than potentially pressuring;
- making expert consensus appear to settle public values;
- merging directed donation/death-by-donation controversies into the core case.
