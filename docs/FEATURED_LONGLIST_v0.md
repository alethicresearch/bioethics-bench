# Featured Collection — longlist v0

**56 topics.** For substantive review by the PI and research/editorial reviewer.

Nothing here is selected, and no scenario or candidate text is drafted. Entries record what a
case would be about and what would make it strong or difficult — enough to cut from, not enough
to build from. Assessed against [`CURATION_FRAMEWORK.md`](CURATION_FRAMEWORK.md).

**Rationale key:** **C** core/canonical · **M** methodologically informative · **F** frontier/contemporary
**Legacy:** derives from or revisits one of the nine historical SACRE examples.

## Clinical treatment, consent and capacity

### 1. Refusal of life-saving transfusion on religious grounds
**Domains:** consent-capacity-refusal · **C**
**Decision:** Should a hospital provide a blood transfusion to a competent adult who refuses it on religious grounds and will otherwise die?
**Importance:** The paradigm case for autonomy against beneficence, and the one where a settled legal answer coexists with continuing clinical and ethical difficulty.
**Position families:** absolute deference to competent refusal; refusal honoured with structured capacity re-assessment; bloodless-medicine alternatives as institutional obligation; override in narrow circumstances (dependants, uncertain capacity).
**Provenance:** case law; Jehovah's Witness Hospital Liaison guidance; anaesthesia/haematology society statements; empirical work on bloodless surgery outcomes.
**Strengths / concerns:** exceptionally clear positions and deep sourcing. Concern: the expert pool may converge strongly on the settled answer, compressing the convergence structure — worth checking whether it produces interesting SACRE output or a landslide.
**Legacy:** no.

### 2. Capacity assessment and supported decision-making
**Domains:** consent-capacity-refusal · disability-mental-health · **C M**
**Decision:** Where a patient's decision-making capacity is contested, should the institution substitute a decision or support the patient in making their own?
**Importance:** The CRPD-driven shift from substituted to supported decision-making is one of the genuine live disagreements in contemporary bioethics, and it splits disability advocacy from parts of clinical practice.
**Position families:** functional capacity test with substitute decision-maker; supported decision-making with no substitution; hybrid with time-limited substitution; will-and-preferences standard replacing best-interests.
**Provenance:** UN CRPD Article 12 and General Comment No. 1; national mental capacity legislation; disability scholarship; psychiatric ethics literature.
**Strengths / concerns:** unusually good source-perspective diversity — public, professional and framework pools genuinely disagree. Concern: G9, the case needs care to be legible without legal background.
**Legacy:** no.

### 3. Emergency treatment without consent
**Domains:** consent-capacity-refusal · **C**
**Decision:** How far may clinicians treat an unconscious patient whose prior wishes are unknown or ambiguously documented?
**Importance:** Tests the limits of presumed consent and the evidentiary weight of informal prior statements.
**Position families:** treat under emergency presumption; treat only to stabilise pending evidence of wishes; give weight to informal reports from family; documented directives only.
**Provenance:** emergency medicine guidelines; case law on advance decisions; empirical work on directive accessibility.
**Strengths / concerns:** bounded and tractable. Concern: risk of overlap with entry 11 (dementia/advance directives) — the collection probably wants one of the two, not both.
**Legacy:** no.

### 4. Adolescent consent and confidentiality
**Domains:** pediatric-neonatal · consent-capacity-refusal · **C**
**Decision:** Should a competent adolescent's request for confidential treatment be honoured against a parent's claim to be informed?
**Importance:** Where autonomy, family authority and developmental capacity genuinely conflict rather than merely trading off.
**Position families:** mature-minor autonomy; parental notification with adolescent assent; service-dependent thresholds (contraception, mental health, substance use); age-based bright lines.
**Provenance:** *Gillick* and successors; adolescent health society guidance; comparative statutes; empirical work on disclosure and care-seeking.
**Strengths / concerns:** strong stakeholder diversity. Concern: G8 — answers vary sharply by jurisdiction and by service type; the scenario must stipulate both.
**Legacy:** no.

### 5. Disclosure of a serious diagnosis against family request
**Domains:** consent-capacity-refusal · global-structural · **C M**
**Decision:** Should clinicians disclose a terminal diagnosis directly to a patient when the family asks them not to, on grounds the patient would not want to know?
**Importance:** One of the few canonical cases where cross-cultural variation is the ethical content rather than noise around it — a direct counterweight to a US-hospital-shaped collection.
**Position families:** unconditional truth-telling; ask-tell-ask / preference elicitation; family-mediated disclosure as legitimate cultural practice; staged disclosure.
**Provenance:** cross-cultural bioethics literature; oncology communication guidelines from multiple regions; empirical studies of disclosure preferences.
**Strengths / concerns:** excellent geographic and cultural breadth, genuinely distinct public and framework positions. Concern: representational tractability — the non-disclosure family must be stated in its own terms, not as paternalism to be refuted.
**Legacy:** no.

## Pediatrics and neonatology

### 6. Parental refusal of life-sustaining treatment for a critically ill newborn
**Domains:** pediatric-neonatal · end-of-life · **C**
**Decision:** Should parents be permitted to refuse life-sustaining treatment for a severely ill newborn with an uncertain prognosis?
**Importance:** The central case for the limits of parental authority and the content of a child's best interests.
**Position families:** parental authority within a harm threshold; best-interests determination by clinicians; time-limited trial of therapy; independent review before withdrawal.
**Provenance:** neonatal society framework documents; case law; prognostic outcome literature; parental decision-making studies.
**Strengths / concerns:** deep sourcing and a real conflict. Concern: G8 — the answer depends heavily on the prognosis stipulated, so the scenario must fix it explicitly and the fixing is itself a representation choice.
**Legacy:** **yes** — `infant-treatment-refusal`. Would need a stipulated prognosis and sourced candidate pools; the legacy version had neither.

### 7. Extreme prematurity at the threshold of viability
**Domains:** pediatric-neonatal · **C M**
**Decision:** At what gestational age should active resuscitation be offered, recommended, or withheld, and who decides?
**Importance:** The threshold has moved with technology while the ethical framework has not settled, and national guidelines genuinely differ.
**Position families:** gestational-age thresholds; individualised prognostic assessment; parental choice within a grey zone; presumption toward resuscitation.
**Provenance:** national neonatal guidelines (which diverge usefully); outcome registries; parent-experience literature.
**Strengths / concerns:** strong for SACRE — guideline divergence gives the expert pool real internal variety, which is unusual. Concern: overlaps entry 6; the two together may over-weight neonatal.
**Legacy:** no.

### 8. Contested best interests in a critically ill child
**Domains:** pediatric-neonatal · **C**
**Decision:** When parents seek continued or experimental treatment that the clinical team judges not in the child's interests, who should decide?
**Importance:** The public-facing form of the best-interests dispute, and the case where public preference and professional judgment diverge most visibly.
**Position families:** clinical best-interests with court recourse; parental authority absent significant harm; independent mediation before litigation; transfer-of-care as a right.
**Provenance:** litigated disputes and their judgments; paediatric ethics commentary; media and public-attitude studies.
**Strengths / concerns:** the public–expert divergence is real and documented, which is exactly what SACRE is built to represent. Concern: G6 — recent cases involve identifiable children and grieving families; the record must be constructed as a represented problem, not a retelling.
**Legacy:** no.

### 9. Pediatric gender-affirming care
**Domains:** pediatric-neonatal · disability-mental-health · **F**
**Decision:** Under what conditions, if any, should puberty suppression or hormonal treatment be provided to adolescents with gender dysphoria?
**Importance:** A live policy question on which national health systems have recently reached different conclusions from similar evidence.
**Position families:** affirmative care on assessment; watchful waiting with psychological support; restriction to research protocols; age-based prohibition.
**Provenance:** national evidence reviews (which diverge); professional society standards of care; systematic reviews; adolescent and family experience literature.
**Strengths / concerns:** genuinely contested with serious sources on multiple sides, and a rare instance of expert bodies reading the same evidence differently. Concern: **the hardest representational-tractability case on this longlist.** Every position family is routinely caricatured by the others. Flagged for close discussion (§ Recommendations).
**Legacy:** no.

## End of life

### 10. Withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment against family wishes
**Domains:** end-of-life · **C**
**Decision:** Should clinicians withdraw life-sustaining treatment they judge non-beneficial when the family insists it continue?
**Importance:** The core futility dispute, and the case that most directly tests whether professional judgment can override family authority.
**Position families:** unilateral withdrawal on physiological futility; process-based conflict resolution; family authority absent physiological futility; institutional policy with external review.
**Provenance:** professional society futility statements; process-based dispute procedures; empirical work on ICU conflict.
**Strengths / concerns:** canonical and well sourced. Concern: overlaps 12 and 14; the end-of-life cluster needs deliberate thinning.
**Legacy:** **yes** — `withdrawal-life-support`.

### 11. Dementia, advance directives and precedent autonomy
**Domains:** end-of-life · disability-mental-health · **C M**
**Decision:** Should an advance directive refusing treatment be honoured for a person with dementia who now appears content?
**Importance:** The sharpest philosophical problem in the collection — whether the earlier self has authority over the later one — with a real clinical decision attached.
**Position families:** precedent autonomy governs; current experiential interests govern; critical-interests reading of the directive; procedural review weighing both.
**Provenance:** the Dworkin/Dresser exchange and its successors; dementia care guidelines; advance-directive empirical literature.
**Strengths / concerns:** exceptional normative richness, and the ethical-framework pool contributes something genuinely distinct rather than restating the expert pool. Strong methodological candidate. Concern: G9 — legible only if the scenario states the contentment and the directive concretely.
**Legacy:** **yes** — `dementia-advance-directive`.

### 12. Brain death and continued somatic support
**Domains:** end-of-life · **C**
**Decision:** Should somatic support continue after a determination of death by neurological criteria when the family disputes the determination?
**Importance:** Where a definitional question — what death is — becomes an allocation and respect question.
**Position families:** death determination is dispositive; accommodation period; religious-objection exemptions; jurisdictional variation in the standard itself.
**Provenance:** brain-death determination guidelines; statutory accommodation provisions; disputed cases; religious authority statements.
**Strengths / concerns:** distinctive — nothing else on the list turns on a definitional question. Concern: G8, the answer varies with jurisdiction's accommodation law.
**Legacy:** no.

### 13. Palliative sedation and the doctrine of double effect
**Domains:** end-of-life · **C**
**Decision:** Should continuous deep sedation be provided for refractory suffering when it may foreseeably shorten life?
**Importance:** The canonical test of intention-based moral distinctions in clinical practice.
**Position families:** double-effect-licensed sedation for refractory symptoms; sedation as proportionate palliation without appeal to intention; restriction to imminently dying patients; rejection of the intention distinction.
**Provenance:** palliative care society frameworks; the double-effect literature; sedation practice studies.
**Strengths / concerns:** the framework pool is doing real work here, which is rarer than it should be. Concern: adjacency to 15 (assisted dying) — related, but the distinction is the point, so both may earn places.
**Legacy:** no.

### 14. Transition to hospice when curative treatment is refused-to-stop
**Domains:** end-of-life · **C**
**Decision:** Should hospice enrolment be conditioned on discontinuing curative treatment?
**Importance:** A structural/reimbursement constraint presenting as a clinical decision.
**Position families:** concurrent care; sequential model preserving hospice's character; case-by-case exceptions; open-access hospice.
**Provenance:** hospice benefit policy; concurrent-care demonstration evaluations; palliative care literature.
**Strengths / concerns:** usefully a *policy* decision rather than a bedside one. Concern: heavily US-reimbursement-specific — G8 risk, and it would deepen rather than correct the geographic skew. Weakest of the four legacy end-of-life topics.
**Legacy:** **yes** — `hospice-transition`.

## Assisted dying

### 15. Physician-assisted dying for terminal illness
**Domains:** assisted-dying · **C**
**Decision:** Should physicians be permitted to prescribe lethal medication to a terminally ill patient who requests it?
**Importance:** The most publicly contested question in bioethics, with sustained disagreement among publics, professions and frameworks.
**Position families:** permitted with eligibility safeguards; prohibited with palliative alternatives; permitted but non-physician-administered; institutional/individual conscientious objection.
**Provenance:** legislation across multiple jurisdictions; medical association positions (which have shifted); public opinion surveys; monitoring reports.
**Strengths / concerns:** the strongest source-perspective diversity on the longlist — public preference, professional position and framework reasoning genuinely diverge and are all well documented. Concern: none material.
**Legacy:** **yes** — `medical-aid-in-dying`.

### 16. Assisted dying where the sole underlying condition is mental illness
**Domains:** assisted-dying · disability-mental-health · **F M**
**Decision:** Should assisted dying be available to a person whose sole underlying condition is a psychiatric disorder?
**Importance:** The live frontier of the assisted-dying debate, where jurisdictions that permit the practice for terminal illness have reached opposite conclusions.
**Position families:** available with enhanced safeguards; categorically excluded; excluded pending evidence on irremediability; available only after exhaustive treatment.
**Provenance:** jurisdictional reviews and deferrals; psychiatric association statements; irremediability literature; disability-rights critique.
**Strengths / concerns:** unusually strong for RE-Iteration — positions here are close enough that directed revision toward coherence would be genuinely informative. Concern: G6 and representational tractability both need care.
**Legacy:** no (extends 15).

### 17. Advance requests for assisted dying in dementia
**Domains:** assisted-dying · end-of-life · **F**
**Decision:** Should an advance request for assisted dying be actionable once the person has lost capacity?
**Importance:** Combines the precedent-autonomy problem of 11 with the assisted-dying question of 15, and is under active legislative consideration.
**Position families:** actionable on documented advance request; requires contemporaneous capacity; actionable with a non-resistance condition; prohibited.
**Provenance:** legislative reviews; the precedent-autonomy literature; jurisdictional monitoring data.
**Strengths / concerns:** genuinely frontier. Concern: **derivative** — much of its content is 11 plus 15. Include only if the collection can afford three assisted-dying entries.
**Legacy:** no.

## Reproduction

### 18. Access criteria for publicly funded IVF
**Domains:** reproduction-genetics · **C**
**Decision:** On what criteria should publicly funded fertility treatment be allocated?
**Importance:** Makes explicit the usually implicit judgments about who is entitled to reproduce with assistance.
**Position families:** clinical-effectiveness criteria only; welfare-of-the-child assessment; equal access without eligibility screening; age or cycle limits on cost-effectiveness grounds.
**Provenance:** national funding criteria (which differ markedly); fertility society guidance; reproductive justice literature.
**Strengths / concerns:** good geographic breadth and a real allocation decision. Concern: SACRE usefulness needs checking — the expert pool may be thin relative to public and framework.
**Legacy:** no.

### 19. Prenatal testing and the disability critique
**Domains:** reproduction-genetics · disability-mental-health · **C M**
**Decision:** Should expanded prenatal screening be offered routinely, and what should accompany a positive result?
**Importance:** Where reproductive autonomy and disability rights conflict directly, and where the disability critique is a serious position rather than an objection to be managed.
**Position families:** routine offer with non-directive counselling; offer conditioned on balanced information including lived-experience accounts; restriction of screening scope; unrestricted access as autonomy.
**Provenance:** the expressivist-objection literature; screening programme evaluations; disability community position statements; obstetric guidelines.
**Strengths / concerns:** excellent stakeholder diversity, and the framework pool has genuinely distinct content. Concern: representational tractability — the disability critique is frequently reduced to a caricature in clinical framing.
**Legacy:** no.

### 20. Selective reduction in multifetal pregnancy
**Domains:** reproduction-genetics · **C**
**Decision:** Should selective reduction be offered, recommended, or restricted in higher-order multiple pregnancy?
**Importance:** Forces the relationship between fetal moral status and maternal/fetal outcome into an explicit decision.
**Position families:** offered as risk reduction; recommended above a threshold; restricted to medical indication; not offered.
**Provenance:** obstetric society guidance; outcome literature; moral-status scholarship.
**Strengths / concerns:** bounded and well sourced. Concern: normative richness may be lower than it appears — professional consensus is fairly settled.
**Legacy:** no.

### 21. Uterus transplantation
**Domains:** reproduction-genetics · clinical-innovation · **F**
**Decision:** Should uterus transplantation be offered outside research protocols, given that it is a non-life-saving transplant with donor risk?
**Importance:** Tests whether the transplant framework extends to procedures whose benefit is reproductive rather than life-saving.
**Position families:** research-protocol only; clinical service with strict criteria; deceased-donor only; not justified given risk profile.
**Provenance:** transplant society statements; trial reports; living-donor ethics literature.
**Strengths / concerns:** genuinely frontier and distinctive. Concern: G4 — literature is thinner than for established cases.
**Legacy:** no.

### 22. Posthumous gamete use
**Domains:** reproduction-genetics · **C**
**Decision:** Should stored gametes be used to conceive after the donor's death without explicit prior written consent?
**Importance:** Tests consent's reach beyond the life of the person who gave it.
**Position families:** explicit written consent required; inferred consent from relationship evidence; spousal request sufficient; prohibited.
**Provenance:** regulatory frameworks; litigated cases; fertility society guidance.
**Strengths / concerns:** clean, bounded, distinctive consent structure. Concern: lower canonical importance than most of this section.
**Legacy:** no.

## Genetics and genome editing

### 23. Heritable human genome editing
**Domains:** reproduction-genetics · clinical-innovation · **C F**
**Decision:** Under what conditions, if any, should heritable genome editing proceed to clinical application?
**Importance:** The defining governance question of contemporary bioethics, with an actual precipitating event and a documented international response.
**Position families:** moratorium pending governance; permitted for serious monogenic disease under strict oversight; permitted where no alternative exists; categorical prohibition on germline modification.
**Provenance:** international commission and WHO reports; national academies statements; the He Jiankui case record; germline ethics literature.
**Strengths / concerns:** outstanding provenance depth, real disagreement, high canonical and frontier value simultaneously. Among the strongest on the longlist.
**Legacy:** no.

### 24. Polygenic embryo screening for non-disease traits
**Domains:** reproduction-genetics · **F M**
**Decision:** Should polygenic scores for non-disease traits be offered in embryo selection?
**Importance:** Commercially available ahead of any governance consensus, which is itself the ethical problem.
**Position families:** prohibited for non-disease traits; permitted with disclosure of predictive limits; permitted for disease risk only; unrestricted parental choice.
**Provenance:** genetics society statements; predictive-validity literature; commercial offerings; enhancement scholarship.
**Strengths / concerns:** strong frontier value; the gap between marketed and evidenced claims gives the expert pool distinctive content. Concern: overlaps 25 — probably one or the other.
**Legacy:** no.

### 25. Genetic enhancement beyond disease prevention
**Domains:** reproduction-genetics · **C**
**Decision:** Should genetic modification of embryos be permitted for enhancement beyond the prevention of disease?
**Importance:** The canonical treatment/enhancement boundary question.
**Position families:** therapy/enhancement line as governance boundary; welfarist permission for enhancement; prohibition on justice grounds; case-by-case for specific traits.
**Provenance:** enhancement literature; national academies reports; public attitude surveys.
**Strengths / concerns:** canonical and clean. Concern: overlaps 23 and 24. As written it is also the most abstract entry in this section — G1 and G2 need attention to give it a real decision context.
**Legacy:** **yes** — `genetic-enhancement`.

### 26. Return of secondary findings in clinical genomics
**Domains:** reproduction-genetics · data-privacy · **C M**
**Decision:** Which unsought findings from clinical sequencing should be returned to the patient, and may a patient decline to know?
**Importance:** A recurring operational decision with a genuine right-not-to-know conflict, and one where professional recommendations changed under criticism.
**Position families:** mandatory return of actionable findings; patient opt-out; return only what was consented to; tiered consent by finding category.
**Provenance:** genomics society recommendations and the revisions to them; right-not-to-know literature; patient preference studies.
**Strengths / concerns:** the documented professional reversal is unusually good material — a rare case where the expert pool's own history shows movement. Good for RE-Iteration.
**Legacy:** no.

### 27. Direct-to-consumer genetic testing and familial implications
**Domains:** reproduction-genetics · data-privacy · **F**
**Decision:** What obligations follow when consumer genetic testing reveals information about relatives who never consented?
**Importance:** Genetic information is inherently familial while consent frameworks are individual, and consumer testing has made the mismatch concrete.
**Position families:** individual consent sufficient; duty to warn relatives; family-consent models; regulatory restriction on consumer offerings.
**Provenance:** duty-to-warn case law and commentary; consumer testing platform policies; genetic privacy literature.
**Strengths / concerns:** distinctive — consent structure differs from everything else on the list. Concern: adjacent to 51 (commercial data); check for redundancy.
**Legacy:** no.

## Transplantation and organ allocation

### 28. Age weighting in organ allocation
**Domains:** allocation-transplantation · **C M**
**Decision:** Should transplant allocation give priority to younger recipients when medical factors are otherwise equal?
**Importance:** The clearest instance of the fair-innings/life-years debate attached to a real allocation system.
**Position families:** urgency and waiting time only; life-years-maximising priority; age as tiebreaker; complete-lives weighting.
**Provenance:** allocation policy documents; the fair-innings literature; public attitude studies on allocation.
**Strengths / concerns:** excellent for SACRE — public preference and framework reasoning diverge sharply and are both well evidenced. Strong methodological candidate.
**Legacy:** **yes** — `organ-allocation`. The strongest of the nine.

### 29. Opt-out (presumed consent) deceased donation
**Domains:** allocation-transplantation · public-health · **C**
**Decision:** Should deceased organ donation operate on an opt-out basis, and what role should family veto retain?
**Importance:** A population policy where several countries have recently changed the default and the outcome evidence is contested.
**Position families:** opt-out with family veto retained; opt-out without veto; opt-in with active promotion; mandated choice.
**Provenance:** national legislation and evaluations; donation registry data; consent-and-defaults literature.
**Strengths / concerns:** genuinely a population policy rather than a bedside decision — useful decision-type diversity. Good geographic breadth.
**Legacy:** no.

### 30. Living donor risk and the limits of altruism
**Domains:** allocation-transplantation · **C**
**Decision:** Should a healthy person be permitted to accept a substantial risk of harm to donate to a stranger?
**Importance:** Where autonomy and non-maleficence conflict with the clinician as the agent of the harm.
**Position families:** donor autonomy with informed consent; risk ceilings set by the programme; directed donation only; independent donor advocate with veto.
**Provenance:** living donation guidelines; donor outcome registries; altruism and undue-inducement literature.
**Strengths / concerns:** clean structure, real conflict, distinct from allocation cases.
**Legacy:** no.

### 31. Retransplantation and adherence history
**Domains:** allocation-transplantation · disability-mental-health · **C M**
**Decision:** Should a patient whose graft failed following non-adherence, or whose organ failure followed alcohol use, receive equal priority for retransplantation?
**Importance:** Where responsibility-sensitive allocation meets the clinical reality that adherence and substance use are themselves health-determined.
**Position families:** responsibility-neutral allocation; outcome-predictive criteria including adherence; sobriety or adherence periods; case-by-case psychosocial assessment.
**Provenance:** transplant programme criteria; the luck-egalitarianism literature; addiction medicine evidence; early-transplant outcome studies.
**Strengths / concerns:** the responsibility question is philosophically deep and the empirical evidence cuts against the intuitive position, which makes for genuinely interesting convergence structure. Concern: representational tractability — the responsibility-sensitive family must be stated seriously.
**Legacy:** no.

## Research ethics

### 32. Placebo controls where effective treatment exists
**Domains:** research-ethics · **C M**
**Decision:** When, if ever, is a placebo-controlled trial justified for a condition with an established effective treatment?
**Importance:** The canonical methodology-versus-participant-welfare conflict, with a documented history of the governing declaration being revised under pressure.
**Position families:** active-control required where effective treatment exists; placebo permitted where methodologically necessary and risk is limited; placebo permitted with rescue provisions; local standard-of-care justification.
**Provenance:** Declaration of Helsinki and its revision history; regulatory guidance; the methodological literature.
**Strengths / concerns:** the revision history is exceptional material — the expert position visibly moved, which is rare and useful for RE-Iteration.
**Legacy:** **yes** — `clinical-trial-placebo`.

### 33. Human challenge studies
**Domains:** research-ethics · public-health · **F M**
**Decision:** Should healthy volunteers be deliberately infected to accelerate vaccine or therapeutic development?
**Importance:** Moved from hypothetical to actual, with volunteer demand exceeding what some ethics frameworks would permit.
**Position families:** permitted with strict criteria and rescue therapy; permitted only where rescue exists; prohibited for pathogens without rescue; permitted on informed-volunteer autonomy.
**Provenance:** WHO challenge-study criteria; conducted study reports; volunteer advocacy positions; risk literature.
**Strengths / concerns:** strong — public willingness and expert caution diverge in a documented, well-evidenced way.
**Legacy:** no.

### 34. Emergency research without prospective consent
**Domains:** research-ethics · **C**
**Decision:** Should research on emergency interventions proceed without prospective individual consent where the condition precludes it?
**Importance:** Without some solution, emergency medicine cannot generate evidence; the solutions all cost something.
**Position families:** exception with community consultation; deferred consent; legally authorised representative only; no research without prospective consent.
**Provenance:** regulatory exception frameworks; community consultation evaluations; emergency research literature.
**Strengths / concerns:** distinctive consent structure and genuine community-level stakeholder involvement.
**Legacy:** no.

### 35. Standard of care in trials conducted in low-resource settings
**Domains:** research-ethics · global-structural · **C M**
**Decision:** Should the control arm receive the global best standard of care or the local standard?
**Importance:** The central question of research justice, with a well-documented controversy behind it and continuing disagreement.
**Position families:** global best standard required; local standard with post-trial access commitments; local standard where the research question requires it; host-community determination.
**Provenance:** the perinatal transmission trial controversy and its literature; CIOMS guidelines; global health justice scholarship.
**Strengths / concerns:** **the strongest global/structural entry on the longlist** — directly corrects the collection's geographic skew, with deep sourcing and real disagreement.
**Legacy:** no.

### 36. Biobanking and broad consent for secondary use
**Domains:** research-ethics · data-privacy · **C**
**Decision:** Is broad consent to unspecified future research adequate, or must secondary uses be re-consented?
**Importance:** Determines whether large-scale biobank research is feasible at all, against a real consent-specificity objection.
**Position families:** broad consent with governance oversight; dynamic consent; study-specific re-consent; opt-out with tiered restrictions.
**Provenance:** biobank governance frameworks; dynamic consent trials; participant preference studies.
**Strengths / concerns:** good institutional-policy decision type. Concern: check redundancy against 49.
**Legacy:** no.

## Public health

### 37. Vaccine mandates
**Domains:** public-health · **C**
**Decision:** Should vaccination be mandated as a condition of school attendance or healthcare employment?
**Importance:** The canonical liberty-versus-collective-protection case, with recent policy experience across many jurisdictions.
**Position families:** mandate with medical exemption only; mandate with medical and religious exemption; strong incentives without mandate; voluntary with access measures.
**Provenance:** national mandate policies and evaluations; public health ethics frameworks; coverage and hesitancy data.
**Strengths / concerns:** excellent source-perspective diversity and geographic breadth; a population policy rather than a bedside decision.
**Legacy:** no.

### 38. Quarantine and isolation powers
**Domains:** public-health · **C**
**Decision:** Under what conditions may individuals be confined to protect others, and what is owed to those confined?
**Importance:** The starkest liberty restriction in public health, and the reciprocity obligation is frequently neglected in framing.
**Position families:** restriction with reciprocal support obligations; least-restrictive-means proportionality; voluntary with support; broad emergency powers with review.
**Provenance:** public health law frameworks; ethics of quarantine literature; outbreak response evaluations.
**Strengths / concerns:** distinctive stakeholder structure — those confined are a distinct constituency. Concern: some overlap with 37 on the liberty axis.
**Legacy:** no.

### 39. Expansion of newborn screening panels
**Domains:** public-health · reproduction-genetics · **C M**
**Decision:** Which conditions should be included in mandatory newborn screening, given that some findings are not actionable?
**Importance:** A screening programme is a population intervention on people who did not consent, and panel scope varies widely between countries.
**Position families:** treatability criterion; broad screening for knowledge value; parental opt-in for non-actionable conditions; tiered panels with consent.
**Provenance:** national screening criteria (which differ usefully); screening programme evaluations; parental experience literature.
**Strengths / concerns:** strong for SACRE — panel divergence between countries gives the expert pool genuine internal variety. Good geographic breadth.
**Legacy:** no.

### 40. Supervised consumption and harm reduction
**Domains:** public-health · disability-mental-health · **C**
**Decision:** Should supervised consumption services be provided, and on what conditions?
**Importance:** Where public preference and public health evidence diverge most sharply and most durably.
**Position families:** provision as evidence-based harm reduction; provision conditional on treatment pathways; abstinence-oriented alternatives; prohibition.
**Provenance:** service evaluations; drug policy frameworks; community consultation records; public attitude data.
**Strengths / concerns:** the documented public–expert divergence is exactly what SACRE represents. Strong community stakeholder presence.
**Legacy:** no.

### 41. Antimicrobial stewardship against individual benefit
**Domains:** public-health · **C M**
**Decision:** Should an antibiotic of last resort be withheld from a patient who might benefit, to preserve its future effectiveness?
**Importance:** The clearest case of an obligation to future persons overriding a present identified patient — a stakeholder class nothing else here represents.
**Position families:** individual clinical benefit governs; stewardship restriction with committee approval; population-level reservation policy; tiered access by resistance profile.
**Provenance:** stewardship guidelines; resistance modelling; the future-generations literature.
**Strengths / concerns:** **distinctive stakeholder structure — future persons as an affected party.** Excellent methodological candidate; the identified-versus-statistical-lives structure gives unusual convergence geometry.
**Legacy:** no.

## Scarce resource allocation

### 42. Pandemic ventilator triage
**Domains:** public-health · allocation-transplantation · **C M**
**Decision:** How should scarce ventilators be allocated when demand exceeds supply?
**Importance:** The canonical crisis-standards case, with real protocols written, criticised and revised under disability-rights challenge.
**Position families:** short-term survival maximisation; life-years or complete-lives weighting; lottery among clinically eligible; priority to healthcare workers on reciprocity.
**Provenance:** crisis standards of care protocols; disability-rights legal challenges and the resulting revisions; triage ethics literature.
**Strengths / concerns:** outstanding. The protocols were written, challenged and revised — a documented movement of expert positions under external critique, which is the best RE-Iteration material on the longlist. Public, expert and framework pools all substantial.
**Legacy:** **yes** — `pandemic-triage`. The other clear keeper alongside 28.

### 43. Reimbursement of very-high-cost therapies
**Domains:** allocation-transplantation · global-structural · **C F**
**Decision:** Should a health system fund a curative therapy priced at a level that displaces substantial other care?
**Importance:** Makes opportunity cost explicit, with named beneficiaries on one side and statistical ones on the other.
**Position families:** cost-effectiveness threshold; rule-of-rescue priority for identified patients; managed entry with outcomes agreements; refusal pending price negotiation.
**Provenance:** HTA decisions and appraisals; pricing literature; rule-of-rescue scholarship; patient advocacy positions.
**Strengths / concerns:** strong decision-type diversity — an institutional funding decision, not a clinical one. The identified/statistical asymmetry is philosophically rich.
**Legacy:** no.

### 44. Routine ICU admission under ordinary scarcity
**Domains:** allocation-transplantation · **M**
**Decision:** How should ICU beds be allocated in ordinary, non-crisis scarcity, where triage protocols do not apply?
**Importance:** The everyday allocation that receives far less attention than crisis triage but affects vastly more decisions.
**Position families:** first-come-first-served; benefit-based prioritisation; explicit triage criteria applied routinely; discharge-to-admit protocols.
**Provenance:** intensive care society guidance; ICU admission decision studies; rationing literature.
**Strengths / concerns:** usefully mundane — the collection needs cases that are not dramatic. Concern: lower canonical recognition; check normative richness is real and not just operational.
**Legacy:** no.

## Disability and mental health

### 45. Involuntary psychiatric treatment
**Domains:** disability-mental-health · **C**
**Decision:** Under what conditions may psychiatric treatment be given without consent?
**Importance:** The most consequential routine coercion in medicine, and one the CRPD framework challenges root and branch.
**Position families:** dangerousness criterion; capacity-based criterion; supported decision-making without involuntary treatment; least-restrictive with advance directives.
**Provenance:** mental health legislation across jurisdictions; CRPD Article 12 commentary; service-user movement positions; coercion outcome literature.
**Strengths / concerns:** the service-user perspective is a genuinely distinct public pool that most cases lack. Concern: overlaps 2 — related but distinguishable, and the collection may want both.
**Legacy:** no.

### 46. Cochlear implantation in deaf children
**Domains:** disability-mental-health · pediatric-neonatal · **C M**
**Decision:** Should cochlear implantation be recommended for a deaf infant of Deaf parents who decline it?
**Importance:** Where the medical model of disability and a cultural-minority self-understanding conflict directly, with a child's open future in between.
**Position families:** implantation as clear best interests; parental authority within a cultural community; deferral to the child's own later decision; bilingual-bicultural without implantation.
**Provenance:** Deaf community position statements; audiology outcome literature; open-future scholarship; parental decision studies.
**Strengths / concerns:** exceptional — the framework pool contributes something unavailable elsewhere, and it directly tests whether a collection can represent a position that rejects the medical framing entirely. Concern: representational tractability requires real care.
**Legacy:** no.

### 47. Compulsory feeding in severe anorexia nervosa
**Domains:** disability-mental-health · end-of-life · **C M**
**Decision:** Should a competent-appearing patient with long-standing severe anorexia be treated against their refusal, or supported in palliative care?
**Importance:** The case where capacity, futility and end-of-life reasoning converge on one patient, and where "terminal anorexia" is itself a contested new framing.
**Position families:** treat under incapacity attribution; honour refusal with palliative support; time-limited compulsory treatment; harm-reduction supported care.
**Provenance:** eating disorder society guidance; the terminal-anorexia debate; capacity case law; recovery literature.
**Strengths / concerns:** genuinely difficult, contemporary, and no position family is comfortable. Concern: G6 — must be constructed as a represented problem; and the framing itself is contested, which the scenario must not settle by wording.
**Legacy:** no.

### 48. Disability weighting in health metrics
**Domains:** disability-mental-health · global-structural · **C**
**Decision:** Should health metrics used for resource allocation weight life with disability as less valuable?
**Importance:** The methodological choice most consequential for disabled people's access, generally made without their input.
**Position families:** QALY weighting as standard; equal weighting regardless of disability; separate equity weights; rejection of aggregate metrics for allocation.
**Provenance:** HTA methodology guidance; disability paradox literature; disability-rights critique of QALYs; equity weighting research.
**Strengths / concerns:** distinctive — the decision is about the measurement instrument, which nothing else on the list is. Concern: G1 and G9 — needs a concrete institutional decision to attach to, or it becomes methodological rather than bioethical.
**Legacy:** no.

## Data and privacy

### 49. Secondary use of health records without re-consent
**Domains:** data-privacy · research-ethics · **C**
**Decision:** May routinely collected health records be used for research without individual re-consent?
**Importance:** Determines whether learning health systems are possible, against a genuine consent objection and documented public-trust failures.
**Position families:** opt-out with governance oversight; explicit consent required; anonymisation-conditional use; public-benefit test with independent approval.
**Provenance:** data governance frameworks; failed and successful national programmes; public deliberation exercises; privacy scholarship.
**Strengths / concerns:** the public-deliberation evidence gives an unusually well-grounded public pool. Concern: redundancy with 36.
**Legacy:** no.

### 50. Digital contact tracing and public health surveillance
**Domains:** data-privacy · public-health · **F**
**Decision:** Should digital proximity tracing be deployed, and should participation be voluntary?
**Importance:** Recently deployed at scale with divergent national approaches and contested effectiveness.
**Position families:** decentralised voluntary; centralised with public health access; mandatory for specified settings; not deployed absent effectiveness evidence.
**Provenance:** national deployments and evaluations; privacy-preserving protocol literature; uptake and effectiveness data.
**Strengths / concerns:** good geographic breadth and genuine architectural alternatives. Concern: the effectiveness evidence is weak enough that the decision may collapse toward one answer.
**Legacy:** no.

### 51. Commercial data partnerships in healthcare
**Domains:** data-privacy · medical-ai-data · **F**
**Decision:** On what terms may a health system share patient data with a commercial technology partner?
**Importance:** Several such partnerships have been found to have proceeded without adequate consent or governance.
**Position families:** prohibition on commercial transfer; permitted with benefit-sharing and governance; permitted for de-identified data only; patient-controlled authorisation.
**Provenance:** regulatory findings on specific partnerships; data governance frameworks; benefit-sharing literature.
**Strengths / concerns:** institutional decision type, real documented failures to source from. Concern: adjacency to 49 and 27.
**Legacy:** no.

## Medical AI

### 52. Algorithmic allocation of care management resources
**Domains:** medical-ai-data · allocation-transplantation · **F M**
**Decision:** Should an algorithm be used to prioritise patients for scarce care management, given that its proxy target may encode existing inequity?
**Importance:** A documented instance where a widely deployed algorithm systematically under-identified need in a patient group because of proxy choice — the ethics is in a technical decision.
**Position families:** deploy with bias auditing and correction; prohibit algorithmic allocation; deploy with clinician override; redesign target variable with affected-community input.
**Provenance:** published audits of deployed algorithms; algorithmic fairness literature; health system deployment reports.
**Strengths / concerns:** **strong** — concrete, documented, and the harm arises from a design choice rather than from intent, which is unusual and instructive.
**Legacy:** no.

### 53. Clinician override of an AI recommendation
**Domains:** medical-ai-data · **F**
**Decision:** What should a clinician do when a validated AI system's recommendation conflicts with their own judgment, and who bears responsibility?
**Importance:** As systems outperform clinicians on narrow tasks, deference becomes a real question rather than a hypothetical one.
**Position families:** clinician judgment always governs; documented justification required to override; AI advisory with no override requirement; system-specific deference by validation strength.
**Provenance:** clinical decision support literature; automation bias research; liability scholarship; regulatory guidance.
**Strengths / concerns:** genuinely frontier; responsibility structure is distinctive. Concern: G2 — needs a concrete clinical decision or it becomes abstract.
**Legacy:** no.

### 54. Disclosure of AI involvement to patients
**Domains:** medical-ai-data · consent-capacity-refusal · **F**
**Decision:** Must patients be told when AI contributed to their diagnosis or treatment recommendation?
**Importance:** Extends informed consent to the provenance of a recommendation rather than its content.
**Position families:** disclosure always required; disclosure where AI is determinative; disclosure on request; no disclosure requirement for decision support.
**Provenance:** emerging regulatory requirements; informed consent scholarship; patient attitude surveys.
**Strengths / concerns:** clean and bounded; public preference evidence is available and diverges from professional convenience. Concern: normative richness may be moderate.
**Legacy:** no.

### 55. Deployment of diagnostic AI with unequal performance across populations
**Domains:** medical-ai-data · global-structural · **F M**
**Decision:** Should a diagnostic system be deployed when it performs well overall but measurably worse for an identifiable subgroup?
**Importance:** Forces a choice between aggregate benefit and equal treatment that cannot be evaded by improving the model.
**Position families:** deploy with subgroup performance disclosure; withhold pending equitable performance; deploy with subgroup-specific thresholds; deploy only where it exceeds local standard of care.
**Provenance:** published performance disparity studies; regulatory guidance on subgroup reporting; fairness-in-ML literature; global health deployment reports.
**Strengths / concerns:** the aggregate-benefit-versus-equity structure is philosophically sharp, and it connects to global health deployment. Concern: overlaps 52 on the fairness axis — likely one of the two.
**Legacy:** no.

## Global and structural bioethics

### 56. Global allocation of a scarce vaccine or therapeutic
**Domains:** global-structural · public-health · **C F**
**Decision:** How should a globally scarce vaccine be allocated between countries?
**Importance:** Recent experience showed allocation frameworks proposed and then substantially not followed, which is itself the ethical material.
**Position families:** population-proportional allocation; need-based by epidemiological burden; allocation to maximise global benefit; national purchase priority as legitimate.
**Provenance:** international allocation framework documents; distribution outcome data; global justice scholarship; national procurement records.
**Strengths / concerns:** **the strongest correction to the collection's geographic skew**, alongside 35. Public, expert and framework pools all substantial, and the gap between stated frameworks and actual behaviour is instructive.
**Legacy:** no.

## What I am not doing

Not selecting the canon. The entries above record what each case is about and where it is strong or difficult; which 24–30 constitute the Featured Collection is a research and editorial judgement.
